Chemical Reactions and Equations

Chemistry · 14 formulas · 4 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

Balanced equation rule

Number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides (Law of Conservation of Mass).

CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2 + heat

Combination reaction; quick lime + water gives slaked lime (exothermic).

CaCO3 heat → CaO + CO2

Decomposition (thermal). Used in lime kilns.

2FeSO4 heat → Fe2O3 + SO2 + SO3

Thermal decomposition of ferrous sulphate; green colour fades.

2Pb(NO3)2 heat → 2PbO + 4NO2 + O2

Brown fumes of NO2.

2H2O electricity → 2H2 + O2

Electrolytic decomposition of water; H2 : O2 = 2 : 1 by volume.

2AgCl sunlight → 2Ag + Cl2

Photolytic decomposition (used in black & white photography).

Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu

Displacement: more reactive Fe pushes out less reactive Cu (blue colour fades).

Na2SO4 + BaCl2 → BaSO4 (white ppt) + 2NaCl

Double displacement / precipitation.

NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H2O

Neutralisation (acid + base).

CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O + heat + light

Combustion of methane (exothermic).

C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy

Respiration = slow combustion of glucose.

CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O

Redox: CuO is reduced, H2 is oxidised.

4Fe + 3O2 + xH2O → 2Fe2O3.xH2O

Corrosion of iron = rusting (needs both air and moisture).

Key points

Evidence of a chemical reaction

  • Change in state, change in colour, evolution of a gas, change in temperature.
  • A reaction in which heat is given out = exothermic; heat absorbed = endothermic.

Types of reactions

  • Combination: two or more reactants → one product.
  • Decomposition: one compound breaks into simpler substances (thermal / electrolytic / photolytic).
  • Displacement: more reactive element displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution.
  • Double displacement: exchange of ions, often gives a precipitate.
  • Oxidation: gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. Reduction: loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.
  • Redox reaction: oxidation and reduction occur together.

Corrosion and rancidity

  • Corrosion: metal attacked by moisture, air, acids. Silver tarnishes, copper gets a green coat, iron rusts.
  • Rancidity: oxidation of fats/oils making food smell and taste bad.
  • Prevention: antioxidants, airtight containers, refrigeration, flushing with nitrogen.

Exam traps

  • Always balance; never change a formula to balance an equation.
  • State symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq) and conditions (heat, sunlight) carry marks.
  • Rusting needs BOTH oxygen and moisture - dry air or boiled water prevents it.

Acids, Bases and Salts

Chemistry · 12 formulas · 4 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

Acid + Metal → Salt + H2

e.g. Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2. Metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series do this.

Acid + Metal carbonate → Salt + H2O + CO2

Na2CO3 + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H2O + CO2 (turns lime water milky).

Acid + Metal hydrogencarbonate → Salt + H2O + CO2

NaHCO3 + HCl → NaCl + H2O + CO2.

Acid + Base → Salt + Water

Neutralisation; heat is evolved.

Acid + Metal oxide → Salt + Water

CuO + 2HCl → CuCl2 + H2O (metal oxides are basic).

Base + Metal → Salt + H2

2NaOH + Zn → Na2ZnO2 + H2 (zinc is amphoteric).

Ca(OH)2 + Cl2 → CaOCl2 + H2O

Bleaching powder (calcium oxychloride).

NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 → NH4Cl + NaHCO3

Solvay process for baking soda.

2NaHCO3 heat → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2

Baking soda on heating gives washing soda's anhydrous form.

Na2CO3 + 10H2O → Na2CO3.10H2O

Washing soda crystals (water of crystallisation).

CaSO4.1/2H2O + 1.5H2O → CaSO4.2H2O

Plaster of Paris sets hard into gypsum.

pH scale 0 ---- 7 ---- 14

pH < 7 acidic, pH = 7 neutral, pH > 7 basic. Lower pH = more H+ ions.

Key points

Acids and bases

  • Acids give H3O+ (H+) in water; bases give OH- in water.
  • Acids: sour, turn blue litmus red, pH < 7. Bases: bitter, soapy, turn red litmus blue, pH > 7.
  • Strong acids/bases ionise completely (HCl, H2SO4, HNO3 / NaOH, KOH); weak ones partly (CH3COOH, H2CO3 / NH4OH, Ca(OH)2).
  • Water is essential: acids do not show acidic behaviour in absence of water (dry HCl gas + litmus = no change).

Important compounds

  • Bleaching powder: CaOCl2, from chlorine + dry slaked lime. Used to bleach, disinfect water.
  • Baking soda: NaHCO3, mild non-corrosive base, used in antacids, baking powder, fire extinguishers.
  • Washing soda: Na2CO3.10H2O, removes permanent hardness of water, cleaning agent.
  • Plaster of Paris: CaSO4.1/2H2O, made by heating gypsum at 373 K; sets in ~10 min, used for casts and toys.
  • Chlor-alkali process: electrolysis of brine gives Cl2 (anode), H2 (cathode), NaOH (near cathode).

pH and daily life

  • Our body works in pH 7.0-7.8; tooth decay starts below pH 5.5.
  • Acid rain when pH of rain water < 5.6.
  • Antacids (mild base) relieve acidity; bee sting (acid) is treated with baking soda, nettle sting (base) with vinegar.
  • Soil pH is corrected with lime (basic) or organic matter/gypsum (acidic).

Exam traps

  • Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules in one formula unit of a salt.
  • Dilution: acid is added to water slowly with stirring - never water to acid.
  • Salts of strong acid + strong base are neutral; strong acid + weak base are acidic; weak acid + strong base are basic.

Metals and Non-metals

Chemistry · 12 formulas · 5 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

4Na + O2 → 2Na2O

Sodium burns in air; Na and K are stored in kerosene.

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO

Magnesium ribbon burns with dazzling white flame; MgO is basic.

2Na + 2H2O → 2NaOH + H2 + heat

Vigorous, exothermic; hydrogen catches fire.

3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2

Iron with steam (red hot iron).

Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2

Metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen.

Zn + 2NaOH → Na2ZnO2 + H2

Zinc and aluminium are amphoteric - react with acids and bases.

Fe2O3 + 2Al → 2Fe + Al2O3 + heat

Thermite reaction, used to weld railway tracks.

2ZnS + 3O2 → 2ZnO + 2SO2 (roasting)

Sulphide ore heated strongly in air.

CaCO3 heat → CaO + CO2 (calcination)

Carbonate ore heated in limited air.

ZnO + C → Zn + CO

Reduction of the oxide with carbon (coke).

2Al2O3 electricity → 4Al + 3O2

Electrolytic reduction of alumina.

Na → Na+ + e- ; Cl + e- → Cl-

Ionic bond: Na2O, MgCl2, CaO are ionic compounds.

Key points

Physical properties

  • Metals: lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors, sonorous, high melting point (exception Hg - liquid).
  • Non-metals: brittle, poor conductors (exception graphite - conductor; iodine - lustrous).
  • Metal oxides are basic; non-metal oxides are acidic (Al2O3 and ZnO are amphoteric).

Reactivity series (decreasing)

  • K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > (H) > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au.
  • Metals above H displace hydrogen from dilute acids; Cu, Ag, Au do not.
  • Displacement reactions show the order: more reactive metal pushes out the less reactive one.

Metallurgy

  • Concentration of ore → Roasting (sulphide) / Calcination (carbonate) → Reduction → Refining (electrolysis).
  • Highly reactive metals (K, Na, Ca, Al) are obtained by electrolytic reduction; moderately reactive (Zn, Fe, Pb) by carbon reduction; less reactive (Cu, Hg) by heating the oxide; Ag, Au occur free.
  • Thermite reaction: Fe2O3 + 2Al → 2Fe + Al2O3, highly exothermic, used in welding.

Corrosion and alloys

  • Corrosion: rusting of iron, black coating on silver, green coating on copper.
  • Prevention: painting, oiling, galvanising (zinc coat), chrome plating, alloying.
  • Alloys: steel (Fe + C), stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni + C), brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), solder (Pb + Sn), amalgam (Hg + metal).
  • Alloying improves hardness, strength, and resists corrosion; pure gold is soft, 22-carat gold is alloyed with silver/copper.

Ionic compounds

  • Formed by transfer of electrons between metal and non-metal; high melting/boiling points; soluble in water; conduct electricity in molten state or solution.
  • Metal loses electrons (cation), non-metal gains electrons (anion).

Carbon and its Compounds

Chemistry · 10 formulas · 4 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O + heat + light

Complete combustion of methane; blue flame.

CH3CH2OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O + heat

Ethanol burns with a blue flame and is used as fuel.

CH3CH2OH alk. KMnO4 / acidified K2Cr2O7 → CH3COOH

Oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid.

CH3COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2

Test for carboxylic acid: brisk effervescence.

CH3COOH + C2H5OH acid → CH3COOC2H5 + H2O

Esterification; sweet-smelling ester.

Ester + NaOH → Sodium salt of acid + Alcohol

Saponification (soap making).

CH2=CH2 + H2 Ni → CH3-CH3

Addition reaction (hydrogenation), used to make vanaspati ghee.

CH4 + Cl2 sunlight → CH3Cl + HCl

Substitution reaction in saturated hydrocarbons.

Alkane CnH(2n+2) | Alkene CnH2n | Alkyne CnH(2n-2)

General formulae of homologous series.

Catenation + tetravalency

Two reasons carbon forms millions of compounds; C-C bonds are strong and stable.

Key points

Bonding in carbon

  • Carbon is tetravalent; forms four covalent bonds - no ions, so most carbon compounds are poor conductors and have low melting points.
  • Catenation: ability to link with other carbon atoms in long chains, branches and rings.
  • Allotropes: diamond (rigid 3-D, hardest), graphite (layers, conducts), fullerene C60.

Saturated and unsaturated

  • Saturated (alkanes): only single bonds, burn with a clean blue flame.
  • Unsaturated (alkenes, alkynes): multiple bonds; addition reactions with H2, Br2.
  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons burn with a yellow, sooty flame due to incomplete combustion.

Functional groups

  • Halogen -X (chloro-), Alcohol -OH (ol), Aldehyde -CHO (al), Ketone -CO- (one), Carboxylic acid -COOH (oic acid).
  • Homologous series: same functional group, successive members differ by -CH2- (14 u).
  • Isomers: same molecular formula, different structure (e.g. n-butane and iso-butane, C4H10).

Ethanol and ethanoic acid

  • Ethanol: liquid, miscible with water, used in medicines, sanitizers, alcoholic drinks; denatured alcohol is unfit for drinking.
  • Ethanoic acid (glacial acetic acid): freezes at 290 K, weak acid, reacts with carbonates and metals, gives vinegar on dilution.
  • Soaps are sodium/potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids; micelles trap oil in water; soap fails in hard water (scum), detergents work.

Life Processes

Biology · 7 formulas · 4 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

6CO2 + 12H2O sunlight, chlorophyll → C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O

Photosynthesis: light energy → chemical energy in glucose.

C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy (ATP)

Aerobic respiration in mitochondria.

C6H12O6 → 2C3H6O3 (lactic acid) + energy

Anaerobic respiration in muscles during vigorous exercise.

C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2 + energy

Fermentation by yeast.

Stomata: gas exchange + transpiration

Guard cells swell (turgid) to open, shrink to close; open in day, close at night.

Human heart: 4 chambers, 2 circuits

Right side - pulmonary (to lungs); left side - systemic (to body). Double circulation.

Nephron = filtration unit of kidney

Glomerulus filters blood → Bowman's capsule → tubule → reabsorption → urine → ureter → bladder.

Key points

Nutrition

  • Autotrophic: photosynthesis using CO2, water, chlorophyll, sunlight. Stomata for CO2, roots for water.
  • Heterotrophic: saprophytic (fungi), parasitic (Cuscuta), holozoic (Amoeba - pseudopodia, Paramecium - cilia).
  • Human digestion: mouth (salivary amylase → starch), stomach (HCl, pepsin → protein), small intestine (bile from liver, pancreatic and intestinal enzymes), villi absorb.
  • Large intestine absorbs water; egestion via anus.

Respiration

  • Aerobic: in mitochondria, releases much more energy; end products CO2 + H2O.
  • Anaerobic: less energy; in muscles gives lactic acid (cramps), in yeast gives ethanol + CO2.
  • Human tract: nostrils → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli (thin, moist, rich in capillaries).
  • Diaphragm and ribs create pressure changes: inhale - diaphragm contracts, ribs rise.

Transportation

  • Blood: plasma + RBC (haemoglobin, O2), WBC (immunity), platelets (clotting).
  • Arteries carry blood away from heart (thick, elastic, no valves); veins towards heart (valves); capillaries are one-cell thick for exchange.
  • Human heart: right atrium → right ventricle → lungs → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta. Valves prevent backflow.
  • Plants: xylem transports water and minerals upward (root pressure, transpiration pull); phloem transports food in both directions.
  • Transpiration cools the plant and creates the pull that lifts water.

Excretion

  • Kidney: nephron filters, reabsorbs glucose, salts, water; urine to bladder.
  • Plants excrete via stomata, lenticels, and by storing waste in leaves that fall, or as resins and gums.
  • Dialysis removes nitrogenous waste when kidneys fail.

Control and Coordination

Biology · 5 formulas · 3 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

Stimulus → Receptor → Sensory neuron → Spinal cord (relay) → Motor neuron → Effector

Reflex arc pathway; fast, involuntary protection.

Neuron: dendrite → cell body → axon → axon terminal → synapse

Impulse travels as an electrical signal, crosses synapse chemically.

Brain: cerebrum (thinking) + cerebellum (balance) + medulla (involuntary)

Hormones: thyroxine (thyroid), insulin (pancreas), adrenaline (adrenal), growth hormone (pituitary), testosterone / oestrogen (gonads)

Plant hormones: auxin (growth, phototropism), gibberellin (stem elongation), cytokinin (cell division), abscisic acid (growth inhibitor, wilting)

Key points

Nervous system

  • Neuron is the structural and functional unit; gap between two neurons is a synapse.
  • CNS = brain + spinal cord. PNS = cranial and spinal nerves.
  • Reflex action: sudden involuntary response (withdrawing hand from heat); reflex arc saves time by routing through the spinal cord.
  • Cerebrum: thinking, memory; cerebellum: posture and balance; medulla oblongata: breathing, heartbeat, blood pressure.
  • Involuntary vs voluntary vs reflex actions: walking is voluntary, heartbeat involuntary, blinking reflex.

Hormones and feedback

  • Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands into the blood.
  • Insulin lowers blood sugar; its deficiency causes diabetes.
  • Adrenaline prepares the body for emergency - faster heartbeat, breathing, more glucose in blood.
  • Iodine is needed to make thyroxine; deficiency causes goitre.
  • Feedback control: e.g. high blood sugar triggers insulin, low sugar stops it.

Coordination in plants

  • Tropism: growth movement in response to a stimulus - phototropism (light), geotropism (gravity), hydrotropism (water), chemotropism (chemicals).
  • Roots show positive geotropism and negative phototropism; shoots the opposite.
  • Nastic movement: non-directional, e.g. touch-me-not leaves droop on touch - due to change in water content of cells.
  • Auxin accumulates on the shaded side, making cells elongate and the shoot bend towards light.

How do Organisms Reproduce?

Biology · 6 formulas · 3 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

Asexual: Fission (Amoeba, Plasmodium) | Budding (yeast, Hydra) | Fragmentation (Spirogyra) | Regeneration (Planaria) | Spore formation (Rhizopus) | Vegetative propagation (potato, sugarcane, rose)

Flower: calyx + corolla + androecium (stamen) + gynoecium (carpel)

Stamen: anther + filament; Carpel: stigma + style + ovary.

Pollination → fertilisation → zygote → embryo → seed → fruit

Pollen tube carries male gamete to the ovule in the ovary.

Human male: testis → vas deferens → urethra; sperm + semen

Human female: ovary → fallopian tube → uterus; egg released every 28 days (menstrual cycle)

Placenta: exchange of nutrients, oxygen, waste between mother and embryo

Key points

Asexual reproduction

  • Only one parent, no gametes, offspring are genetically identical (clones).
  • Binary fission (Amoeba, Leishmania), multiple fission (Plasmodium).
  • Budding in yeast and Hydra; fragmentation in Spirogyra; regeneration in Planaria (specialised cells).
  • Spore formation in Rhizopus - spores have thick walls and survive harsh conditions.
  • Vegetative propagation: runners, tubers, bulbs, cuttings, grafting, tissue culture - faster and preserves desirable traits.

Sexual reproduction in plants

  • Stamen is the male part, carpel the female part; both may be in one flower (bisexual) or different flowers.
  • Self-pollination (same flower) and cross-pollination (wind, water, insects, animals).
  • After fertilisation: ovule → seed, ovary → fruit, zygote → embryo.
  • Germination needs water, oxygen and suitable temperature.

Human reproduction

  • Puberty: sexual maturity at 10-14 years, controlled by hormones (testosterone, oestrogen).
  • Male: testes make sperm and testosterone; sperm is a motile cell with a head, middle piece and tail.
  • Female: ovaries release one egg per cycle; fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube.
  • Menstrual cycle about 28 days; menstruation occurs when the egg is not fertilised.
  • Contraception: barrier (condom), chemical (oral pills), IUCD (copper-T), surgical (vasectomy/tubectomy).
  • STDs: gonorrhoea, syphilis (bacterial), warts, HIV-AIDS (viral).

Heredity

Biology · 6 formulas · 3 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

Monohybrid F2 phenotype ratio = 3 : 1 (dominant : recessive)

Genotype ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

Dihybrid F2 ratio = 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

Round-yellow 9, round-green 3, wrinkled-yellow 3, wrinkled-green 1.

Human chromosomes: 23 pairs = 22 autosomes + 1 sex pair

Female XX, male XY.

Cross: XX (mother) × XY (father) → 50% XX girls, 50% XY boys

The father's sperm determines the sex of the child.

Genotype = genetic make-up (TT, Tt, tt); Phenotype = visible trait

Punnett square

A grid used to predict all possible combinations of alleles in the offspring.

Key points

Inherited vs acquired traits

  • Inherited traits are passed in DNA from parents (eye colour, height tendency).
  • Acquired traits are learnt or gained during life (riding, cut marks) and are NOT passed to the next generation because they do not change the DNA of germ cells.

Mendel's laws

  • Law of dominance: in a pair of contrasting traits, only the dominant one appears in the F1 generation.
  • Law of segregation: alleles separate during gamete formation, each gamete gets one allele.
  • Law of independent assortment: traits are inherited independently of each other (shown by the dihybrid cross).
  • F1 tall plants (Tt) are all tall, but selfing them gives tall and short plants in 3 : 1.

Sex determination

  • Human beings have 23 pairs of chromosomes; the 23rd pair is XX in females and XY in males.
  • All eggs carry X; sperm are of two kinds, X and Y.
  • Fusion of X egg with X sperm gives a girl, with Y sperm gives a boy - so the father determines sex.
  • In birds the female is heterogametic (ZW), so the mother determines sex.

Light - Reflection and Refraction

Physics · 10 formulas · 4 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

1/v + 1/u = 1/f

Mirror formula (all distances with sign convention, pole as origin).

m = h'/h = -v/u

Magnification of a mirror. m negative = real & inverted; m positive = virtual & erect.

R = 2f

Radius of curvature = twice the focal length.

1/v - 1/u = 1/f

Lens formula (thin lens).

m = h'/h = v/u

Magnification of a lens.

P = 1/f (f in metres)

Power in dioptre (D). Convex f positive, concave f negative. P = 100/f(cm).

n = c/v

Absolute refractive index = speed of light in vacuum / in medium. n >= 1.

n21 = v1/v2 = n2/n1 = sin i / sin r

Snell's law; refractive index of medium 2 w.r.t. medium 1.

Sign convention

Distances measured from pole/optical centre; along incident light +ve, opposite -ve; heights above principal axis +ve.

1/f = (n-1)(1/R1 - 1/R2)

Lens maker's relation (concept: larger n → smaller f, more bending).

Key points

Reflection

  • Laws: angle of incidence = angle of reflection; incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.
  • Plane mirror: image is virtual, erect, same size, laterally inverted, as far behind as the object is in front.
  • Spherical mirror: pole P, centre of curvature C, principal focus F, radius R, aperture.
  • Concave mirror: converging; used in torches, headlights, solar cookers, dentists' mirrors, shaving mirrors.
  • Convex mirror: diverging, always virtual, erect and diminished; used as rear-view and in shops (wider field).

Image formation by a concave mirror

  • Object at infinity → image at F, real, highly diminished.
  • Beyond C → between F and C, real, inverted, diminished.
  • At C → at C, real, inverted, same size.
  • Between C and F → beyond C, real, inverted, enlarged.
  • At F → at infinity, highly enlarged.
  • Between P and F → behind the mirror, virtual, erect, enlarged.
  • Convex mirror: object anywhere → between P and F, virtual, erect, diminished.

Refraction

  • Bending of light when it goes from one transparent medium to another due to change in speed.
  • From rarer to denser: ray bends towards the normal; from denser to rarer: away from the normal.
  • Snell's law: sin i / sin r = constant for a given pair of media.
  • Convex lens: converging; concave lens: diverging.
  • Lens uses: convex in magnifying glass, spectacles for hypermetropia, camera; concave in spectacles for myopia, peepholes.
  • Optical density is not the same as mass density - kerosene (n = 1.44) is optically denser than water (n = 1.33).

Exam traps

  • Real images are always inverted; virtual images are erect (for a single mirror/lens).
  • Use consistent sign convention; write the formula first, then substitute.
  • For a concave mirror, f and R are negative; for a convex lens, f is positive.
  • A ray through the optical centre goes undeviated; a ray parallel to the principal axis passes through F.

The Human Eye and the Colourful World

Physics · 5 formulas · 3 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

Near point = 25 cm, far point = infinity

For a normal young adult; 25 cm is the least distance of distinct vision.

P = 1/f (corrective lens)

Myopia: concave lens, negative power. Hypermetropia: convex lens, positive power.

Range of vision 25 cm to infinity

Power of accommodation: ciliary muscles change the lens curvature.

Dispersion: white light → VIBGYOR

Violet bends most, red least (violet has the shortest wavelength).

Scattering ~ 1/lambda4

Shorter wavelengths scatter much more (Rayleigh scattering) - why the sky is blue.

Key points

Structure of the eye

  • Cornea (refracts most of the light), aqueous humour, iris (controls pupil size), lens (fine focus), ciliary muscles, retina (rods for dim light, cones for colour), optic nerve.
  • Blind spot: where the optic nerve leaves the eye - no rods or cones there.
  • Persistence of vision about 1/16 s; used in cinema.

Defects of vision

  • Myopia (short-sightedness): distant objects blurred, image forms in front of retina; corrected by concave lens; far point comes closer than infinity.
  • Hypermetropia (long-sightedness): near objects blurred, image behind retina; corrected by convex lens; near point recedes.
  • Presbyopia: age-related loss of accommodation, corrected by bifocal lenses.
  • Cataract: clouding of the lens, corrected by surgery with an artificial lens.

Dispersion and the atmosphere

  • Prism disperses white light into seven colours - different colours slow down by different amounts in glass.
  • Rainbow: refraction + dispersion + total internal reflection inside water droplets, then refraction again.
  • Atmospheric refraction: stars twinkle (varying refraction), planets do not; sun is visible about 2 minutes before actual sunrise and after sunset.
  • Scattering: blue sky (blue scattered most), red sun at sunrise/sunset (blue removed over a long path), black sky to astronauts (no atmosphere), red danger signals (red scattered least, visible far).
  • Tyndall effect: scattering of light by colloidal particles, e.g. sunlight through a canopy or a beam in a smoky room.

Electricity

Physics · 11 formulas · 3 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

I = Q/t

Current = charge/time; 1 A = 1 C/s. Ammeter in series.

Q = n e

e = 1.6 × 10-19 C; 1 C is about 6.25 × 1018 electrons.

V = W/Q

Potential difference = work done per unit charge; 1 V = 1 J/C. Voltmeter in parallel.

V = IR

Ohm's law (at constant temperature). R in ohm; 1 ohm = 1 V/A.

R = rho l / A

rho = resistivity (ohm metre), l = length, A = area of cross-section.

Series: R = R1 + R2 + R3 ; same current

Total resistance increases; voltage divides.

Parallel: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 ; same voltage

Total resistance decreases below the smallest branch.

P = VI = I2 R = V2 / R

Electric power in watt; 1 W = 1 V × 1 A.

H = I2 R t

Joule's law of heating (H in joule).

1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 J

1 unit of electricity = 1 kWh.

Energy = P × t

kWh = (watt × hours)/1000.

Key points

Current and potential difference

  • Conventional current flows opposite to electron flow.
  • Circuit symbols: cell, battery, resistor, variable resistor (rheostat), ammeter, voltmeter, plug key, bulb.
  • Ammeter: low resistance, in series. Voltmeter: high resistance, in parallel.

Resistance

  • Depends on length (R proportional to l), area (inversely), material (resistivity) and temperature.
  • Metals have low resistivity; alloys higher (used in heating elements because they do not oxidise easily); nichrome is used in heaters and irons.
  • Resistivity of copper 1.62 × 10-8 ohm m, silver 1.60 × 10-8 ohm m, nichrome 100 × 10-8 ohm m.
  • Series circuits: one break stops everything; parallel: appliances work independently at the same voltage.

Heating effect and power

  • Used in electric iron, toaster, heater, bulb filament (tungsten, high melting point).
  • Fuse: a short piece of low-melting-point wire that melts and breaks the circuit on overload.
  • Domestic wiring uses parallel connections at 220 V in India.
  • 1 unit (kWh) is the commercial unit of electrical energy.

Key points

Magnetic field

  • Field lines emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole outside the magnet; they never intersect.
  • Field is stronger where lines are closer; inside the magnet lines go from S to N.
  • Field around a straight conductor is concentric circles; direction reverses with current.
  • Field of a circular loop is stronger with more turns and smaller radius.
  • Solenoid: uniform field inside, used to make electromagnets (soft iron core).

Force and motor

  • A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force; force is maximum when current is perpendicular to the field.
  • Electric motor converts electrical energy to mechanical energy; split ring (commutator) reverses current every half turn.
  • Force on a charge moving in a field is used in cyclotrons and in the deflection of particles.

Induction and generator

  • Electromagnetic induction: changing magnetic field linked with a coil induces a current (Faraday).
  • Galvanometer deflects when the magnet moves or the coil moves; faster motion → larger induced current.
  • Electric generator: mechanical energy to electrical energy; AC generator uses slip rings, DC generator a split ring commutator.
  • In India AC changes direction every 1/100 second (50 Hz).

Domestic circuits and safety

  • Live (red/brown), neutral (black/blue), earth (green). Earth wire is a safety wire connected to a metal plate in the ground.
  • Appliances are connected in parallel; the earth wire protects the user if the metal body becomes live.
  • Fuse in the live wire; short circuit (live touches neutral) and overloading cause excessive current.
  • MCB (miniature circuit breaker) is a modern substitute for the fuse.

Our Environment

Biology · 5 formulas · 4 topic blocks

Formulas & equations

10% law: energy available at next trophic level = 10% of previous level

e.g. 10,000 J in grass → 1,000 J in deer → 100 J in lion.

Food chain: producer → primary consumer → secondary consumer → tertiary consumer

Grass → deer → lion. Food web = many interconnected chains.

Trophic levels: T1 producers, T2 herbivores, T3 carnivores, T4 top carnivores

Ozone: O2 + O (from UV) → O3

O3 absorbs harmful UV radiation in the stratosphere.

Ozone depletion: CFCs + UV → Cl radicals destroy O3

Ozone hole over Antarctica; Montreal Protocol to phase out CFCs.

Key points

Ecosystem

  • Biotic components (producers, consumers, decomposers) and abiotic components (air, water, soil, temperature).
  • Producers: green plants and algae. Decomposers: bacteria and fungi break down dead matter and return nutrients.
  • Ecosystems may be natural (forest, pond) or artificial (garden, aquarium, crop field).

Food chains and energy flow

  • Energy flows from the sun to producers and then through consumers - it is unidirectional.
  • Only about 10% of energy passes to the next level, so food chains rarely have more than 4-5 levels.
  • Harmful chemicals (pesticides) accumulate and magnify at higher trophic levels - biomagnification.
  • Removing one trophic level disturbs the whole chain; food webs make ecosystems more stable.

Ozone layer

  • Ozone (O3) is formed in the upper atmosphere by UV action on O2 and shields us from UV-B.
  • CFCs from refrigerators and aerosol sprays release chlorine that destroys ozone.
  • UV radiation causes skin cancer, cataract, damage to crops and phytoplankton.
  • The ozone hole is largest over Antarctica; international action has banned CFCs.

Waste management

  • Biodegradable waste (food, paper, cotton) is broken down by microbes; non-biodegradable (plastic, DDT, glass) persists and pollutes.
  • Waste segregation at source, composting, recycling, and refusing single-use plastics reduce pollution.
  • Plastic blocks drains, harms animals and enters food chains as microplastics.

Chemical Reactions and Equations

Chemistry · 6 questions with answers

01Why is the amount of gas collected in one test tube during electrolysis of water double that in the other?

Water contains hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 2 : 1 by volume (H2O). On electrolysis, two volumes of hydrogen are released for every one volume of oxygen, so the hydrogen tube (cathode side) collects double the gas.

02What is a redox reaction? Give one example.

A reaction in which one substance is oxidised and another is reduced at the same time. Example: CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O. CuO loses oxygen (reduced), H2 gains oxygen (oxidised).

03Why does the colour of copper sulphate solution change when an iron nail is dipped in it?

Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from copper sulphate solution: Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu. The blue colour of CuSO4 fades and turns light green (ferrous sulphate) while a reddish-brown copper deposit forms on the nail.

04Why is respiration considered an exothermic reaction?

During respiration glucose is oxidised and energy is released: C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy. Since heat/energy is given out, it is exothermic.

05What happens when dilute hydrochloric acid is added to iron filings? Write the equation.

Hydrogen gas is evolved and ferrous chloride forms: Fe + 2HCl → FeCl2 + H2 (gas bubbles, test tube becomes warm).

06How is rancidity prevented?

By adding antioxidants, storing food in airtight containers, refrigeration, and packing snacks in nitrogen gas so that oxygen does not oxidise the fats and oils.

02

Acids, Bases and Salts

Chemistry · 6 questions with answers

01Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry litmus paper?

Acidic behaviour needs H+ ions, which are produced only when HCl dissolves in water. In the absence of moisture, HCl does not ionise, so no H+ is available and litmus does not change.

02What is the chlor-alkali process? Name the products and their electrodes.

Electrolysis of concentrated brine (NaCl solution). Chlorine is liberated at the anode, hydrogen at the cathode, and sodium hydroxide forms near the cathode: 2NaCl + 2H2O → 2NaOH + Cl2 + H2.

03Why is baking soda used in making cakes soft and spongy?

Baking soda (NaHCO3) reacts with an acid or decomposes on heating to release CO2 gas, which gets trapped in the batter and makes the cake rise and become soft and spongy.

04What is Plaster of Paris? Why must it be stored in a moisture-proof container?

It is calcium sulphate hemihydrate, CaSO4.1/2H2O, obtained by heating gypsum at 373 K. In the presence of moisture it absorbs water and sets into hard gypsum (CaSO4.2H2O), becoming useless, so it is stored in moisture-proof containers.

05A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. Why?

To make the milk slightly alkaline so that the lactic acid produced by bacteria is neutralised and the milk takes longer to set into curd.

06How is the pH of soil corrected?

If the soil is too acidic, quick lime or slaked lime (basic) is added; if too basic, organic matter (compost) or gypsum is added to bring pH near neutral.

03

Metals and Non-metals

Chemistry · 6 questions with answers

01Why is sodium kept immersed in kerosene?

Sodium is so reactive that it reacts with oxygen and moisture in air, catches fire, and can even react with the moisture of the skin. Kerosene keeps air and water away, preventing this.

02Why do ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points?

Ionic compounds are held by strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions, so a large amount of energy is needed to break the lattice.

03What is meant by an amphoteric oxide? Give two examples.

An oxide that reacts with both acids and bases to give salt and water. Examples: aluminium oxide (Al2O3) and zinc oxide (ZnO).

04Explain why the galvanisation of iron prevents rusting.

Galvanisation coats iron with a layer of zinc. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it is oxidised first (sacrificial protection) and the zinc oxide layer is adherent, keeping air and moisture away from iron.

05Write the steps for extracting a metal from its sulphide ore.

The sulphide ore is first converted to oxide by roasting (heating strongly in air), then the oxide is reduced to the metal using carbon or a more reactive metal, and finally the metal is refined by electrolysis.

06Why does copper not react with dilute hydrochloric acid?

Copper lies below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace hydrogen from the acid; hence no reaction occurs.

04

Carbon and its Compounds

Chemistry · 6 questions with answers

01Why does carbon form a very large number of compounds?

Because of its tetravalency and the property of catenation - carbon atoms link with one another to form long chains, branched chains and rings, and also bond with other elements like H, O, N, S, giving millions of stable compounds.

02What is a homologous series? Give an example.

A family of organic compounds having the same functional group and similar chemical properties, in which successive members differ by a -CH2- unit and 14 u in molecular mass. Example: CH4, C2H6, C3H8 (alkanes).

03Why is the flame of a saturated hydrocarbon blue while that of an unsaturated one is yellow and sooty?

Saturated hydrocarbons get sufficient oxygen for complete combustion, giving a clean blue flame. Unsaturated hydrocarbons have a higher carbon percentage and burn incompletely even with enough air, releasing unburnt carbon particles - a yellow sooty flame.

04How would you distinguish between ethanol and ethanoic acid using a chemical test?

Add a pinch of sodium hydrogencarbonate to each. Ethanoic acid gives brisk effervescence of CO2 (which turns lime water milky); ethanol gives no effervescence.

05What are micelles? Why does soap not work well in hard water?

Micelles are spherical clusters in which the hydrophobic tails of soap molecules point inwards towards the oil droplet and the hydrophilic heads face water, letting oil be washed away. In hard water, Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions react with soap to form an insoluble scum, so soap is wasted and little lather forms.

06Why is a mixture of ethanol and water used as a fuel in some countries?

Ethanol burns with a clean blue flame without producing harmful gases and is a renewable fuel from crops; blending it with petrol (gasohol) reduces crude oil dependence and pollution.

05

Life Processes

Biology · 6 questions with answers

01Why is diffusion insufficient to meet the oxygen needs of multicellular organisms?

In multicellular organisms most cells are far from the body surface, so simple diffusion cannot supply enough oxygen to all of them at the rate they need it. They therefore need a transport system (blood) and respiratory organs.

02What are the differences between aerobic and anaerobic respiration?

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen, occurs in mitochondria, completely breaks glucose into CO2 and water, and releases a large amount of energy. Anaerobic respiration occurs without oxygen in the cytoplasm, gives lactic acid (muscles) or ethanol + CO2 (yeast), and releases much less energy.

03How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?

Its inner lining has numerous finger-like projections called villi, which greatly increase the surface area for absorption and are richly supplied with blood vessels that carry absorbed food to every cell.

04Why do we get muscle cramps after running?

During vigorous exercise oxygen supply is insufficient, so muscles respire anaerobically and lactic acid accumulates, causing cramps and fatigue.

05What is double circulation? Why is it necessary in humans?

Blood passes through the heart twice in one complete cycle - once through the pulmonary circuit (heart to lungs and back) and once through the systemic circuit (heart to body and back). It keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, ensuring an efficient supply of oxygen to the tissues of warm-blooded animals.

06How do plants get rid of their waste products?

By transpiration (excess water), by storing wastes in leaves that fall off, as resins and gums in old xylem, and by excreting oxygen and CO2 through stomata and lenticels.

06

Control and Coordination

Biology · 6 questions with answers

01What is a reflex arc? Draw/describe its pathway.

The pathway taken by a nerve impulse in a reflex action: receptor → sensory neuron → relay neuron in spinal cord → motor neuron → effector (muscle/gland). It lets the body respond immediately without waiting for the brain.

02Why is the use of iodised salt advisable?

Iodine is essential for the thyroid gland to synthesise thyroxine, which regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism. Its deficiency causes goitre (swollen neck) and can lead to cretinism in children, so iodised salt is recommended.

03How does chemical coordination take place in animals?

Endocrine glands secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them to specific target organs where they act. Because hormones reach all cells, their levels are controlled by feedback mechanisms.

04Why does the shoot bend towards light?

Auxin, a plant hormone, accumulates on the shaded side of the shoot. It stimulates cells there to elongate more than on the illuminated side, so the shoot bends towards the light (positive phototropism).

05What happens at the synapse between two neurons?

The electrical impulse reaching the axon terminal triggers the release of chemicals (neurotransmitters) that cross the gap and start an electrical impulse in the dendrite of the next neuron. The signal is converted from electrical to chemical and back.

06Which hormone is called the emergency hormone and why?

Adrenaline, secreted by the adrenal glands. It speeds up heartbeat, breathing and blood flow to muscles and releases glucose into blood, preparing the body to deal with a stressful situation.

How do Organisms Reproduce?

Biology · 6 questions with answers

01Why is variation beneficial to a species but not necessarily for an individual?

Variation gives a population the chance that some individuals will survive a changed environment, so the species continues. For an individual, a particular variation may be neutral or even harmful in the present environment, but it becomes useful only when conditions change.

02What are the advantages of vegetative propagation?

Plants that do not produce viable seeds can be grown; the new plants are genetically identical to the parent so desirable characters are preserved; and flowering and fruiting occur earlier than in plants raised from seed.

03Why is DNA copying an essential part of reproduction?

DNA carries the blueprint of the organism's body design and proteins. Copying it ensures the offspring inherit the parent's characteristics. Small variations during copying create differences that are the raw material of evolution.

04How does the embryo get nourishment inside the mother's body?

Through the placenta, a disc-like tissue embedded in the uterine wall with villi that interlock with the mother's blood supply. It allows glucose, oxygen and nutrients to pass from mother to embryo and wastes to pass back.

05What is the role of the seminal vesicles and prostate gland?

Their secretions provide fluid that nourishes the sperm, makes their transport easier and neutralises the acidity of the reproductive tract, forming semen.

06What changes occur in the body at puberty?

Increase in height and body fat, sweating and pimples, hair growth in armpits and genital area, thinner skin, and in boys deep voice, facial hair and broad shoulders; in girls breast development and the start of menstruation.

Heredity

Biology · 6 questions with answers

01Why are acquired traits not inherited?

Acquired traits arise from changes in body cells or from use and disuse during a lifetime; they do not alter the DNA of the germ cells (gametes), so they cannot be passed on to the next generation.

02A cross between a tall pea plant (TT) and a short pea plant (tt) gives all tall plants in F1. Why?

All F1 plants receive T from one parent and t from the other, so their genotype is Tt. T (tall) is dominant over t (short), so only the tall trait is expressed. Selfing F1 gives tall : short in 3 : 1.

03How is the sex of a child determined in humans?

The mother contributes an X chromosome through the egg, while the father's sperm may carry X or Y. XX makes a girl and XY makes a boy, so the sex of the child is determined by the father's sperm.

04What is the difference between a gene and an allele?

A gene is a section of DNA that codes for a particular protein or trait. Alleles are the alternative forms of the same gene (for example T and t for plant height) present at the same position on homologous chromosomes.

05Explain the dihybrid cross and its result.

Mendel crossed pea plants differing in two traits - round-yellow (RRYY) and wrinkled-green (rryy). F1 were all round-yellow (RrYy). Selfing gave four phenotypes in the ratio 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green, showing that the two traits are inherited independently.

06How do genes control traits?

Genes carry the code for making proteins. These proteins may act as enzymes or structural materials that produce the trait; for example, in pea seeds a gene decides whether an enzyme makes the seed round or wrinkled.

Light - Reflection and Refraction

Physics · 6 questions with answers

01State the two laws of reflection of light.

1. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. 2. The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.

02Why is a convex mirror used as a rear-view mirror in vehicles?

A convex mirror always forms an erect, virtual and diminished image and has a wider field of view, so the driver can see a large area of traffic behind.

03An object 5 cm high is placed 20 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the position and size of the image.

u = -20 cm, f = -15 cm. 1/v = 1/f - 1/u = -1/15 + 1/20 = -1/60, so v = -60 cm: the image is 60 cm in front of the mirror. m = -v/u = -(-60)/(-20) = -3, so h' = -15 cm: the image is real, inverted and 15 cm tall.

04Why does a pencil appear bent when partly dipped in water?

Light coming from the part of the pencil under water travels from a denser to a rarer medium and bends away from the normal at the water-air surface. This refraction makes the pencil appear bent and raised at the surface.

05The refractive index of diamond is 2.42. What is the meaning of this statement?

It means the speed of light in vacuum is 2.42 times its speed in diamond, i.e. light travels 2.42 times faster in vacuum than in diamond. Diamond is highly optically dense, so it bends light strongly and sparkles.

06Find the power of a concave lens of focal length 2 m.

P = 1/f = 1/(-2) = -0.5 D. The negative sign shows it is a diverging (concave) lens.

The Human Eye and the Colourful World

Physics · 6 questions with answers

01What is the power of accommodation of the eye?

The ability of the eye lens to change its focal length (by the action of ciliary muscles) so that objects at different distances are focused sharply on the retina. It is maximum in children and decreases with age.

02A person cannot see objects beyond 1.2 m clearly. Name the defect and the lens used.

Myopia (short-sightedness). It is corrected with a concave lens of suitable power that forms the image of a distant object at the person's far point.

03Why does the sky appear blue?

Molecules of air scatter shorter (blue) wavelengths much more strongly than longer (red) ones. Scattered blue light reaches our eyes from all directions, so the sky looks blue.

04Why does the sun appear reddish at sunrise and sunset?

Near the horizon sunlight travels a much longer path through the atmosphere, so most of the blue and green light is scattered away and only the longer-wavelength red light reaches us.

05Why do stars twinkle but planets do not?

Stars are point sources, so atmospheric refraction along a constantly changing path makes their apparent position and brightness fluctuate. Planets are extended sources - the variations from different points average out, so they shine steadily.

06What is the Tyndall effect? Give two examples.

The scattering of light by very fine suspended particles in a colloid, making the beam visible. Examples: sunlight passing through a canopy of trees, a beam of light entering a smoke-filled room, sunlight through mist.

Electricity

Physics · 6 questions with answers

01Define 1 volt.

The potential difference between two points is 1 volt if 1 joule of work is done in moving a charge of 1 coulomb from one point to the other. 1 V = 1 J/C.

02Why are alloys used in electric heating devices?

Alloys have higher resistivity than pure metals, so they produce more heat for the same current, and they do not oxidise (burn) easily even at high temperatures. Nichrome is commonly used.

03Two resistors 4 ohm and 6 ohm are connected in parallel across a 12 V battery. Find the total current.

1/R = 1/4 + 1/6 = 5/12, so R = 2.4 ohm. I = V/R = 12/2.4 = 5 A.

04Why is the tungsten filament of a bulb thick and coiled?

Tungsten has a very high melting point (about 3380 degrees C) and high resistivity, so it glows white hot without melting. A coiled thick wire gives high resistance in a small length and slows vaporisation, giving a longer life.

05Why is series arrangement not used for domestic circuits?

In series the same current flows through all appliances, so each gets less voltage, one faulty appliance breaks the whole circuit, appliances of different power ratings cannot work properly, and the total resistance is high.

06An electric heater of 1000 W runs for 5 hours daily. Find the units consumed in 30 days.

Energy per day = 1 kW × 5 h = 5 kWh. For 30 days = 5 × 30 = 150 kWh = 150 units.

Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

Physics · 6 questions with answers

01State the right-hand thumb rule.

Hold the current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand with the thumb pointing in the direction of the current; then the curled fingers give the direction of the magnetic field lines around the conductor.

02Why does a current-carrying conductor kept in a magnetic field experience a force?

The magnetic field of the current interacts with the external magnetic field; the resulting force is perpendicular to both the field and the current and is given by Fleming's left-hand rule. It is maximum when the conductor is perpendicular to the field.

03What is electromagnetic induction?

The production of an induced current in a coil when the magnetic field linked with it changes - by moving a magnet near the coil, moving the coil, or changing the current in a nearby coil.

04Explain the working of an electric motor.

A rectangular coil placed in a magnetic field carries current. The two sides of the coil experience forces in opposite directions (Fleming's left-hand rule), producing a torque that rotates the coil. The split ring reverses the current every half rotation so rotation continues in the same direction.

05Why is the earth wire used in domestic circuits?

It provides a low-resistance path to the ground. If the metal body of an appliance becomes live due to a fault, current flows to earth and the fuse blows, preventing a severe electric shock to the user.

06What is the frequency of AC in India? What does it mean?

50 Hz. The current changes direction 100 times a second, i.e. it reverses every 1/100 second, completing 50 full cycles per second.

Our Environment

Biology · 6 questions with answers

01Why are decomposers essential in an ecosystem?

They break down dead plants and animals and their wastes into simple substances that go back into the soil and are reused by producers. Without them, nutrients would remain locked in dead matter and the cycle of matter would stop.

02Why is the number of trophic levels in a food chain limited?

Because only about 10% of the energy is transferred to the next level, so energy available becomes too small to support further levels after four or five.

03What is biological magnification? Give one example.

The increase in concentration of a harmful non-biodegradable chemical at successive trophic levels. Example: pesticides like DDT in a water body reach high concentrations in fish and then in birds and humans.

04How is ozone formed in the upper atmosphere and why is it important?

UV radiation splits O2 into oxygen atoms, which combine with O2 to form O3. The ozone layer absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the sun and protects living organisms from skin cancer, cataracts and genetic damage.

05Why are plastics a serious environmental problem?

They are non-biodegradable, so they persist for decades, choke drains and water bodies, harm animals that swallow them, and release toxic gases when burnt. Recycling and refusing single-use plastics are the remedies.

06If 10,000 J of energy is available to producers, how much reaches a tertiary consumer?

Applying the 10% law: 1,000 J to herbivores, 100 J to secondary consumers and 10 J to tertiary consumers.
