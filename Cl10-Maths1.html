Every whole number bigger than 1 is built out of primes, and there is only one way to build it. That single sentence is the engine of this chapter — it gives you HCF and LCM without guesswork, and it lets you prove that numbers like √2 can never be written as a fraction.

1.2 · The fundamental theorem

02Every number has one prime recipe

Take 360. You can split it as 36 × 10, or 4 × 90, or 8 × 45. Different first moves — but if you keep splitting until nothing splits any further, you always land on the same primes.

Interactive · Prime factoriser
Type any number up to 100,000 and watch it come apart.
—

How to split by hand

Start with the smallest prime that divides the number. Divide, then repeat on the quotient. Stop when the quotient is 1.

360 → 2 × 180 → 2 × 90 → 2 × 45 → 3 × 15 → 3 × 5 → 5. So 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5.

Why unique? If there were two different recipes, a prime from one would have to appear in the other. Primes cannot be built from other primes, so the two recipes must be the same.

03HCF and LCM from the recipes

HCF asks: what is the biggest thing that divides both? Look at the shared primes and take the smallest power of each. LCM asks: what is the smallest number both divide? Take the largest power of every prime that shows up.

1.2 · A favourite question

04Can 6n ever end in 0?

A number ends in 0 only if it is divisible by 10, and 10 = 2 × 5. So the prime recipe must contain both a 2 and a 5. Check the recipe of 6n below and see what is missing.

6n = (2 × 3)n = 2n × 3n. The only primes present are 2 and 3 — there is never a 5.

So 6n is never divisible by 10, and therefore never ends in 0, however large n gets.

The general rule A number ends in 0 exactly when its prime recipe contains both 2 and 5. This is why 4n, 6n and 12n never end in 0, while 10n and 20n always do.
1.3 · Proof by contradiction

05Why √2 refuses to be a fraction

You cannot check every fraction — there are infinitely many. So we use the one trick that settles all of them at once: assume the opposite, and let arithmetic break it.

Step-through · The classic proof
Six steps. Each one is forced by the step before.
Assume the opposite. Suppose √2 is rational. Then √2 = a/b for some integers a and b, with b ≠ 0.
Tidy the fraction. Cancel any common factors so that a and b share nothing except 1. We say a and b are coprime.
Square both sides. 2 = a²/b², so a² = 2b². That means 2 divides a².
Use the prime theorem. If a prime divides a square, it divides the number itself. So 2 divides a. Write a = 2c.
Substitute back. 2b² = 4c², so b² = 2c². By the same reasoning, 2 divides b.
The contradiction. Both a and b are divisible by 2 — but we cancelled those factors in step 2. Nothing is left to give, so the assumption was false. √2 is irrational.

The same skeleton, every time

To prove √3 is irrational, replace 2 by 3 everywhere. To prove √5, replace it by 5. The argument never changes shape.

For a number like 3 + 2√5, you first prove √5 is irrational, then show that a rational plus an irrational stays irrational.

The two rules you need

Rule 1. If a prime p divides a², then p divides a.

Rule 2. The sum or difference of a rational and an irrational is irrational — and so is a non-zero rational times an irrational.

06Which fractions stop, and which repeat?

The prime recipe also answers a question you have wondered about since Class VII: why does 1/8 stop at 0.125, while 1/3 runs forever?

Only 2s and 5s → the decimal terminates.

Why A terminating decimal is just a fraction with denominator 10, 100, 1000 — and those are built from 2s and 5s only. Any other prime in the denominator can never be cancelled away.
Worked examples

07Two examples that carry the marks

Example · HCF of 96 and 404, then LCM

Step-through · Example 3
The pattern every HCF/LCM question follows.
Factorise both. 96 = 2⁵ × 3 and 404 = 2² × 101.
HCF: shared primes only. The only common prime is 2, and its smallest power is 2². So HCF = 4.
LCM: every prime, greatest power. 2⁵ × 3 × 101 = 9696.
Check. 4 × 9696 = 38,784 = 96 × 404. The shortcut confirms it.

Example · Proving 3 + 2√5 is irrational

Step-through · Example 7
Two irrationality facts stacked on top of each other.
We know √5 is irrational. That was proved with the same contradiction argument.
Assume 3 + 2√5 is rational and call it r. Then 2√5 = r − 3, so √5 = (r − 3)/2.
Right side is rational, because r and 3 are rational and dividing by 2 keeps it rational.
Left side is irrational. A number cannot be both. The assumption fails, so 3 + 2√5 is irrational.

09Four traps

01

HCF × LCM only for two numbers

For three numbers the product rule fails. The book's note gives the correct three-number formula.

02

Smallest power, not smallest number

HCF takes the smallest power of each shared prime — 2³ and 2⁵ share 2⁵.

03

Cancel before you square

The contradiction proof only works because a and b are coprime. Say so explicitly — it earns a mark.

04

“Composite” needs a reason

Write the number as a product of two factors bigger than 1. Saying “it is composite” without showing the factor loses the mark.

Exercise 1.2 · Irrational numbers

Assume √5 = a/b with a, b coprime. Then 5b² = a², so 5 divides a² and hence 5 divides a. Write a = 5c, giving 5b² = 25c², i.e. b² = 5c², so 5 divides b. Both a and b are divisible by 5, contradicting that they are coprime. Hence √5 is irrational.

Let 3 + 2√5 = r, rational. Then √5 = (r − 3)/2, which is rational — contradicting the irrationality of √5. So 3 + 2√5 is irrational.

(i) If 1/√2 = r rational (r ≠ 0), then √2 = 1/r is rational — a contradiction.

(ii) If 7√5 = r, then √5 = r/7 is rational — a contradiction.

(iii) If 6 + √2 = r, then √2 = r − 6 is rational — a contradiction.

Chapter summary

15Five lines to revise

  • Every composite number has exactly one prime factorisation (order ignored).
  • HCF = product of the smallest powers of common primes; LCM = product of the greatest powers of all primes.
  • For two numbers, HCF × LCM = a × b.
  • If a prime p divides a², then p divides a.
  • √2, √3, √5 … are irrational — proved by assuming the opposite and reaching a contradiction.
