Gender, Religion and Caste

Social diversity does not by itself threaten democracy; it is how divisions enter politics that matters. This chapter examines gender, religion and caste in Indian politics, asks when their expression is healthy, and explains secularism and the politicisation of caste.

Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

The existence of social diversity does not by itself threaten democracy. Political expression of social differences is possible and sometimes quite desirable in a democratic system. This chapter applies that idea to three kinds of social difference in India — gender, religion and caste — and asks, in each case, what the division looks like, how it is expressed in politics, and whether that expression is healthy.

Gender division is hierarchical and seen everywhere, yet rarely recognised in the study of politics; it is not based on biology but on social expectations and stereotypes. A sexual division of labour assigns all work inside the home to women, and the public/private split keeps women’s role in public life, especially politics, minimal. In India women lag behind on literacy, paid work, wages and safety, and the child sex ratio has fallen to 919. Their political representation is very low — 14.36 per cent in the Lok Sabha in 2019, less than 5 per cent in State Assemblies — though one-third of local body seats are reserved, giving over 10 lakh elected women, and the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam 2023 now provides 33 per cent reservation in the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly.

Religion is a different case. Ideas and values drawn from religions can legitimately play a role in politics, and people may express demands as members of a religious community, as long as every religion is treated equally. Communalism begins when religion is seen as the basis of the nation or of social community, when one religion is pitted against another and state power is used to establish domination. India’s answer is the secular state: no official religion, freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion or none, prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, and power to intervene to ensure equality within communities — as in the ban on untouchability.

Caste is peculiar to India: a hereditary occupational division sanctioned by rituals, based on exclusion and untouchability. Modernisation has weakened caste hierarchy but caste has not disappeared — most people still marry within their caste, and caste remains linked to economic status. In politics, parties choose candidates and make appeals on caste lines, yet no constituency has a clear majority of one caste, no party wins all the votes of a caste, and governments are regularly voted out. Politics also changes caste — it is caste that gets politicised, as groups expand, enter coalitions and demand their share of power.

Gender and Politics — the Public/Private Division

Gender division is a form of hierarchical social division seen everywhere, but it is rarely recognised in the study of politics. It tends to be understood as natural and unchangeable — however, it is not based on biology but on social expectations and stereotypes.

Boys and girls are brought up to believe that the main responsibility of women is housework and bringing up children. This is reflected in a sexual division of labour in most families: women do all work inside the home — cooking, cleaning, washing clothes, tailoring, looking after children — and men do all the work outside it. It is not that men cannot do housework; they simply think that it is for women to attend to these things. When these jobs are paid for, men are ready to take them up: most tailors or cooks in hotels are men.

Similarly, it is not that women do not work outside the home. In villages women fetch water, collect fuel and work in the fields; in urban areas poor women work as domestic helpers in middle-class homes while middle-class women work in offices. In fact the majority of women do some sort of paid work in addition to domestic labour. But their work is not valued and does not get recognition.

Definition

Sexual division of labour: a system in which all work inside the home is either done by the women of the family, or organised by them through the domestic helpers.

The result of this division is that although women constitute half of humanity, their role in public life — especially politics — is minimal in most societies. Earlier only men were allowed to participate in public affairs, vote and contest for public offices. Gradually the gender issue was raised in politics: women organised and agitated for equal rights, including the extension of voting rights, and demanded an enhanced political and legal status, better education and career opportunities. More radical women’s movements aimed at equality in personal and family life as well — these are called feminist movements.

Political expression of gender division and political mobilisation on this question helped to improve women’s role in public life. We now find women working as scientists, doctors, engineers, lawyers, managers and college and university teachers, which were earlier not considered suitable for women. In some parts of the world — for example the Scandinavian countries, Sweden, Norway and Finland — the participation of women in public life is very high.

Women’s Disadvantage in India

In our country women still lag much behind men despite some improvement since Independence. Ours is still a male-dominated, patriarchal society, and women face disadvantage, discrimination and oppression in various ways.

  • Literacy: the literacy rate among women is only 54 per cent compared with 76 per cent among men, and a smaller proportion of girl students go for higher studies. Girls perform as well as boys — if not better — at school, but they drop out because parents prefer to spend their resources on boys’ education.
  • Work and wages: the proportion of women among highly paid and valued jobs is still very small. On average an Indian woman works one hour more than an average man every day, yet much of her work is not paid and therefore not valued. The Equal Remuneration Act 1976 provides that equal wages should be paid for equal work — yet in almost all areas, from sports and cinema to factories and fields, women are paid less than men even when both do exactly the same work.
  • Sex selection: in many parts of India parents prefer sons and find ways to have a girl child aborted before she is born. Such sex-selective abortion led to a decline in the child sex ratio to merely 919 — and in some States it has fallen below 850 or even 800.
  • Violence: there are reports of various kinds of harassment, exploitation and violence against women. Urban areas have become particularly unsafe, and women are not safe even within their own homes from beating, harassment and other forms of domestic violence.

Women’s Political Representation

All this is well known, yet issues related to women’s well-being are not given adequate attention. This has led many feminists and women’s movements to the conclusion that unless women control power, their problems will not get adequate attention. One way to ensure this is to have more women as elected representatives.

In India the proportion of women in legislatures has been very low. The percentage of elected women members in the Lok Sabha touched 14.36 per cent of its total strength for the first time in 2019, and their share in the State Assemblies is less than 5 per cent. In this respect India is among the bottom group of nations in the world — behind the averages for several developing countries of Africa and Latin America. In government, cabinets are largely all-male even when a woman becomes Chief Minister or Prime Minister.

One way to solve this problem is to make it legally binding to have a fair proportion of women in elected bodies. This is what the Panchayati Raj has done: one-third of seats in local government bodies — panchayats and municipalities — are reserved for women, and there are now more than 10 lakh elected women representatives in rural and urban local bodies.

Women’s organisations and activists had long demanded a similar reservation of at least one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies, and a bill with this proposal was pending before Parliament for many decades. In 2023 the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women’s Reservation Act, 2023) was passed, which will give 33 per cent reservation of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and also the Delhi Assembly.

Gender division is an example that some form of social division needs to be expressed in politics. It also shows that disadvantaged groups do benefit when social divisions become a political issue — would women have made the gains noted above if their unequal treatment had not been raised in the political domain?

Religion, Communalism and Politics

Religious division is not as universal as gender, but religious diversity is fairly widespread. Many countries including India have followers of different religions, and even where most people belong to the same religion there can be serious differences about the way it is practised — as in Northern Ireland. Unlike gender differences, religious differences are often expressed in the field of politics.

Consider three instances: Gandhiji used to say that religion can never be separated from politics — and by religion he meant not any particular religion but moral values that inform all religions, believing that politics must be guided by ethics drawn from religion. Human rights groups have demanded that the government take special steps to prevent communal riots and protect religious minorities. The women’s movement has argued that family laws of all religions discriminate against women, and demanded that government change these laws to make them more equitable.

All these involve a relationship between religion and politics — but they do not seem very wrong or dangerous. Ideas, ideals and values drawn from different religions can and perhaps should play a role in politics. People should be able to express in politics their needs, interests and demands as members of a religious community, and those who hold power should sometimes be able to regulate the practice of religion to prevent discrimination and oppression. These political acts are not wrong as long as they treat every religion equally.

Communalism

The problem begins when religion is seen as the basis of the nation. It becomes more acute when religion is expressed in politics in exclusive and partisan terms, when one religion and its followers are pitted against another — when beliefs of one religion are presented as superior, when the demands of one religious group are formed in opposition to another, and when state power is used to establish the domination of one religious group over the rest. This manner of using religion in politics is communal politics.

Communal politics is based on the idea that religion is the principal basis of social community. Communalism involves thinking along these lines: the followers of a particular religion must belong to one community; their fundamental interests are the same; any difference they may have is irrelevant or trivial for community life; people who follow different religions cannot belong to the same social community; and if they have commonalities these are superficial and immaterial, their interests are bound to be different and to involve conflict. In its extreme form, communalism leads to the belief that people belonging to different religions cannot live as equal citizens within one nation — either one has to dominate the rest, or they have to form different nations.

This belief is fundamentally flawed. People of one religion do not have the same interests and aspirations in every context; everyone has several other roles, positions and identities; there are many voices inside every community, and all these voices have a right to be heard. Any attempt to bring all followers of one religion together in a context other than religion is bound to suppress many voices within that community.

The Secular State

Communalism was and continues to be one of the major challenges to democracy in our country. The makers of our Constitution were aware of this, which is why they chose the model of a secular state. This choice is reflected in several constitutional provisions:

  • There is no official religion for the Indian state. Unlike the status of Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Islam in Pakistan or Christianity in England, our Constitution does not give a special status to any religion.
  • The Constitution provides to all individuals and communities freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion, or not to follow any.
  • The Constitution prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion.
  • At the same time, the Constitution allows the state to intervene in matters of religion in order to ensure equality within religious communities — for example, it bans untouchability.

Understood in this sense, secularism is not just an ideology of some parties or persons; it constitutes one of the foundations of our country. Communalism should not be seen as a threat to some people in India — it threatens the very idea of India, which is why it needs to be combated. But a secular Constitution like ours is necessary but not sufficient to combat communalism: communal prejudices and propaganda need to be countered in everyday life, and religion-based mobilisation needs to be countered in the arena of politics.

Caste and Politics — Caste Inequalities

Unlike gender and religion, caste division is special to India. All societies have some social inequality and some division of labour, and in most societies occupations are passed on from one generation to another. The caste system is an extreme form of this: what makes it different is that hereditary occupational division was sanctioned by rituals. Members of the same caste group were supposed to form a social community practising the same or similar occupation, marrying within the caste group, and not eating with members of other caste groups.

The caste system was based on exclusion of and discrimination against the “outcaste” groups, who were subjected to the inhuman practice of untouchability. That is why political leaders and social reformers like Jotiba Phule, Gandhiji, B.R. Ambedkar and Periyar Ramaswami Naicker advocated and worked to establish a society in which caste inequalities are absent.

Partly due to their efforts and partly due to other socio-economic changes, castes and the caste system in modern India have undergone great changes. With economic development, large-scale urbanisation, growth of literacy and education, occupational mobility and the weakening of the position of landlords in villages, the old notions of caste hierarchy are breaking down. Now, most of the time in urban areas it does not matter much who is walking along next to us on a street or eating at the next table in a restaurant. The Constitution prohibited caste-based discrimination and laid the foundations of policies to reverse the injustices of the caste system.

Yet caste has not disappeared. Some older aspects have persisted: even now most people marry within their own caste or tribe; untouchability has not ended completely despite constitutional prohibition; and the effects of centuries of advantage and disadvantage continue to be felt. The caste groups that had access to education under the old system have done very well in acquiring modern education as well, while those who did not have access, or were prohibited from it, have lagged behind — which is why there is a disproportionately large presence of “upper caste” among the urban middle classes, and why caste continues to be closely linked to economic status.

The proportion of each religious community has not changed much since Independence: in percentage terms the Hindu, Jain and Christian shares have declined marginally since 1961, while the Muslim, Sikh and Buddhist shares have increased slightly. There is a common but mistaken impression that the Muslim proportion is going to overtake other communities — expert estimates for the Sachar Committee show it is expected to rise by only about 3 to 4 per cent in the next 50 years. Similarly, the SC, ST and OBC together account for about two-thirds of the country’s population and about three-fourths of the Hindu population.

Caste in Politics

As with communalism, casteism is rooted in the belief that caste is the sole basis of social community — that people of the same caste form a natural social community with the same interests, which they do not share with anyone from another caste. As with communalism, this belief is not borne out by experience. Caste is one aspect of our experience, but it is not the only relevant or the most important aspect.

What caste does to politics

  • When parties choose candidates, they keep in mind the caste composition of the electorate and nominate candidates from different castes to muster the support needed to win. When governments are formed, parties usually take care that representatives of different castes and tribes find a place.
  • Parties and candidates make appeals to caste sentiment to muster support, and some parties are known to favour some castes and are seen as their representatives.
  • Universal adult franchise and the principle of one-person-one-vote compelled political leaders to gear up to mobilising and securing political support, and brought new consciousness among people of castes hitherto treated as inferior and low.

But the focus on caste can give the impression that elections are all about caste and nothing else — which is far from true:

  • No parliamentary constituency has a clear majority of one single caste, so every candidate and party needs the confidence of more than one caste and community.
  • No party wins the votes of all the voters of a caste — a “vote bank” usually means that a large proportion, not all, of that caste votes for a party.
  • Many parties may put up candidates from the same caste, so some voters have more than one candidate from their caste while many have none.
  • The ruling party and the sitting MP or MLA frequently lose elections — which could not happen if all castes and communities were frozen in their political preferences.

Clearly, while caste matters in electoral politics, so do many other factors: voters’ strong attachment to political parties (often stronger than attachment to caste or community); different interests within the same caste depending on economic condition, so that rich and poor, or men and women of the same caste, often vote very differently; and people’s assessment of the government’s performance and the popularity of leaders, which are often decisive.

Politics in caste

But the relation is not one-way. Politics too influences the caste system and caste identities by bringing them into the political arena. It is not politics that gets caste-ridden — it is the caste that gets politicised. This takes several forms:

  • Each caste group tries to become bigger by incorporating neighbouring castes or sub-castes earlier excluded from it.
  • Various caste groups are required to enter into a coalition with other castes or communities, and thus into dialogue and negotiation.
  • New kinds of caste groups have come up in the political arena, like “backward” and “forward” caste groups.

Thus caste plays different kinds of roles in politics. In some situations, expression of caste differences gives many disadvantaged communities the space to demand their share of power — caste politics has helped people from Dalit and OBC castes gain better access to decision-making, and several political and non-political organisations have agitated for an end to discrimination and for more dignity and access to land, resources and opportunities. At the same time, exclusive attention to caste can produce negative results: politics based on caste identity alone is not healthy in a democracy, it can divert attention from pressing issues like poverty, development and corruption, and in some cases caste division leads to tensions, conflict and even violence.

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

Discuss the perceptions of an ideal woman that prevail in our society. Do you agree with any of these?

Model answer

Common perceptions include the woman as self-sacrificing mother, as the one responsible for housework and children, as modest and obedient, and as the custodian of family honour. These are stereotypes, not descriptions — they ignore women’s paid work, their abilities and their rights. A better image of an ideal person, for any gender, would be someone who is free to choose her work and her life, and who shares both domestic and public responsibilities. (Student answers will vary.)

2.

Why are we discussing things like household work in a textbook on Political Science? Is this politics?

Model answer

Yes, if politics is about power. Male dominance in the household is a form of power, and the public/private division keeps women out of public life. Because the gender division is treated as “natural”, it is rarely examined politically — which is exactly why the chapter examines it.

3.

Conduct a time use survey in your own household and see whether women work more.

Model answer

Record each adult’s hours daily for a week under income-generating work, household work, reading and recreation, talking, self-care and sleep, then average. Most households find women’s total working hours higher, with a much larger share of unpaid household work. (Student activity.)

4.

Can you identify your State on the child sex ratio map? What is its child sex ratio? Which States are below 900?

Model answer

Answers depend on your State. The map shades States by child sex ratio; States such as Haryana, Punjab, Delhi, Rajasthan and parts of the north-west have historically recorded ratios below 900, while Kerala and some north-eastern States are higher. The exercise asks you to read the map, not memorise it.

5.

Compare the child sex ratio map with the campaign poster. How do the two tell us about the same issue?

Model answer

The map shows the result — fewer girl children per thousand boys in particular States — and the poster states the cause: “50 million women missing in South Asia due to different forms of violence and discrimination”. Together they show that sex-selective abortion and discrimination are not private choices but a measurable, social pattern.

6.

Why is women’s representation so low in India? Have the Americas and Europe achieved a satisfactory level?

Model answer

Reasons include patriarchal attitudes that treat politics as a male domain, the cost and risk of contesting elections, family and care responsibilities, male control of party organisations and ticket distribution, and the absence of reservation until recently. The Americas (29.5%) and Europe (26.4%) are far above India (11.8%) but below the Nordic countries (42.3%), so they have improved without reaching parity — satisfactory only in the Nordic case.

7.

If casteism and communalism are bad, what makes feminism a good thing? Why not oppose all who divide society on any line — caste, religion or gender?

Model answer

Because the three are not the same. Communalism and casteism claim that religion or caste is the sole basis of community and set groups against each other; feminism claims that women and men should have equal rights and opportunities, and it seeks to end a hierarchy rather than to create one. Recognising a division that already exists, in order to remove an injustice, is not the same as manufacturing a division to win power.

8.

“I am not religious. Why should I bother about communalism and secularism?” Answer this.

Model answer

Because communalism threatens the very idea of India — a country where people of every religion, and of none, live as equal citizens. A person who is not religious still has an interest in not being treated as a member of a religious community against her will, in citizenship being defined by law rather than religion, and in public order and safety. Secularism protects everyone’s freedom, including the freedom not to believe.

9.

“I often crack jokes about people from one religion. Does that make me communal?”

Model answer

Not necessarily — but it can slide into it. The chapter says the most common expression of communalism is in everyday beliefs: religious prejudices and stereotypes so routine that we fail to notice them. A joke that rests on the inferiority of another religion feeds exactly that habit of mind, which is why the chapter says prejudices must be countered in everyday life.

10.

Do you think political leaders are right to treat people belonging to a caste as “vote banks”?

Model answer

No. It reduces citizens to a single identity, ignores the different interests of rich and poor, men and women within the caste, and diverts attention from poverty, development and corruption. The chapter’s evidence also shows the assumption is factually weak: no party wins all the votes of a caste, and sitting members are frequently defeated.

11.

“I don’t care what my caste is. Why are we discussing all this? Are we not promoting casteism by talking about caste?”

Model answer

Silence does not make caste disappear — it leaves existing inequalities unexamined. The chapter’s reply is that wherever there is domination, it should be discussed: naming caste inequality is the condition for ending it, just as naming gender inequality was the condition for improving women’s position.

12.

Will caste disappear if we keep mum about it?

Model answer

No. Discrimination persists in marriage, in access to education and in economic status, and untouchability has not ended completely despite prohibition. What changes it is law plus political mobilisation of the disadvantaged — which is what the chapter documents, along with the risks of exclusive caste politics.

Important Questions and Answers

Board-style practice

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

Explain the sexual division of labour and its consequences. (5 marks)

Model answer

Boys and girls are brought up to believe that women’s main responsibility is housework and bringing up children, which produces a sexual division of labour: women do all work inside the home — cooking, cleaning, washing, tailoring, caring for children — and men work outside it. It is not that men cannot do housework; they think it is for women, though they readily take up the same tasks when paid, as most hotel cooks and tailors are men. Nor do women avoid work outside the home: in villages they fetch water, collect fuel and work in fields; in cities poor women work as domestic helpers and middle-class women in offices. The majority of women do paid work in addition to domestic labour, but their work is not valued or recognised. The consequence is that although women are half of humanity, their role in public life, especially politics, is minimal — which is why the gender issue had to be raised politically, through feminist movements demanding equal rights, better education and equality in personal and family life.

2.

Describe the disadvantages women face in India with examples. (5 marks)

Model answer

(i) Education: female literacy is 54 per cent against 76 per cent for men, and fewer girls go for higher studies; girls drop out though they perform as well as boys, because parents spend more on sons. (ii) Work and wages: few women hold highly paid jobs; an average Indian woman works an hour more a day than an average man, but much of her work is unpaid; and despite the Equal Remuneration Act 1976, women are paid less than men even for the same work in sports, cinema, factories and fields. (iii) Sex selection: preference for sons and sex-selective abortion have brought the child sex ratio down to 919, and below 850 or even 800 in some States. (iv) Violence: harassment, exploitation and violence, with urban areas particularly unsafe and domestic violence inside the home.

3.

Explain the need for reservation of seats for women, and describe what has been done. (5 marks)

Model answer

Because women’s problems will not get adequate attention unless women control power, and one way to ensure that is more women as elected representatives. India’s record is poor: 14.36 per cent in the Lok Sabha in 2019, less than 5 per cent in State Assemblies, and largely all-male cabinets even with a woman Chief Minister or Prime Minister — placing India among the bottom group of nations, below the world average of 24 per cent. The Panchayati Raj has reserved one-third of seats in local bodies, producing more than 10 lakh elected women representatives, and in 2023 the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam provided 33 per cent reservation in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly. Gender division shows that disadvantaged groups do benefit when a social division becomes a political issue.

4.

What is communalism? Explain its various forms. (5 marks)

Model answer

Communalism is using religion in politics in exclusive and partisan terms: it begins when religion is seen as the basis of the nation or of social community, when one religion is pitted against another, and when state power is used to establish the domination of one religious group over the rest. It takes four forms: everyday beliefs — prejudices, stereotypes and a sense of superiority, so common that they go unnoticed; a quest for political dominance, taking the form of majoritarian dominance for a majority community or a demand for a separate political unit by a minority; political mobilisation on religious lines, using sacred symbols, religious leaders, emotional appeal and fear, with special appeals to one religion’s voters; and, in its ugliest form, communal violence, riots and massacre, of which Partition produced some of the worst.

5.

Why is communalism fundamentally flawed? How does the Constitution combat it? (5 marks)

Model answer

Because it assumes that the followers of a religion form one community with identical interests and that people of different religions cannot share a community. In fact people of one religion do not have the same interests and aspirations in every context; everyone has several roles, positions and identities; there are many voices inside every community, and all have a right to be heard — so any attempt to unite all followers of a religion in a non-religious context suppresses many voices. The Constitution answers it with a secular state: no official religion; freedom for all individuals and communities to profess, practise and propagate any religion or none; prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion; and power for the state to intervene in religious matters to ensure equality within communities, as by banning untouchability. But a secular Constitution is necessary, not sufficient — prejudices must be countered in everyday life and communal mobilisation in politics.

6.

What is the caste system? How has it changed in modern India? (5 marks)

Model answer

Caste is a hereditary occupational division sanctioned by rituals: members of a caste practised the same or similar occupation, married within the group and did not eat with members of other castes, and the system was based on exclusion of and discrimination against the “outcaste” groups, who suffered untouchability. Reformers — Jotiba Phule, Gandhiji, B.R. Ambedkar and Periyar Ramaswami Naicker — worked to end caste inequality, and socio-economic change has transformed the system: economic development, urbanisation, literacy and education, occupational mobility and the weakening of village landlords have broken down the old hierarchy, and the Constitution prohibits caste discrimination. Yet caste has not disappeared: most people still marry within their caste, untouchability persists in places, and centuries of advantage and disadvantage continue — upper castes remain over-represented among the urban middle classes and the rich, and caste is still linked to economic status.

7.

How does caste operate in politics, and why can it not determine election results alone? (5 marks)

Model answer

Caste operates when parties choose candidates keeping the caste composition of the electorate in mind and nominate candidates from different castes; when governments are formed with representatives of different castes and tribes; when parties and candidates appeal to caste sentiment, some being seen as representatives of particular castes; and through universal adult franchise, which compelled leaders to mobilise support and brought new consciousness among castes earlier treated as inferior. But caste cannot determine results alone: no parliamentary constituency has a clear majority of one caste; no party wins all the votes of a caste; several parties may field candidates from the same caste while many voters have none from theirs; and ruling parties and sitting members frequently lose. Voters’ attachment to parties, differences of interest within a caste by economic condition and gender, and assessments of government performance are often decisive.

8.

“It is not politics that gets caste-ridden; it is caste that gets politicised.” Explain. (3 marks)

Model answer

Politics influences caste by bringing caste identities into the political arena. Each caste group tries to become bigger by incorporating neighbouring castes and sub-castes; caste groups must enter coalitions with other castes and communities, which forces dialogue and negotiation; and new kinds of groups such as “backward” and “forward” castes appear in politics. In this way caste identities are reshaped by political competition, and disadvantaged communities gain space to demand their share of power.

9.

What are the positive and negative effects of caste in politics? (3 marks)

Model answer

Positive: expression of caste differences gives disadvantaged communities space to demand power; caste politics has helped Dalits and OBCs gain better access to decision-making, and organisations have agitated for an end to discrimination and for dignity, land and opportunities. Negative: politics based on caste identity alone is unhealthy, diverts attention from poverty, development and corruption, and can lead to tension, conflict and violence.

10.

Distinguish between the role of religion in politics and communalism. (3 marks)

Model answer

Ideas, ideals and values drawn from religions can and perhaps should play a role in politics; people may express their needs and demands as members of a religious community, and the state may regulate religious practice to prevent discrimination and oppression — all of which is acceptable as long as every religion is treated equally. Communalism begins when religion is seen as the basis of the nation or of community, when one religion is presented as superior and pitted against another, and when state power is used to establish the domination of one religious group over the rest.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

“Reserving seats for women in local bodies has done more for Indian democracy than any speech about equality.” Do you agree?

Model answer

Largely yes. The one-third reservation produced over 10 lakh elected women, changed the composition of local decision-making and brought issues of water, health and schooling into panchayat agendas — a structural change that speeches could not achieve. But the chapter also records limits: many women are proxies for male relatives, gram sabhas meet irregularly, and powers and funds remain with the States. So the reservation is necessary but not sufficient — which is exactly why the argument moved on to reservation in legislatures, now provided by the 2023 Act. (Model answer.)

2.

Why might a poor Dalit woman say that gender, caste and religion are “one problem, not three”?

Model answer

Because she experiences them together: low wages and unpaid housework (gender), exclusion from land and education and the risk of untouchability (caste), and often membership of a minority religion as well. The chapter separates the three for analysis, but in a single life they compound — which is why it insists that within every caste and community there are many voices, and that rich and poor, or men and women, of the same caste often have different interests. (Model answer.)

3.

A candidate says: “I will win because my caste is 30 per cent of this constituency.” What is wrong with this arithmetic?

Model answer

Three things. No constituency has a clear majority of one caste, so 30 per cent is not enough to win — allies must be found. No party wins all the votes of a caste: internal differences of income and gender, and party loyalty, split it. And sitting members are frequently defeated, which would be impossible if a caste voted as one bloc. The candidate also needs to reckon with government performance and the popularity of rivals. (Model answer.)

4.

Is a secular state neutral towards religion, or does it intervene? Use the Indian model.

Model answer

Both, in a specific sense. The Indian state has no official religion and does not favour any faith — that is neutrality between religions, and it protects the freedom not to believe. But it is not neutral about inequality inside religions: the Constitution allows it to intervene to ensure equality within communities, which is how untouchability was banned and how the women’s movement’s demand to reform discriminatory family laws is justified. So the model is “equal respect for all religions, plus reform where a community denies equality to its own members”. (Model answer.)

5.

Why does the chapter insist that social division is not the problem, but its political expression can be?

Model answer

Because diversity is a fact and a resource — the chapter opens by saying its political expression is sometimes desirable. When gender inequality entered politics, women gained education, laws and representation; when caste entered politics, Dalits and OBCs gained access to decision-making. But when a division is treated as the sole basis of community, as communalism and casteism do, it sets groups against each other and distorts politics. The variable is not difference but whether politics treats one identity as everything. (Model answer.)

6.

Suggest two practical measures, other than reservation, to raise women’s political participation.

Model answer

(i) Inner-party democracy: require parties to field a minimum share of women candidates and publish the data, since parties control tickets. (ii) Support structures: crèches, safety and transport, and funding limits that reduce the cost of contesting, so that care responsibilities and money do not exclude women. Public financing of campaigns and training programmes for first-time candidates would help too. (Model answer.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

Social diversity does not by itself threaten democracy, but the way divisions enter politics matters. Gender division is hierarchical and universal yet rarely recognised in politics; it is based on social expectations, not biology. A sexual division of labour confines women to unpaid work inside the home, so although women are half of humanity their role in public life is minimal. In India women lag on literacy (54 per cent against 76 per cent), on pay despite the Equal Remuneration Act 1976, on safety, and in the child sex ratio, which has fallen to 919. Their representation is very low — 14.36 per cent in the Lok Sabha in 2019, under 5 per cent in State Assemblies, 11.8 per cent against a world average of 24 per cent — though one-third of local body seats are reserved, giving over 10 lakh elected women, and the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam 2023 provides 33 per cent reservation in the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and Delhi Assembly. Gender is thus the case where political expression of a division has helped the disadvantaged.

Religion is different. Values drawn from religions can legitimately inform politics, and people may express demands as members of a religious community, provided every religion is treated equally. Communalism begins when religion becomes the basis of the nation or of community and one religion is pitted against another; it appears as everyday prejudice, as a quest for dominance, as mobilisation on religious lines, and finally as communal violence. Its belief is flawed because no community has a single voice. India answers it with a secular state: no official religion, freedom of religion for all, no discrimination on religious grounds, and power to intervene for equality within communities — as in the ban on untouchability. But a secular Constitution is necessary, not sufficient.

Caste is peculiar to India: a hereditary occupational division sanctioned by ritual, based on exclusion and untouchability. Reformers and socio-economic change have transformed it, yet caste persists — in marriage, in untouchability, and in economic status, with Scheduled Tribes and Castes far more likely to be poor than upper castes. In politics, parties weigh caste in choosing candidates and make caste appeals, and universal adult franchise brought new consciousness to excluded groups. But caste alone cannot decide elections: no constituency has a majority of one caste, no party wins all of a caste’s votes, and sitting members lose. Politics also reshapes caste, which gets politicised as groups expand, form coalitions and demand power. That can empower the excluded, but exclusive caste politics diverts attention from poverty, development and corruption and can produce conflict.
