Probability

A coin has no memory. A die has no favourites. Once you accept that, probability stops being mysterious: you count the outcomes you want, count all the outcomes there are, and divide.

The whole idea

01One formula, three habits

Theoretical probability counts. That is all. The skill is in being careful about what counts as an outcome — because if your outcomes are not equally likely, the formula gives a confident wrong answer.

P(E) = number of outcomes favourable to Enumber of all possible outcomes
Habit 1

List the sample space

Write every possible outcome once. One coin: 2. One die: 6. Two coins: 4. Two dice: 36.

Habit 2

Check equally likely

If outcomes are not equally likely, count the underlying equally likely cases instead — like the 36 pairs rather than the 11 possible sums.

Habit 3

Use the complement

“At least one” is almost always easier as 1 − P(none).

Where the definition comes from This classical definition is due to Pierre-Simon Laplace (1795): when outcomes are equally likely, probability is simply the favourable fraction. Cardano wrote the first book on games of chance in the 16th century; Bernoulli, de Moivre and Laplace turned it into a theory.

05P(not E) = 1 − P(E)

An event either happens or it does not — there is no third option. So the two probabilities must add to 1. This single line saves more time in exams than any other.

Worked examples

09Examples worth doing twice

Example 4 · The ace and its complement

Step-through · Example 4
One card from 52. P(ace) and P(not ace).
Sample space. Well shuffling means all 52 cards are equally likely.
Aces. Four of them (one per suit), so P(E) = 4/52 = 1/13.
Not an ace. 52 − 4 = 48 cards, so 48/52 = 12/13.
Faster. P(not E) = 1 − 1/13 = 12/13. Same answer, no counting.

Example 9 · At least one head in two tosses

Step-through · Example 9
The word “at least” is a signal to use the complement.
Sample space. (H,H), (H,T), (T,H), (T,T) — four equally likely outcomes. (H,T) is different from (T,H) because the coins are different.
Favourable. At least one head: (H,H), (H,T), (T,H) — three outcomes.
Answer. P = 3/4.
By complement. P(no head) = 1/4, so P(at least one) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. When the event says “at least”, do this first.

Before the exam

11Five traps

01

Unequal “outcomes”

Sums 2 to 12 are 11 outcomes but not equally likely. Always count the 36 pairs.

02

Order matters

(H,T) and (T,H) are two different outcomes. Collapsing them into “one head” is the classic error.

03

Without replacement

If a card or pen is not put back, the total drops to 51 or 19. Read the wording carefully.

04

Impossible vs unlikely

P = 0 means impossible; P = 1 means certain. A probability of −1.5 or 15/2 is simply not a probability.

05

“At least one”

Do it by complement: 1 − P(none). Direct counting is slower and error-prone.

Chapter summary

17Six facts that cover everything

  • P(E) = favourable outcomes ÷ all possible outcomes (equally likely)
  • A sure event has probability 1; an impossible event has 0
  • For every event, 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
  • The probabilities of all elementary events add to 1
  • P(not E) = 1 − P(E)
  • More trials → experimental probability approaches theoretical probability
