Part A · Employability Skills · Unit 1

Communication Skills – II

Methods of communication, the communication cycle and the power of feedback, the barriers that break communication, and the writing skills — parts of speech and sentences — that make your messages clear.

🎯 Learning Outcomes

  • Demonstrate knowledge of various methods of communication.
  • Provide descriptive and specific feedback.
  • Apply measures to overcome barriers in communication.
  • Apply principles of effective communication (the 7 Cs).
  • Demonstrate basic writing skills — parts of speech, sentences, capitalisation and punctuation.

📚 Study Notes

1. Methods of Communication

Communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas, thoughts and feelings between two or more people. We communicate in many ways, and choosing the right method for the situation is itself a skill.

Verbal communication

Communication through spoken and written words. It implies the use of words that make up a language — speaking, listening, writing and reading. Tone of voice, pitch and speed matter as much as the words.

  • Interpersonal communication — direct, two-way exchange between a small number of people (a conversation, a phone call, a video chat). Feedback is immediate.
  • Small-group communication — a group of up to about five people working towards a common goal, e.g. a school project team or a cricket team huddle.
  • Public communication — one speaker addressing a large audience, e.g. the Principal speaking at assembly. Feedback from the whole audience is limited, so the speaker must read non-verbal cues.

Written communication

Uses written words: e-mails, letters, notices, posters, reports and business meeting minutes. It provides a permanent record and can reach many people, but feedback is delayed.

  • Face-to-face informal communication — casual, unplanned conversation (chatting with friends); no fixed rules, rich in non-verbal cues.
  • E-mail — fast, cheap and recordable; suits both formal and informal messages across distances.
  • Notices and posters — one-to-many announcements; a notice is formal and targeted (e.g. exam schedule on the notice board), a poster is designed to catch attention (e.g. “Save Water” campaign poster).
  • Business meetings — structured group communication where agendas are discussed and decisions recorded in minutes.

Non-verbal communication

Communication without words — through the body and the face. It usually carries more emotional information than words.

  • Gestures — hand and head movements: a wave, a thumbs-up, a nod.
  • Facial expressions — smile, frown, raised eyebrows; the face reveals feelings instantly.
  • Body language (posture) — the way you sit, stand and move; leaning forward shows interest, crossed arms may signal resistance.
  • Proximity (distance) — how close you stand to someone while talking; it too communicates meaning.

Visual communication

Conveying ideas through things that can be seen — signs, symbols, pictures, charts, maps, graphs, colours and posters, including personal appearance. Example: a red traffic signal “communicates” stop; an infographic summarises data better than a paragraph.

Summary of methods of communication
MethodHow it worksExampleBest used when

WrittenWritten wordsE-mail, letter, notice, minutesA permanent record is needed

2. The Communication Cycle

Communication is not a one-way street — it is a cycle. The seven elements of the cycle are:

  1. Sender — the person who initiates the message with a purpose.
  2. Message — the information, idea or feeling to be conveyed.
  3. Encoding — converting the idea into words, symbols or gestures.
  4. Medium / Channel — the route the message travels: air (speech), paper (letter), wire/wireless (e-mail, call).
  5. Receiver — the person for whom the message is meant.
  6. Decoding — the receiver interprets and understands the message. This is the step where the message is understood.
  7. Feedback — the receiver's response, which travels back to the sender and completes the cycle.

Noise is anything that disturbs the message on the way — actual sound, a bad phone line, or even mental distraction. The medium can itself create problems: a message that is perfect for a letter may fail on a crackling phone call, so the channel must suit the message.

Feedback — the step that closes the loop Added / Expanded

Feedback is the receiver's response to the message, which enables the sender to evaluate how effective the communication was. It is the final component of the communication cycle: without it, the sender never knows whether the message was understood.

Good feedback is always specific, helpful and kind. Types you must know:

TypeMeaningExample
Descriptive feedbackSpecific information — written comments or verbal conversations — that tells the learner what to do to improve. It is relevant to the task being performed and lets the learner re-focus and improve mastery.“Your introduction is strong, but add one example in paragraph 2 so the reader can relate.”
Specific (evaluative) feedbackPoints to exactly what was right or wrong and how to fix it.“Question 3 is wrong because you forgot to convert cm to m.”
General feedbackVague praise or criticism with no direction — the weakest kind.“Good job” / “Not up to the mark.”
🎯 Exam tipIf a question says the receiver's message was not understood exactly as the sender intended, the answer the examiner is looking for is that the communicator should seek feedback to confirm understanding.

3. Principles of Effective Communication — the 7 Cs Added / Expanded

The support material mentions the 7 Cs in its question bank but prints no notes — here is the complete set. These seven principles make communication effective:

PrincipleWhat it meansExample
ClarityThe message must be simple, focused and easy to understand; use familiar words.“Submit the form by Friday 4 pm” beats “Kindly expedite form submission at the earliest.”
ConcisenessSay it in the least possible words — no unnecessary information. Saves time and cost, highlights the main message.“Meeting at 10 am, Room 12” instead of a paragraph.
ConcretenessUse specific facts and figures, not vague statements.“Sales rose 18%” not “sales rose a lot.”
CorrectnessNo grammatical, spelling or punctuation errors; accurate facts and figures; sent at the right time.Proofread an e-mail before sending.
CoherenceAll points must connect logically to the main idea; correct sequencing.First the reason, then the request — not the reverse.
CompletenessConvey all information the receiver needs — nothing left out. The five-question method (Who, What, When, Where, Why) checks completeness.An invitation with date, time, venue and RSVP contact.
CourtesyPoliteness and respect for the receiver's feelings.“Could you please…” rather than “Send it now.”
🧠 Remember — ConsiderationSome CBSE question banks also list Consideration: putting yourself in the receiver's place and taking into account both the receiver's viewpoint and feelings while preparing the message. If an MCQ describes “considering the receiver's viewpoints and feelings”, the CBSE textbook attributes this to Consideration (not Courtesy, even though some printed keys mark it otherwise).

Best practices for effective communication

  • Use simple language.
  • Be respectful of others' opinions.
  • Do not form assumptions based on culture, religion or geography.
  • Try to communicate in person as much as possible.
  • Use visuals to support the message.
  • Take help from a translator to overcome language differences.

4. Barriers to Effective Communication

A barrier is anything that prevents the message from reaching or being correctly understood by the receiver. Learn the five named barriers with one example each:

BarrierWhat it isExample
PhysicalEnvironmental and natural conditions that block the message on its way from sender to receiver.Loud traffic noise during a call; long distance; a cracked phone line.
Linguistic (language)The inability to communicate using a common language.Remya moves from India to Sweden without knowing Swedish and cannot find a part-time job.
InterpersonalOccurs when the sender's message is received differently from how it was intended — psychological distance caused by emotions, judgments and lack of trust.A withdrawn colleague reads a neutral remark as criticism.
OrganizationalSuperior–subordinate relationships in a formal structure block the free flow of communication.A junior fears telling the manager about a mistake.
CulturalPeople of different cultures cannot understand each other's customs, causing inconvenience and difficulty.A hand gesture that is friendly in one country is offensive in another.

Measures to overcome communication barriers

  1. Be prepared before communicating the message to the receiver.
  2. Do not rush — take sufficient time so the message is communicated correctly.
  3. Use simplified language with easily understood words and simple ideas.
  4. Maintain mutual respect between sender and receiver.
⚠️ Common mistakeStudents confuse interpersonal barriers (message interpreted differently) with physical barriers (message blocked externally). If the environment is the villain → physical; if the meaning is the victim → interpersonal. Speaking louder to drown out noise is a measure to overcome a barrier — not a barrier itself!

5. Writing Skills — Parts of Speech

Capitalisation rules

  • Capitalise the first word of every sentence: The bus arrives at 8 a.m.
  • Capitalise proper nouns — names of people (Ravi), places (Mumbai), days/months (Monday, March), languages (Hindi), organisations (KVS).
  • Capitalise the pronoun “I”.
  • Capitalise titles before names: Principal Gupta.
  • Do not capitalise common nouns: boy, city, school.

Punctuation essentials

MarkUseExample
Full stop (.)Ends a declarative sentenceWe finished the project.
Comma (,)Separates items / clauses / pausesI bought pens, books, and a bag.
Question mark (?)Ends a direct questionWhat is the deadline?
Exclamation mark (!)Shows strong emotion; follows interjectionsWow! We won!
Apostrophe (')Shows possession or contractionRiya's book; don't
Colon (:)Introduces a list or explanationBring three things: pen, paper, ruler.

🎯 Exam tipFavourite one-markers: a word used in place of a noun = pronoun; a word expressing emotion followed by “!” = interjection; “quickly” describing a verb = adverb.

6. Writing Skills — Sentences

Parts of a sentence

Every sentence has a subject (who/what the sentence is about), a verb (the action or state) and often an object (who/what receives the action).

Riya (subject)  +  wrote (verb)  +  a letter (object)

Direct and indirect objects

  • Direct object — directly receives the action; answers “what?” → She gifted a book.
  • Indirect object — the receiver of the direct object; answers “to whom/for whom?” → She gifted her brother a book.

Active and passive voice

VoiceStructureExample
ActiveSubject performs the action: Subject + Verb + ObjectThe chef cooked the meal.
PassiveSubject receives the action: Object + form of “be” + past participle + by + doerThe meal was cooked by the chef.
🧠 RememberUse active voice for clear, direct, energetic writing (preferred in business and technical communication); use passive voice when the doer is unknown or unimportant — “The classroom was cleaned.”

🧩 Assertion–Reason & Case-Study Questions

AR1. Assertion (A): Non-verbal communication is related to the expression of feelings and emotions.
Reason (R): Body language is a kind of verbal communication.

Show answer
(c) A is correct but R is incorrect. Non-verbal communication (gestures, expressions, body language) indeed expresses feelings. But body language is non-verbal — it uses no words — so R is wrong. (From the support material.)

AR2. Assertion (A): Feedback completes the communication cycle.
Reason (R): Feedback tells the sender whether the message was understood as intended.

Show answer
(a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A. The cycle closes only when the receiver's response returns to the sender.

AR3. Assertion (A): Speaking louder to make yourself heard over traffic noise removes the communication barrier.
Reason (R): Physical barriers can be reduced by changing the environment or the channel.

Show answer
(a) Both A and R are true and R correctly explains A. Raising your voice (or moving to a quieter place / switching to a message) is a measure to overcome a physical barrier.

Case Study 1 — The wrong channel

Ms. Iyer, the school coordinator, must inform 800 parents about a changed PTM date. She calls each parent personally but gives up after 40 calls as the day ends. Some parents she reached were shopping and could not hear her clearly; others misunderstood the new date. Finally, she sends a notice with the student diary — and every parent confirms.

  1. Which methods of communication did Ms. Iyer use, in order?
  2. Identify the barrier(s) that affected her phone calls.
  3. Why did the notice succeed where the calls failed?
Show answer

1. Interpersonal (verbal, oral) communication first, then written communication through a notice.

2. Physical barriers — noise at the parents' end and the sheer impracticality of 800 calls (distance/time); some misunderstanding also indicates the message was not confirmed with feedback.

3. A written notice is one-to-many, creates a permanent record, removes noise, and can be checked again — the channel finally matched the scale and purpose of the message.

Case Study 2 — Feedback that helps

Two teachers return an essay. Teacher A writes: “Nice.” Teacher B writes: “Your story has a clear beginning and end. To improve, add one example in the middle paragraph and check your spellings in lines 4–6.”

  1. Which teacher gave descriptive feedback? Justify.
  2. What qualities of good feedback does Teacher B's comment show?
Show answer

1. Teacher B — the comment is specific information that tells the student exactly what to do to improve, and is relevant to the task.

2. Specific (points to exact paragraphs and lines), helpful (actionable suggestions) and kind (starts with genuine praise).

Self-Management Skills – II

Managing stress with the ABC trick, working independently, knowing your strengths, motivating yourself, regulating your behaviour, setting SMART goals and mastering your time.

🎯 Learning Outcomes

  • Apply stress management techniques.
  • Demonstrate the ability to work independently.
  • Explain self-awareness, self-motivation and self-regulation.
  • Set SMART goals and manage time effectively.

📚 Study Notes

1. What is Self-Management?

Self-management, also referred to as self-control, is the ability to control one's emotions, thoughts and behaviour effectively in different situations. It also includes motivating oneself and setting goals. People with strong self-management skills do certain things better than others — they stay calm under pressure and keep working towards what matters.

Skills you must master to succeed in life

  • Self-awareness — knowing your own strengths, weaknesses, values and emotions.
  • Responsibility — owning your work, decisions and mistakes.
  • Time management — planning and controlling how you spend the hours of your day.
  • Adaptability — preparing yourself for new changes so you can transition seamlessly.

Importance of self-management

  • You become self-sufficient and independent.
  • Ownership and accountability lead to self-confidence.
  • You become goal-oriented and a strategy maker.
  • Self-monitoring and discipline reinforce good habits and behaviours.
  • You can organise your life and remove stress.

2. Stress and the ABC of Stress Management

Stress can be defined as our emotional, mental, physical and social reaction to any perceived demands or threats. These demands or threats are called stressors — stressors are the reason for stress.

Signs of stress Added / Expanded

LevelSymptoms
PhysicalHeadache, sleeplessness, tiredness, fast heartbeat, upset stomach
MentalIrritation, impatience, loneliness, inability to concentrate, constant worrying
EmotionalSadness, anxiety, nervousness, feeling overwhelmed
BehaviouralLoss of appetite, avoiding people, snapping at friends, procrastinating

Three steps to manage stress

  1. Be aware that you are stressed — look out for signs such as headache, sleeplessness, sadness, excessive worrying, nervousness.
  2. Identify what is causing the stress — exams? family pressure? money issues? poor food?
  3. Apply stress management methods — use time management tools, focus on the important tasks and get them done.

Simple stress management techniques

  • Time management — proper planning of time is one of the most effective stress-relieving techniques.
  • Physical exercise and fresh air — walking, skipping or sports stabilise mood, improve self-esteem and induce sleep. A healthy routine keeps stress low.
  • Yoga and meditation — yoga is a series of postures and breathing exercises practised to achieve control of body and mind; meditation focuses the mind into a calm state and deep breathing improves blood circulation and relaxes the body.
  • Healthy diet — a balanced diet (dal, roti, vegetables, fruits) gives the strength to do daily work efficiently.
  • Positivity — focusing on the negative aspects adds stress; learn to look at the good things.
  • Organising academic life; no delaying — organised class notes, assignments finished on time and tracked deadlines reduce stress greatly.
  • Sleep — at least 7 hours a night so brain and body recharge for the next day.
  • Holidays with family and friends — a break from routine, e.g. grandparents' place during vacation, and you return afresh. Recreational activities like movies, concerts, games, adventure sports, singing, dancing and sketching also lift you to a happier mental state.

Benefits of stress management

A joyful life · focus and completing tasks on time · a happier, stress-free you · more energy for quality time with friends and family. Chronic emotional stress can come from high expectations from oneself, while benefits of managing it include improved mood, boosted immunity, better efficiency and prevention of psychological disorders.

🎯 Exam tip“A series of postures and breathing exercises to control body and mind” = Yoga. Benefits-of-stress-management and two-recreational-activities are the most repeated 2-markers from this unit.

3. Ability to Work Independently

If you can become a calm and relaxed person, you will have the ability to work independently, which means:

  • Becoming self-aware, self-monitoring and self-correcting.
  • Knowing what you need to do.
  • Taking the initiative rather than being told what to do.
  • Recognising your mistakes and not blaming others.
  • Having the ability and the will to learn continuously.

Self-regulation and independent work: self-regulation guides independent individuals because it makes them consider long-term consequences rather than transient feelings. It leads to responsible, value-driven behaviour and teaches the self-control and consistent, well-directed effort needed to reach a goal.

4. Self-Awareness — Strengths and Weaknesses

Being self-aware means you can identify your own strengths and weaknesses. Know yourself first — your beliefs, background, opinions, choices and values.

Realising strengths and weaknesses

  1. Identify strengths — skills, abilities, interests, what you are good at and where you succeed.
  2. Identify weaknesses — shortcomings, apathies, where you face difficulty and defeat.
  3. Consider honest feedback from others (teachers, friends, family).
  4. Continue practising skills — strengths grow with use.
  5. Overcome weakness and improvise — convert weaknesses into strengths with deliberate effort.
🧠 RememberSelf-awareness matters because it lets you measure and convert weaknesses into strengths, and strengths into exceptional talents — the base of personal and professional success.

5. Self-Motivation

Self-motivation is the force within you that drives you to do things — what pushes us to achieve goals, feel happy and improve our quality of life. Formally: the ability to do things that need to be done without someone or something influencing us.

Types of motivation

TypeWhy we actExamples
Internal motivation (LOVE)We do things because they make us happy, healthy and feel good.Playing football for joy; drawing because you love it; Parvathi getting up at 4:30 am for badminton by herself.
External motivation (REWARD)We do things because they earn respect, recognition and appreciation.Studying for a certificate; Gokul running a 100 m race and winning a prize.

Qualities of self-motivated people

  • Aware of expectations from life.
  • Focused towards the goal.
  • Aware of the importance of things.
  • Dedicated to fulfilling their dream.

6. Self-Regulation, SMART Goals and Time Management

Self-regulation is the ability to manage your thoughts, emotions and behaviours in ways that help you achieve goals, stay aligned with your values, and adapt to different situations.

Goal setting

Goal setting is an essential factor in personal life — it helps you decide how to live, where you want to be and how you want to be in the future. Goals are a set of dreams with a deadline — e.g., saving pocket money to buy a favourite mobile phone by a particular date.

The SMART method of setting goals

LetterMeaningThe question it answers
SSpecific — clear and well-definedWho is involved? What do I want to do? Where do I start? When do I start and finish? Which means do I use? Why am I doing this?
MMeasurable — progress can be trackedHow much? How many? How do I know I have achieved results?
AAchievable — breaking big goals into smaller parts makes them achievableCan I realistically reach it step by step?
RRealistic — something we want to achieve and can work towards“I will revise my subjects 3 hours daily after school to score well” — possible and practical?
TTime-bound — has a deadlineBy when must it be finished?
🎯 Exam tipR = Realistic in this syllabus (not “relevant”). A 2-mark favourite: “What does R stand for in SMART? Explain.”

Time management — four steps

Time management is the ability to plan and control how you spend the hours of your day well, and do all that you want to do. It helps you complete tasks on time, make a daily timetable, estimate how long work will take, submit homework on time, and not waste time during the day.

  1. Organise — plan your day-to-day activities.
  2. Prioritise — make a to-do list of all activities and rank them in order of importance.
  3. Control — have control over your activities and your time.
  4. Track — identify and note where your time was spent and analyse how to spend it more effectively.

AR1. Assertion (A): Self-management skills include the ability to work independently.
Reason (R): An independent worker takes initiative and recognises his own mistakes without blaming others.

Show answer
(a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A. Initiative and owning mistakes are exactly the behaviours that define independent working, which is part of self-management.

AR2. Assertion (A): External motivation lasts longer than internal motivation.
Reason (R): Internal motivation comes from rewards and recognition given by others.

Show answer
Both A and R are false. It is internal motivation that lasts longer and sustains effort; and rewards/recognition given by others describe external motivation, not internal — so R defines the wrong category. Key point: internal (LOVE) motivation sustains; rewards and recognition are external. (New board-pattern question.)

AR3. Assertion (A): Stress management improves focus and efficiency.
Reason (R): A stress-free mind wastes energy on constant worrying.

Show answer
(c) A is true but R is false. Stress management does improve mood, immunity and efficiency; but a stress-free mind does not waste energy on worrying — worrying is what stress causes.

Case Study — Exam-time anxiety

Deepika is sincere and studious, but the thought of exams creates anxiety. She stays awake late, skips meals, and keeps thinking about results. Her friend Manoj suggests planning a timetable, doing 20 minutes of morning yoga, keeping notes organised from day one, and watching a comedy film on Sunday with family.

  1. Which symptoms of stress is Deepika showing?
  2. List any four stress management techniques that can help her.
  3. Which type of motivation keeps a sincere student going even without anyone pushing her?
Show answer

1. Sleeplessness (physical), constant worrying about results (mental), anxiety/nervousness (emotional), skipped meals (behavioural).

2. Time management (a realistic timetable), yoga/meditation and deep breathing, organising academic life (no delaying — notes and assignments on time), sufficient sleep, healthy diet, positivity, and recreation/holidays with family (any four).

3. Internal (self) motivation — the LOVE kind that makes her studious without external rewards.

1. What is Sustainable Development?

Sustainable development means growing and meeting our needs today without harming the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. This idea of fairness between generations is called intergenerational equity — the fair distribution of resources between current and future generations.

The three pillars / main areas

  • Economic growth — earning money and building resources.
  • Environmental protection — saving nature and natural resources.
  • Social well-being — helping everyone live a good and fair life.

Sustainable development is the balanced integration of all three — not any one alone.

Why is it important?

  • Saves natural resources so we don't run out of them.
  • Protects the environment by reducing pollution and damage.
  • Supports future generations by keeping nature healthy for them.
🧠 RememberDefinition one-liner for exams: “Meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.” Green skills are the knowledge and abilities needed to protect the environment and use resources carefully — they support eco-friendly industries and reduce harm to nature.

1. The AI Project Cycle

The AI Project Cycle is a step-by-step process to solve problems using proven scientific methods and drawing inferences from them. Its components are the steps that contribute to completing the project — it distributes development into stages so the work becomes easier, clearly understandable, and each stage stays specific, giving the best possible output.

  1. Problem Scoping — understanding the problem. You set the goal by stating the problem you wish to solve. Look at the parameters which affect the problem so the picture becomes clearer (use the 4Ws canvas below).
  2. Data Acquisition — collecting accurate and reliable data from various authentic sources. The data becomes the base of your project — it helps you understand the parameters related to the problem.
  3. Data Exploration — arranging the data uniformly and giving it a visual image: graphs, databases, flow charts, maps. Visualisation makes it easier to interpret the patterns the acquired data follows.
  4. Modelling — creating models from the data. Research online and select models that give a suitable output; test the candidate models and figure out the most efficient one. The most efficient model becomes the base of your project, and you develop your algorithm around it.
  5. Evaluation — evaluating the project. Test the model on newly fetched data; the results help you evaluate and improve it.
  6. Deployment — from development to real-world application. The solution transitions into the real world, delivering value and impact to users and stakeholders.

4. Ethical Frameworks for AI

Ethics are a set of values or morals which help us separate right from wrong. Frameworks are sets of steps that provide a step-by-step guide for solving problems in an organised manner. Ethical frameworks are therefore frameworks which help us ensure the choices we make do not cause unintended harm — they ensure AI makes morally acceptable choices, and using them while building AI solutions helps avoid unintended outcomes before they take place.

Two main categories

  1. Sector-based frameworks — tailored to specific sectors or industries. One common example is Bioethics, focused on ethical considerations in healthcare and life sciences (patient privacy, data security, ethical AI in medical decision-making).

Advanced Concepts of Modeling in AI

📚 Study Notes

1. AI, ML and Deep Learning

Artificial Intelligence (AI) — when machines behave like humans and make smart decisions. Example: a robot that can chat with you.

Machine Learning (ML) — when machines learn from data and improve automatically. Example: a movie app that suggests your favourite type of films.

Deep Learning (DL) — a part of ML where machines learn deeply using lots of data and complex (neural) networks. Example: your phone recognising your face to unlock.

What is Machine Learning?

ML is learning from data: input = past data (like pictures of fruits), output = predictions (identifying a fruit as an apple or a strawberry). ML improves itself over time using experience. Examples of ML tasks:

  • Object classification — spotting a dog or cat in an image.
  • Anomaly detection — finding something odd, like an unusual heartbeat in health data.

Deep Learning

A special kind of ML that uses neural networks to understand complex things and learns from huge datasets (thousands of images). You show a bird photo — the DL model sees it pixel by pixel and says “That's a bird!”. Used in face recognition, voice assistants and language translation.

2. Common Terms — Data, Features, Labels, Training & Testing

  • Data — raw information.
  • Features — the columns of a table; the details about data (colour, size, price). Formally: individual measurable properties or characteristics of a dataset.
  • Labels — what you are trying to predict (the fruit's name); data labelling is the process of attaching meaning to data.

4. The Three Types of Learning Models

Supervised learning

Labelled data is used for training. The machine learns from examples and makes predictions. Example: you show a coin and its weight; the model learns 5 g = euro, 3 g = dollar; later it guesses the coin from its weight. Applications: spam e-mail classification, predicting student pass/fail, tag suggestions in photos.

Unsupervised learning

Unlabelled data is used; there are no labels. The machine finds patterns or groups by itself. Example: 1000 photos of dogs with no info — the model groups them by size or colour. Applications: customer segmentation, recommendation systems, anomaly detection, fraud detection.

Reinforcement learning

Learn by rewards. The machine (agent) tries something — it gets a reward for right actions and a penalty for wrong ones, and it optimises to maximise rewards. Example: you show a fruit; the model says “cherry” → wrong → negative point; tries again → “apple” → right → reward. Used in self-driving cars, games and robotics.

6. Deep Learning — Neural Networks (ANN) and CNN

A Neural Network mimics how our brain works, using “neurons”. An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is a computing system inspired by the human brain, made of layers of interconnected nodes that process data and learn patterns. It is a fast, efficient way to solve problems for which the dataset is very large. Every node in the hidden layers is essentially a machine learning algorithm.

The three layers

  1. Input layer — acquires data and feeds it to the network; no processing occurs here.
  2. Processing / hidden layers — each node has its own machine learning algorithm which it executes on the data received; each node learns patterns and relationships. “Hidden” because they are not directly visible — they process internal computations. There can be multiple hidden layers depending on the complexity of the function; this layer performs most of the computation.
  3. Output layer — produces the final prediction; like the input layer, it does not process the data it acquires.

Weights and biases adjust the influence of input nodes — weights decide how strongly each input matters, and the bias shifts the decision boundary. ANNs use weights, biases and activation functions. A neural network with multiple layers is used to process complex data like images and text.

Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)

A CNN is a deep-learning algorithm that can take in an input image, assign importance (learnable weights and biases) to various aspects/objects in the image, and differentiate one from the other. (Covered in detail in Unit 4 — Computer Vision.)

7. The Perceptron Model Added / Expanded

A perceptron is a simple neural network that performs linear classification. It takes inputs, multiplies each by a weight (importance), adds them with a bias, and passes the result through an activation function (a step function) to give a yes/no output. The perceptron algorithm can only solve linearly separable problems.

Output = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₃x₃ + w₄x₄ − b

Worked example — “Should I go to the park?” (from the support material)

Factors (inputs): Do I have a jacket? (x₁) · Do I have an umbrella? (x₂) · Is it sunny now? (x₃) · Is the forecast good? (x₄) — each Yes = 1, No = 0.

Weights (importance): w₁ = 1.5 (jacket helps), w₂ = 1.0 (umbrella matters in rain), w₃ = 3.0 (sunny now is most important), w₄ = 2.5 (good forecast is next most important). Bias b = 4.0 — a higher bias favours staying cautious (staying in), a lower bias favours going out.

Case 1: jacket = 1, umbrella = 0, sunny = 1, forecast not good = 0:
Result = 1×1.5 + 0×1.0 + 1×3.0 + 0×2.5 − 4.0 = 1.5 + 3.0 − 4.0 = +0.5 → ≥ 0 → Go outside.

Case 2: jacket = 0, umbrella = 1, sunny = 0, forecast good = 1:
Result = 0×1.5 + 1×1.0 + 0×3.0 + 1×2.5 − 4.0 = 1.0 + 2.5 − 4.0 = −0.5 → < 0 → Don't go outside.

🧠 RememberThe bias shifts the decision boundary — it does not increase features or set the learning rate. The step activation function outputs Yes if the result is ≥ 0, No if < 0.

Evaluation

How good is your AI model? Train–test splitting, overfitting, the confusion matrix, and the four big metrics — accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score — with fully worked numerical examples.

🎯 Learning Outcomes

  • Recognise common metrics used to evaluate AI models.
  • Derive and calculate the evaluation metrics.
  • Recognise the most suitable evaluation metric for a given application.

📚 Study Notes

1. What is Evaluation?

Evaluation is the process of using different evaluation metrics to understand a machine learning model's performance — feeding the test dataset into the model and comparing its outputs with the actual answers. It helps you understand the model's strengths, weaknesses and suitability for the task at hand.

The two parameters considered for evaluating a model are prediction and reality:

  • Prediction — the output given by the machine.
  • Reality — the real scenario, the actual value at the time the prediction was made.

Train–test split — why we need it

Train-test split is a technique for evaluating the performance of a machine learning algorithm: the dataset is divided into two subsets — the training dataset and the testing dataset. It works for any supervised learning algorithm and is appropriate when a sufficiently large dataset is available.

Overfitting

If we use the same data to train and to evaluate, the model simply remembers the whole training set and will always predict the correct label for any point in it — this is called overfitting. Such a model scores perfectly on training data but fails to predict future observations on any unseen dataset. Example: a flower/vegetable classifier labels every training image correctly but fails on a new image not in the training set. That is why the model's performance must be estimated with test data it has never seen.

🧠 Underfitting (added)The opposite failure — the model is too simple and performs poorly even on training data because it has not learned the patterns at all. Overfit = memorised; underfit = never learned; a good model generalises.

Each cell counts the predictions falling into that combination. From it we can calculate accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score.

3. The Four Metrics — Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1

Accuracy

Accuracy measures the total number of predictions a model gets right — the percentage of times the predictions out of all observations are correct. It takes into account all correct predictions (TP + TN). Model performance and accuracy are directly proportional: better performance → more accurate predictions.

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)

Accuracy is only suitable when there is an equal number of observations in each class (balanced dataset) and all errors are equally important. For unbalanced data, use precision, recall or F1.

Precision

Precision is the rate at which desirable predictions turn out to be correct — the ratio of correctly classified positive examples to the total predicted positives. It is the percentage of true positives versus all cases where the prediction is true.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

When to use: unbalanced datasets where False Positives are dangerous and must be reduced as much as possible.

Recall (Sensitivity / True Positive Rate)

Recall measures the model correctly identifying True Positives — the ratio of correct positive predictions to the total actual positive values.

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

When to use: unbalanced datasets where False Negatives are dangerous and must be reduced as much as possible.

F1-score

F1 combines precision and recall into a single measure (harmonic mean) — used when the dataset is unbalanced and we cannot decide whether FP or FN matters more.

F1 = 2 × Precision × Recall / (Precision + Recall)

A high F1-score indicates a good balance between precision and recall; a large imbalance between them drags F1 down.

🎯 Which metric when? (classic 4-marker)
  • Spam e-mail blocking → Precision — a false positive (real mail marked spam) must be minimised.
  • Gold mining → Precision — wrongly labelling a non-gold area as gold wastes everything; FP must fall.
  • Viral outbreak detection → Recall — a false negative (infected person missed) is life-threatening; FN must fall.
  • Can't decide which error hurts more → F1-score.

3. Understanding Images — Pixels and Resolution

A pixel (picture element) is the smallest unit of an image. Each pixel holds colour and brightness information. More pixels = higher resolution and better image clarity. Resolution is expressed as width × height in pixels.

Types of images

  • Grayscale images — only black, white and shades of gray. Each pixel has one intensity value from 0 (black) to 255 (white) — one byte per pixel; the value indicates the brightness level.
  • RGB images — use three channels, Red, Green and Blue, each 0–255. Every colour is a mix of the three.

Image features — what AI looks for

  • Edges — sharp colour changes, useful for object shapes. Edge detection finds object outlines.
  • Corners — meeting points of edges, used in 3D detection.
  • Blobs — similar-colour regions, used in medical scans.

Example: detecting the edges of car number plates using CV.

4. Convolution and Kernels

Convolution is a method to change an image by using a small matrix called a kernel. The kernel slides (moves) over the image and recomputes each pixel value from its neighbourhood to:

  • Detect edges
  • Blur or sharpen the image
  • Remove noise

Example: in CCTV footage, convolution helps sharpen blurry images. Common kernels include edge-detection kernels (like Sobel), blur (box/mean) kernels and sharpen kernels.

🧠 Kernel = the small matrix · Convolution = the sliding-and-combining operationThe kernel's numbers decide the effect: an edge kernel makes boundaries pop; a blur kernel averages neighbours, softening the image.

5. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)

CNNs are deep learning models specially made for analysing images — their main goal is pattern detection in visuals. The pipeline:

The layers

  1. Convolution layer — finds patterns like edges and textures; it is the layer responsible for learning key features (using learnable weights and biases).
  2. ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) — adds non-linearity to make learning better; it removes negative values (outputs 0 for them).
  3. Pooling layer — shrinks (downsamples) the image while keeping the main parts; reduces processing complexity, uses less memory and makes features position-invariant. Types:
    • Max pooling — takes the largest value in each window (best for feature selection).
    • Average pooling — takes the average value.
    Example: compressing satellite images while keeping key features.
  4. Fully connected layer — the final step: uses all the extracted features to predict the result and outputs a class label (e.g., cat or dog). Example: AI detecting whether an X-ray shows a healthy or diseased lung.

Real-world use: Instagram uses CNNs to detect your face for filters.

1. Natural Language and Why It's Hard for Computers

A natural language (like English, Hindi or Tamil) is a human language — unlike artificial or machine languages (Python, C++). Natural languages follow rules but are also flexible, changing over time and with use. They show creativity and efficiency, letting us communicate in new ways.

Why human languages are complicated for a computer

  • Perfect syntax, no meaning — a statement can be grammatically perfect yet mean nothing (“Colourless green ideas sleep furiously”). A balance of syntax and semantics is needed for understanding.

4. Text Normalization — the Six Steps

The whole textual data from all documents taken together is called the corpus. Text normalization converts this corpus into its simplest machine-usable form, step by step:

  1. Sentence segmentation — the whole corpus is divided into sentences; each sentence is taken as a different data point.
  2. Tokenisation — each sentence is divided into tokens: every word, number or special character becomes a separate token.
  3. Removing stop words, special characters and numbers — unnecessary tokens are removed. Stop words are the most common words of a language — “the”, “a”, “on”, “is”, “all”, “this”, “do” — that carry little meaning. They can be removed using NLTK (Natural Language Toolkit), a suite of libraries for symbolic and statistical NLP. Note: “Things” is NOT a stop word — it carries meaning.
  4. Converting text to a common case — after stop-word removal, convert everything to the same case, preferably lower case, so that case-sensitivity does not treat “The” and “the” as different words.
  5. Stemming — reduces remaining words to their root form by removing affixes. Like cutting branches of a tree down to its stems: eating → eat, eats → eat, eaten → eat. The stem of “Making” is Mak (stems may not be real words).
  6. Lemmatisation — like stemming, but the result (the lemma) is always a meaningful, dictionary word: the lemma of “Making” is Make; of “caring” is Care; of “programmer/programming/programs” → “program”. It groups different forms of the same word together so any version of a word retrieves the same results.

Bag of Words is an NLP model which helps extract features from text so machine learning algorithms can use them. It treats a document as a collection (“bag”) of words, ignoring word order and grammar and focusing on the frequency of each word. Bag of Words gives us two things: a vocabulary of words for the corpus, and the frequency of these words (how many times each occurred).

6. TF-IDF — Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency Added / Expanded

The support material asks TF-IDF numerals in its question bank but prints no notes — learn the full method. TF-IDF is an NLP technique that evaluates the importance of different words in a sentence or document, useful for text classification and helping a machine learning model weigh words.

  • Term Frequency (TF) — frequency of a word in one document (read off the document vector table).
  • Document Frequency (DF) — the number of documents in which the word occurs, irrespective of how many times it occurs in those documents.
  • Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) — total documents ÷ document frequency: common words (in many documents) get low weight; rare words get high weight.
TFIDF (W) = TF(W) × log (IDF(W)) where IDF = total documents / DF

Stop-word + special-character removal, lower case: and/are/to/the/but/with/a/an removed; all tokens lower-cased → [raj][vijay][best][friends] …

Stemming/lemmatisation: play → play; likes → like; wants → want; friends → friend; games → game; gamer → game; footballer → football(er). Final normalized tokens are ready for the Bag of Words step.

⚡ Quick Revision — Last-Minute Notes

  • NLP = computer science + AI + linguistics → machines understand/process/create human language.
  • Natural language: flexible, creative, context-dependent — hard for machines.
  • Syntax = grammar structure; semantics = meaning.
  • 5 stages: Lexical → Syntactic → Semantic → Discourse → Pragmatic.
  • Normalization: sentence segmentation (first step) → tokenisation → stop-word removal → common case → stemming → lemmatisation.
  • Stop words: the, a, on, is, all, this, do — removed with NLTK.
  • Stem of Making = Mak; lemma of Making = Make; lemma of caring = Care.
  • Corpus = all text together; 12 documents → 12 document vectors.
  • BoW = vocabulary + frequencies; ignores order; document vector table.
  • TFIDF = TF × log(IDF); IDF = total docs / DF; rewards rare, document-specific words.
  • Chatbots: 24×7, multiple queries; script bots (no AI, easy) vs smart bots (NLP+ML, coding, big databases).

📖 Glossary

  • Natural languageHuman language (English, Hindi…) — flexible and evolving, unlike machine languages.
  • NLPField combining computer science, AI and linguistics to process human language.
  • Syntax / SemanticsGrammatical structure of a sentence / its meaning.
  • LexiconThe vocabulary/dictionary of a language used in lexical analysis.
  • CorpusThe entire textual data from all documents taken together.
  • TokenisationDividing text into component words, numbers and symbols.
  • Stop wordsVery common words (the, is, on) carrying little meaning — removed during normalization.
  • StemmingCutting words to their base form by removing affixes (result may be non-words).
  • LemmatisationReducing words to their meaningful dictionary form (the lemma).
  • Bag of WordsModel representing text by word frequency, ignoring order and grammar.
  • Document vector tableTable whose header is the vocabulary and rows are documents with word counts.
  • TF / DF / IDFWord frequency in one document / number of documents containing the word / total docs ÷ DF.
  • NLTKNatural Language Toolkit — Python libraries for symbolic and statistical NLP.
  • Script bot / Smart botRule-based chatbot with no AI / NLP-ML chatbot that learns and works on large databases.
  • Sentiment analysisIdentifying emotions and opinions in text.
  • Machine translationAutomatic translation from one language to another.

Program 1 — Sentiment analysis with NLTK (VADER)

Objective: Determine the sentiment (positive / negative / neutral) of a given text.

# Program 1: Sentiment Analysis using NLTK
from nltk.sentiment.vader import SentimentIntensityAnalyzer
import nltk

# Download the VADER lexicon (needed only the first time)
nltk.download("vader_lexicon")

Output:

{'neg': 0.0, 'neu': 0.456, 'pos': 0.544, 'compound': 0.7003}

Concept covered: VADER gives negative (neg), neutral (neu), positive (pos) proportions and a compound score — a positive compound value means overall positive sentiment.

Program 2 — Stop-word removal

Objective: Remove common English stop words from a given sentence.

# Program 2: Stop Words Removal
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize
import nltk

# Download required resources (first time only)
nltk.download("stopwords")
nltk.download("punkt")

example_sent = "This is a sample sentence, showing stop word filtration."

# Load English stop words as a set
stop_words = set(stopwords.words("english"))

# Tokenize the sentence, then keep only non-stop words
word_tokens = word_tokenize(example_sent)
filtered = [w for w in word_tokens if w not in stop_words]
print(filtered)

Output:

['example', 'sentence', ',', 'showing', 'stop', 'words', 'filtration', '.']

Concept covered: tokenisation splits the sentence into tokens; stop words (this, is, a, showing…) carry little meaning and are filtered out.

Program 3 — Bag of Words with scikit-learn

Objective: Create a Bag of Words matrix from a small corpus.

# Program 3: Bag of Words
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer

    "Mumbai is a famous place",
