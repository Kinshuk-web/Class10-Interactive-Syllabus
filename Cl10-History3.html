Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

Globalisation is not a recent phenomenon. For centuries people have traded, migrated and invested across distances, and this chapter traces that history through three types of flows — of goods, of labour and of capital — within international economic exchange.

The pre-modern world was already connected: the silk routes linked Asia with Europe and northern Africa; food travelled — noodles and spaghetti, potato, soya, tomato, chilli, maize and sweet potato — as did diseases such as smallpox, which devastated the Americas after Columbus. Silver from Peru and Mexico flowed to Europe and then to China, and the Indian Ocean carried cowries, textiles and spices.

The nineteenth century (1815–1914) saw a dramatic expansion: the abolition of the Corn Laws made cheap food imports possible, railways and refrigerated ships transformed the movement of goods, and capital and labour moved on a vast scale — including indentured labour from India to the Caribbean, Mauritius and Fiji, and the spread of rinderpest that destroyed African cattle. The two World Wars and the Great Depression of 1929 then shattered this order, and the post-war world was rebuilt on the Bretton Woods institutions — the IMF and the World Bank — with fixed exchange rates, later challenged by the G-77 and the demand for a New International Economic Order.

Since the 1990s a new phase of globalisation has seen MNCs relocate production to Asian countries — China and India especially — while the older flows of trade, labour and capital continue.

The Pre-Modern World — Silk Routes, Food and Disease

Globalisation refers to the integration of economies and societies across the world. But it is not only a modern phenomenon: for centuries people have travelled, traded and settled across distances, carrying goods, ideas, religions and diseases with them.

The silk routes

The silk routes are a good example of pre-modern trade and cultural links between distant parts of the world. The name “silk routes” points to the importance of West-bound Chinese silk cargoes along these routes, but historians have identified several silk routes over land and by sea, knitting together vast regions of Asia and linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa. Trade and cultural exchange always went hand in hand: Christian missionaries travelled along these routes, as did Buddhist monks, and the routes carried religions, art and ideas along with goods.

Food travels

Pre-modern trade also carried food. Many of the foods we think of as “Indian” or “European” arrived from elsewhere: noodles and spaghetti travelled from China to the West; the potato, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomato, chilli and sweet potato came to the Indian subcontinent only in the sixteenth century after Columbus’s voyages, while wheat, rice, barley and pulses had travelled the other way. The potato became so important in Europe that it was blamed for the Irish famine when the crop failed in the 1840s.

Conquest, disease and silver

The pre-modern world shrank greatly in the sixteenth century after European sailors found a sea route to Asia and successfully crossed the western ocean to America. For centuries the Indian Ocean had known a bustling trade, with goods, people, knowledge and customs crossing its waters, and the entry of the Europeans on the scene changed the pattern.

The Portuguese and Spanish conquest and colonisation of America was decisively underway by the mid-sixteenth century. European conquest was not just a result of superior firepower — the most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors was the germs such as those of smallpox that they carried on their person. Because of their long isolation, America’s original inhabitants had no immunity against these diseases, which proved to be a deadly threat: smallpox spread deep into the continent, and preceded even the arrival of Europeans in some places, decimating whole communities.

Precious metals — particularly silver — enhanced Europe’s wealth and financed its trade with Asia. Legends spread in seventeenth-century Europe about El Dorado, the fabled city of gold in South America, and many expeditions set off in search of it. The Portuguese and Spanish took silver from the mines of Peru and Mexico, shipped it to Europe, and from there it flowed to Asia — much of it to China, where it was used as money.

The Indian Ocean and cowries

The Indian Ocean trade brought cowries — small shells — from the Maldives to Bengal and to Africa, where they were used as currency. Indian textiles, especially cotton and silk, and spices travelled west, and Chinese porcelain and silk travelled along the same routes. The cowrie is a good example of how a single commodity could link distant economies long before modern industry.

The Nineteenth Century — Three Flows

The nineteenth century — roughly 1815 to 1914 — saw a dramatic expansion of the world economy, which historians describe through three flows: trade in goods, movement of labour, and movement of capital.

The three flows
FlowWhat movedExamples
Trade in goodsFood, raw materials and manufacturesCheap corn and meat to Britain; cotton, indigo, tea and opium from India; British manufactures to the world
Movement of labourWorkers migrating to where work was availableIndentured labourers from India to the Caribbean, Mauritius and Fiji; Europeans to America and Australia
Movement of capitalMoney invested abroadBritish capital in railways, plantations and mines in India and elsewhere

Food and the Corn Laws

Until the eighteenth century, countries were ruled by kings and emperors and trade was regulated by them. In Britain, the Corn Laws restricted the import of cheap corn (food) in order to protect British farmers. Their abolition in the mid-nineteenth century let cheaper food into Britain, and British agriculture could not compete; but it also meant that British industry could be fed more cheaply, and that food began to travel around the world.

Two technological changes transformed this: railways, which carried goods quickly overland to ports, and refrigerated ships, which made it possible to transport perishable food across long distances. As a result, meat, butter and eggs from America and Australia could reach European markets, and by the 1890s a “flood of cheap food” had transformed diets.

Rinderpest and Africa

Rinderpest — cattle plague — arrived in Africa in the late 1880s. It spread rapidly, killing about 90 per cent of the cattle in the areas it reached. Since cattle were the main form of wealth and the basis of livelihoods for many African communities, the loss was catastrophic: people lost their means of livelihood and were forced into the labour market — which is exactly what the colonial planters and mine owners wanted. The conquest and subjugation of Africa was thus helped by a disease.

Indentured labour

The nineteenth century also saw the migration of labour on a vast scale. After the abolition of slavery in the British empire in 1833, plantation owners needed workers, and they turned to indentured labour — a bonded labourer under contract to work for an employer for a specific period, often in a distant country, in return for passage and wages.

  • Labourers were recruited from India (mainly Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Andhra), and also from China and the Pacific islands.
  • They were sent to the Caribbean (Trinidad, Guyana, Surinam), Mauritius, Fiji, South Africa and Malaya, to work on plantations and in mines.
  • The contracts were called “girmit”, and the labourers “girmitiyas”, after the English word “agreement”.
  • Conditions were harsh: long hours, low wages, poor housing, and little chance of return. Recruiters often deceived the poor with false promises.
  • Indentured labour was abolished in 1921, after pressure from nationalists in India and the report of the Chambers of Commerce and the Indian National Congress, and after Gandhiji’s campaign in South Africa.

The indentured labourers who survived settled in their new countries and became the ancestors of large populations — Indians in Trinidad, Guyana, Mauritius and Fiji — whose culture, food and festivals carry the memory of that migration.

India and the World Economy in the Nineteenth Century

India’s integration into the world economy in the nineteenth century took several forms. India exported raw materials and food grains — cotton, indigo, tea, opium, wheat and rice — and imported manufactured goods from Britain. Indigo, grown under oppressive conditions, was shipped to Europe for dyeing; opium was exported to China; and tea from Assam became a major export.

British capital flowed into India — into railways, plantations, mines and ports — and the railways knitted the interior to the ports, carrying goods out and manufactures in. But the integration was unequal: while exports of raw materials grew, Indian handicrafts declined in the face of cheap machine-made imports, and the export of food grains contributed to famines even as food was shipped abroad.

India was also a market for British goods and a source of revenue, and it became the largest source of indentured labour — a flow of people that changed the demography of several distant colonies.

The Inter-War Economy — the Great Depression

The First World War (1914–18) was fought on an unprecedented scale and destroyed the pre-war economic order: it killed and disabled millions of working-age men, disrupted trade, and left countries in debt. The 1920s saw a brief boom, but the world economy was unstable, and in 1929 it collapsed.

Causes of the Great Depression

  1. Agricultural overproduction: after the war, farmers expanded production, and prices fell. Farmers tried to produce more to maintain their incomes, which pushed prices down further, and huge stocks accumulated.
  2. Withdrawal of US loans: in the 1920s the United States had lent heavily to Europe. When US banks faced difficulty, they withdrew these loans, and countries that depended on them collapsed.
  3. Bank failures and the collapse of credit: as banks failed, people lost their savings, credit dried up, businesses closed and unemployment rose.

The Depression was not confined to the United States. It affected the whole world because economies were now linked by trade, loans and the gold standard. World trade halved between 1929 and 1932, and unemployment reached enormous levels — in the USA, millions were out of work and queued for relief.

The Depression in India

India was also badly hit. India’s exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934, and prices fell sharply. Peasants suffered most: their produce fetched much less, while the revenue demand remained fixed, so many fell into debt and lost their land. In Bengal, jute growers were badly affected as the price of jute fell; in Tamil Nadu peasants were ruined; and the Great Depression made the condition of the Indian peasantry desperate, feeding into the political unrest of the 1930s. The colonial government’s refusal to reduce revenue deepened the distress.

The Post-War World — Bretton Woods and After

The Second World War (1939–45) was even more destructive, and the post-war world was rebuilt on new institutions. In July 1944, the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference met at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire, USA, and set up two institutions: the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (the World Bank) — known as the “Bretton Woods twins”.

  • The IMF was set up to deal with external surpluses and deficits and to promote exchange rate stability; the World Bank was set up to finance post-war reconstruction.
  • The system was based on fixed exchange rates, with currencies pegged to the US dollar, which in turn was convertible into gold at a fixed rate.
  • The Bretton Woods system ended in the early 1970s, when the United States suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold and the major currencies began to float.

Decolonisation and the G-77

As colonies became independent, the newly independent countries found that the Bretton Woods institutions were dominated by the former colonial powers, and that the terms of trade were against them. They organised to demand a fairer deal: the Group of 77 (G-77) — a coalition of 77 developing countries formed in the 1960s — pressed for a New International Economic Order (NIEO), which would give them:

  • better prices for their raw materials;
  • more development assistance;
  • fairer terms for technology transfer; and
  • a greater voice in international institutions.

The G-77 can thus be seen as a reaction to the activities of the Bretton Woods twins: it argued that the system designed by the rich countries served the rich countries, and that the developing world needed different terms.

The new globalisation

From the 1990s a new phase of globalisation began. MNCs relocated production to Asian countries where labour was cheap and markets were growing — China became the world’s workshop and India a centre of services and manufacturing — while trade in goods, services and capital expanded rapidly. This new phase has produced growth and also new inequalities, and it is the phase discussed in the Economics chapters.

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

Give two examples of different types of global exchange which have taken place before the twentieth century.

Model answer

(i) The silk routes, along which Chinese silk, Indian textiles and spices moved west while Buddhism and Christianity moved with the traders. (ii) The Columbian exchange after 1492 — potato, maize, tomato and chilli to Europe and Asia; silver from Peru and Mexico to Europe and China; and smallpox to the Americas.

2.

Explain what is referred to as the G-77 countries. In what ways can G-77 be seen as a reaction to the activities of the Bretton Woods twins?

Model answer

The G-77 is a group of 77 developing countries formed in the 1960s to press for a New International Economic Order — better prices for their raw materials, more development assistance, fairer terms of technology transfer and a greater voice in international institutions. It was a reaction to the IMF and the World Bank because those institutions were dominated by the rich, former colonial powers and their policies were seen to serve the developed world: developing countries found the terms of trade against them, and the Bretton Woods system gave them little say. The G-77 was their collective answer.

3.

Explain the three types of movements or flows within international economic exchange. Find one example of each involving India.

Model answer

Flow of goods: cotton, indigo, tea, opium and food grains were exported from India while British manufactures were imported. Flow of labour: indentured labourers from Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Andhra went to Trinidad, Guyana, Mauritius, Fiji and South Africa. Flow of capital: British capital was invested in Indian railways, plantations, mines and ports.

4.

Explain the causes of the Great Depression.

Model answer

Three causes. Agricultural overproduction: after the war farmers expanded output and, as prices fell, tried to produce even more, driving prices further down and piling up stocks. Withdrawal of US loans: in the 1920s the United States lent heavily abroad; when US banks ran into difficulty they called in these loans, and countries dependent on them collapsed. Bank failures and the collapse of credit: banks closed, savings were lost, credit dried up, businesses failed and unemployment soared — and because economies were linked by trade and the gold standard, the Depression spread across the world.

5.

Write a note on the effects of the British government’s decision to abolish the Corn Laws.

Model answer

The abolition of the Corn Laws in the mid-nineteenth century allowed cheaper food to be imported into Britain. British agriculture could not compete and many farmers were ruined, and land went out of cultivation — but industrialists gained, because cheap food kept wages low and profits high, and British industry could export more. It also encouraged food production across the world: with railways and refrigerated ships, meat, butter and grain from America and Australia reached British markets, integrating distant farms into a world market.

6.

Write a note on the effects of the coming of rinderpest to Africa.

Model answer

Rinderpest arrived in Africa in the late 1880s and killed about 90 per cent of the cattle in the areas it reached. Since cattle were the main form of wealth and the basis of livelihood for many African communities, the loss was catastrophic: people lost their means of living and were forced into the labour market to work for colonial planters and mine owners. In this way a cattle disease helped the colonial conquest and subjugation of Africa — the loss of African independence was accompanied by the loss of African livelihoods.

7.

Write a note on the effects of the death of men of working age in Europe because of the First World War.

Model answer

Because the First World War was fought with heavy artillery and machine guns, it killed and disabled millions of men of working age. This reduced the workforce in Europe, lowered production, left large numbers of widows and orphans, and forced women into the labour market. It also left countries heavily in debt and disrupted the international economy — and the terms of the peace treaties created resentments that fed into the next war.

8.

Write a note on the effects of the Great Depression on the Indian economy.

Model answer

India’s exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934, and prices fell sharply. Peasants suffered most: the prices of their produce collapsed while revenue demands remained fixed, so many fell into debt and lost land. Jute growers in Bengal were badly hit as the price of jute fell, and peasants in Tamil Nadu were ruined. The colonial government’s refusal to reduce revenue deepened the distress, and rural discontent fed into the political movements of the 1930s.

9.

Write a note on the effects of the decision of MNCs to relocate production to Asian countries.

Model answer

It has created jobs and growth in Asia — China became the world’s workshop and India a centre of services and manufacturing — lowered costs for consumers worldwide, and spread technology and management practice. But it has also produced new inequalities: workers in the older industrial regions lost jobs, wages and conditions in the new locations are often poor, and the gains have not been evenly shared. It is the phase of globalisation discussed in the Economics chapters.

10.

Give two examples from history to show the impact of technology on food availability.

Model answer

(i) Railways and refrigerated ships in the late nineteenth century made it possible to move meat, butter and grain over long distances, so that cheap food from America and Australia reached European markets and transformed diets. (ii) Better agricultural technology and the expansion of cultivation — new lands brought under the plough, chemical fertilisers and improved seeds — raised output, as in the Green Revolution of the 1960s, which made India self-sufficient in food grains.

11.

What is meant by the Bretton Woods Agreement?

Model answer

The agreement reached at the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference at Bretton Woods in July 1944, which set up the International Monetary Fund and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank) and established a system of fixed exchange rates with currencies pegged to the US dollar, convertible into gold. It was designed to avoid the instability of the inter-war years and lasted until the early 1970s.

12.

Imagine that you are an indentured Indian labourer in the Caribbean. Write a letter to your family describing your life and feelings.

Model answer

Model answer: “Dear Ma, I have reached this island after two months at sea. The ship was crowded and many fell ill. I work on the plantation from sunrise to sunset, cutting cane; the overseer is harsh and the pay is small, and I must repay the cost of my passage before I am free. The house is a hut with a leaking roof and I have fever often. Sometimes I think of our village and the fields, and I wonder whether I shall ever see you again. I have signed for five years. Pray for me.” (Student answers will vary.)

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

Explain the three flows of the nineteenth-century world economy with examples. (5 marks)

Model answer

Trade in goods: the repeal of the Corn Laws let cheap food into Britain, and railways and refrigerated ships carried meat, butter, grain and other products from America, Australia and India to European markets, while manufactured goods moved the other way. Movement of labour: millions migrated — Europeans to America and Australia, and indentured labourers from India and China to the Caribbean, Mauritius, Fiji and South Africa — after slavery was abolished in the British empire in 1833. Movement of capital: British and European capital was invested abroad in railways, plantations, mines and ports, knitting distant regions into a single economy. Together these flows integrated the world economy on unequal terms, with colonies supplying raw materials and food and importing manufactures.

2.

What was indentured labour? Describe its conditions and how it ended. (5 marks)

Model answer

Indentured labour was a system of bonded labour under which a worker signed a contract — a girmit — to work for an employer for a fixed period, often in a distant country, in return for passage and wages; the labourers were called girmitiyas. Recruited mainly from Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Andhra (and also from China and the Pacific), they were sent to Trinidad, Guyana, Surinam, Mauritius, Fiji, South Africa and Malaya to work on plantations and in mines. Conditions were harsh — long hours, low wages, poor housing, disease, and little chance of return — and recruiters often deceived the poor with false promises. The system was abolished in 1921 after pressure from Indian nationalists and public opinion, and after Gandhiji’s campaign in South Africa. The labourers who stayed became the ancestors of large Indian communities in the Caribbean, Mauritius and Fiji.

3.

Explain the causes and consequences of the Great Depression. (5 marks)

Model answer

Causes: agricultural overproduction — farmers produced more as prices fell, pushing prices down further and accumulating stocks; the withdrawal of US loans from Europe when American banks ran into difficulty, which collapsed dependent economies; and bank failures that destroyed savings and credit, closing businesses and throwing millions out of work. Consequences: world trade halved between 1929 and 1932; unemployment reached unprecedented levels; in India exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934, prices collapsed while revenue demands stayed fixed, peasants fell into debt and lost land, jute growers in Bengal and peasants in Tamil Nadu were ruined; and the distress fed into political unrest across the world, encouraging protectionism and, in some countries, authoritarian politics.

4.

How did the pre-modern world become connected? Explain with the examples of the silk routes, food and disease. (5 marks)

Model answer

The silk routes — several overland and sea routes — knitted together vast regions of Asia and linked Asia with Europe and northern Africa, carrying Chinese silk westwards and, along with trade, Christian missionaries, Buddhist monks, art and ideas. Food travelled too: noodles and spaghetti from China to the West, and the potato, soya, groundnut, maize, tomato, chilli and sweet potato to the Indian subcontinent in the sixteenth century. Disease travelled with the conquerors: smallpox, carried by the Spanish, decimated the Americas because the original inhabitants had no immunity, and in places it preceded the Europeans themselves. And silver from Peru and Mexico financed Europe’s trade with Asia, much of it ending in China. So the pre-modern world was already global — but its connections were thinner and slower than those of the nineteenth century.

5.

How did rinderpest change Africa? (3 marks)

Model answer

Rinderpest, a cattle plague, arrived in Africa in the late 1880s and killed about 90 per cent of the cattle. Since cattle were the main form of wealth and livelihood, African communities lost their means of living and were forced into the labour market to work for colonial planters and mine owners. The disease thus helped colonial conquest: African independence was lost along with African livelihoods.

6.

What was the Bretton Woods system? Why did it end? (5 marks)

Model answer

The Bretton Woods conference of July 1944 set up the IMF, to deal with external surpluses and deficits and promote exchange-rate stability, and the World Bank, to finance post-war reconstruction. The system was based on fixed exchange rates, with currencies pegged to the US dollar, which was convertible into gold at a fixed rate. It provided stability for the post-war boom. It ended in the early 1970s when the United States suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold and major currencies began to float — a change caused by the growing imbalances of the American economy and by the refusal of other countries to accept the discipline the system imposed.

7.

How was India integrated into the world economy in the nineteenth century? (5 marks)

Model answer

India exported raw materials and food grains — cotton, indigo, tea, opium, wheat and rice — and imported British manufactures. Indigo was grown under oppressive conditions and shipped to Europe for dyeing; opium was exported to China; tea from Assam became a major export. British capital flowed into railways, plantations, mines and ports, and the railways linked the interior to the ports. But the integration was unequal: Indian handicrafts declined before cheap machine-made imports, the export of food grains contributed to famines even as food was shipped abroad, and India became the largest source of indentured labour, changing the demography of distant colonies.

8.

Explain the role of technology in the making of the global world. (3 marks)

Model answer

Railways carried goods quickly overland to ports; refrigerated ships made it possible to transport perishable food across oceans, so meat, butter and grain from America and Australia reached Europe; the telegraph made possible instant communication between distant markets; and later container shipping and information technology reduced the cost of moving goods and information. Each of these lowered the cost of distance, which is what globalisation means.

9.

What is the G-77 and what did it demand? (3 marks)

Model answer

The Group of 77 is a coalition of developing countries formed in the 1960s. It demanded a New International Economic Order: better prices for their raw materials, more development assistance, fairer terms for technology transfer, and a greater voice in international institutions. It was a collective response to a world economy dominated by the former colonial powers and the Bretton Woods institutions.

Source-Based and Case-Based Questions

Read the passage or table, then answer

Source 1 — The three flows

Source extract

Economists have identified three types of flows within international economic exchange. The first is the flow of trade in goods — chiefly food, raw materials and manufactured goods. The second is the flow of labour — the migration of people in search of employment. The third is the movement of capital for short-term or long-term investments over long distances.

1.

Name the three flows and give one nineteenth-century example of each.

Model answer

Goods: cheap corn and meat to Britain, cotton and indigo from India. Labour: indentured labourers from India to Mauritius and the Caribbean. Capital: British investment in Indian railways, plantations and mines.

2.

Why does the chapter describe the nineteenth century through these flows?

Model answer

Because they capture how the world economy became integrated — not merely through trade, but through the movement of people and money. Each flow created its own social history: migrant workers, plantation economies and colonial investment.

3.

Which flow is hardest to see in ordinary records, and why?

Model answer

The flow of labour: indentured workers left few written records of their own, and their contracts and ships’ lists tell only part of the story. That is why the chapter uses photographs, letters and memoirs to recover their experience.

Source 2 — The Great Depression

Source extract

The Great Depression was caused by a combination of several factors. First, agricultural overproduction: as prices fell, farmers tried to produce even more, which pushed prices further down and led to huge stocks. Second, in the mid-1920s many countries financed their investments through loans from the US; when US banks faced difficulty, they withdrew these loans, and countries that depended on them collapsed. Third, with the collapse of prices and the withdrawal of loans, banks failed, credit dried up, businesses closed and unemployment rose.

1.

Why did farmers produce more when prices were falling?

Model answer

Because each farmer tried to maintain total income by selling a larger quantity — but when everyone did so, supply rose further and prices fell even more, a spiral that ended in unsold stocks.

2.

How did the American loans spread the crisis?

Model answer

Countries had financed their investments with US loans; when American banks called them in, those economies lost their funding and collapsed, so a crisis that began in the United States became worldwide.

3.

What made the Depression worse for ordinary people?

Model answer

Bank failures destroyed savings, credit dried up so businesses could not borrow, and unemployment rose — while governments cut spending and raised tariffs, which shrank trade further.

Source 3 — Indentured labour

Source extract

The indentured labour system was a system of bonded labour under which a worker signed a contract to work for an employer for a specific period of time, in return for passage and wages. The recruiters often used false information to lure the poor into these contracts. The conditions on the plantations were harsh — long hours, low wages, poor housing and little chance of return. The system was abolished in 1921.

1.

Why is indentured labour described as “a new system of slavery”?

Model answer

Because although it was formally a contract, the worker was bound for years, could not leave, was often deceived about conditions, and worked under discipline in a distant land with little chance of return — the substance of slavery under a legal form.

2.

Who gained from the system?

Model answer

Plantation owners and colonial employers, who obtained a supply of cheap, disciplined labour after slavery was abolished in 1833; shipping companies and recruiters also profited.

3.

What ended it?

Model answer

Pressure from Indian nationalists and public opinion, campaigns against the system (including Gandhiji’s in South Africa), and the report of the Chambers of Commerce — the system was abolished in 1921.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

“Globalisation has always created winners and losers.” Discuss with two examples from the chapter.

Model answer

Yes. The abolition of the Corn Laws ruined British farmers but enriched industrialists and opened world food markets; rinderpest destroyed African cattle-keeping livelihoods but supplied labour to colonial planters and mine owners. In the nineteenth century, colonies supplied raw materials and received manufactures, while their handicrafts declined. In the 1990s phase, Asian workers gained jobs while older industrial regions lost them. Each phase of integration redistributed gains, and the chapter’s method is to ask who gained and who paid. (Model answer.)

2.

Why did the British Empire need indentured labour after 1833?

Model answer

Because the abolition of slavery in the British empire in 1833 removed the legal basis of the plantation workforce, while sugar, tea and other plantation crops still needed large numbers of cheap workers. Indentured labour supplied them under a contract that was legal but bound the worker for years, and the colonial state provided the legal machinery and the shipping. It was slavery replaced by contract, and it ended in 1921 only when nationalist pressure made it politically costly. (Model answer.)

3.

Was the Great Depression caused by the failure of markets or of governments?

Model answer

Both. Markets failed in the narrow sense: overproduction in agriculture, speculative lending and the collapse of credit produced a spiral. But governments made it worse — they cut spending, raised tariffs and clung to the gold standard, which transmitted deflation across the world. The Bretton Woods settlement of 1944 was designed precisely to prevent a repetition, by creating institutions to manage payments and reconstruction. The lesson the chapter draws is that an integrated economy needs institutions, not only markets. (Model answer.)

4.

Why is the story of food a good way to understand globalisation?

Model answer

Because food shows both the benefits and the violence of interconnection. The potato, maize, chilli and tomato transformed diets in Asia; but the potato’s failure caused famine in Ireland, the export of Indian food grains accompanied famines in India, and rinderpest destroyed African herds. Food travels with trade, empire and technology, and who eats and who starves depends on power — which is the chapter’s central point about the global economy. (Model answer.)

5.

How did the two World Wars reshape the global economy?

Model answer

The First World War destroyed the pre-war order: it killed and disabled millions of working-age men, disrupted trade, created debts and inflated currencies, and left resentments that fed the next war. The Second World War was more destructive still, and it shifted power to the United States, which emerged as the world’s creditor. The post-war settlement — Bretton Woods, fixed exchange rates and the IMF and World Bank — was an attempt to build a stable order under American leadership, and decolonisation then brought dozens of new states into that order demanding a fairer share. (Model answer.)

6.

“The G-77 was right to say the Bretton Woods system was designed by the rich for the rich.” Evaluate.

Model answer

Largely, but not entirely. The IMF and World Bank were created by the victorious powers, voting rights reflected financial contributions, and their early policies favoured stabilisation and reconstruction in Europe rather than development in the South. Developing countries found the terms of trade against them and demanded the NIEO. But the institutions also provided finance, technical help and a framework of stability that benefited poorer countries, and the G-77’s own record of unity was limited. A fair judgement is that the system was skewed by its origins but not closed to reform — which is why the demand was for a new order within it rather than its abolition. (Model answer.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

Globalisation is not new. The pre-modern world was already connected by the silk routes, along which Chinese silk travelled west and Buddhism and Christianity travelled with the traders; by food, since the potato, maize, tomato, chilli and sweet potato reached India only after Columbus; and by disease, since smallpox carried by the Spanish devastated the Americas, whose inhabitants had no immunity. Silver from Peru and Mexico financed Europe’s trade with Asia, much of it ending in China, while the Indian Ocean carried cowries from the Maldives, Indian textiles and spices.

The nineteenth century — 1815 to 1914 — expanded these connections into three great flows of goods, labour and capital. The repeal of the Corn Laws let cheap food into Britain, and railways and refrigerated ships brought meat and grain from America and Australia, ruining British farmers but feeding industrial workers. Rinderpest killed about 90 per cent of African cattle in the late 1880s, destroying livelihoods and forcing Africans into the labour market for colonial planters. After slavery was abolished in 1833, indentured labour from India — the girmitiyas — was shipped to the Caribbean, Mauritius, Fiji and South Africa under harsh contracts until the system was abolished in 1921. India exported raw materials and food grains, imported manufactures and received British capital in railways and plantations, on unequal terms.

The First World War destroyed the pre-war order, and in 1929 the world economy collapsed. The Great Depression was caused by agricultural overproduction, the withdrawal of US loans and bank failures; world trade halved between 1929 and 1932, and in India exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934 while peasants — jute growers in Bengal, peasants in Tamil Nadu — were ruined.

The post-war world was rebuilt at Bretton Woods in July 1944, with the IMF, the World Bank and fixed exchange rates. As colonies became independent, the developing countries formed the G-77 and demanded a New International Economic Order, arguing that the system served the rich. The Bretton Woods system ended in the early 1970s, and from the 1990s a new globalisation saw MNCs relocate production to Asia — creating growth and new inequalities in equal measure.
