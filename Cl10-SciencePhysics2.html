Electric Current, Circuit & Potential

The Stuff (Electric Charge)

Just as flowing water constitutes a water current, flowing electric charge constitutes an electric current. In metallic wires, the charge carriers are electrons. Charge is a fundamental property of matter — it comes in two kinds, positive (+) and negative (–), and like charges repel while unlike charges attract.

Q = I × t

(also, total charge Q = n × e for n electrons)

  • SI Unit: Coulomb (C) — charge flowing in 1 second when I = 1 A
  • Charge of one electron: e = –1.6 × 10–19 C
  • 1 C = charge of ≈ 6 × 1018 electrons

The Flow (Current)

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor. A continuous and closed path of electric current is called an electric circuit — if the circuit breaks anywhere, current stops flowing.

Convention check: in metallic wires, electrons carry the charge, but the direction of electric current is taken opposite to the flow of electrons (i.e., along the direction positive charges would move).

I = Q / t
  • SI Unit: Ampere (A);  1 A = 1 C / 1 s
  • Small currents: 1 mA = 10–3 A, 1 µA = 10–6 A
  • 1 coulomb ≈ charge of 6 × 1018 electrons (electron charge = 1.6 × 10–19 C)
  • Measured by: Ammeter (always connected in series)

The Push (Potential)

Analogy: water flows in a tube only when there is a pressure difference between its two ends. Similarly, charges flow in a wire only when there is an electric pressure difference — the potential difference — produced by a cell or battery (chemical action inside the cell generates it).

Electric potential difference between two points is the work done to move a unit charge from one point to the other.

V = W / Q
  • SI Unit: Volt (V);  1 V = 1 J / 1 C
  • Measured by: Voltmeter (always connected in parallel)

  • Ammeter: connected in series — the whole circuit current passes through it.
  • Voltmeter: connected in parallel across the component whose PD is to be measured.

Ohm's Law Simulator

V ∝ I

The potential difference, V, across the ends of a given metallic wire in an electric circuit is directly proportional to the current flowing through it, provided its temperature remains the same. Found by Georg Simon Ohm (1827).

V = I × R

R is a constant called Resistance.

R = V / I
1 Ω = 1 V / 1 A
I = V / R
double R → half the current

High Yield: The V-I graph for Ohmic conductors is always a straight line passing through the origin; its slope (V/I) equals R. A rheostat is a variable resistance used to change current without changing the voltage source.

What decides R?

Resistance arises because moving electrons are restrained by the attraction of atoms among which they move. Precise measurements show:

  • R ∝ l — longer wire, more resistance (ammeter reading halves when length doubles).
  • R ∝ 1/A — thicker wire, less resistance (current increases with a thicker wire).
  • Material: a copper wire of the same size conducts better than nichrome — captured by ρ.
R = ρ l / A   ⇒   ρ = R A / l

ρ (rho) = electrical resistivity, SI unit Ω m. It is a characteristic property of the material, and varies with temperature.

Why alloys? (High-yield reasoning)

  • Resistivity of an alloy is generally higher than that of its constituent metals.
  • Alloys do not oxidise (burn) readily at high temperature → used in electric irons, toasters, heating devices (nichrome).
  • Tungsten (MP 3380 °C) is used almost exclusively for bulb filaments.
  • Copper & aluminium (very low resistivity) are used for electrical transmission lines.

Derivation: Series (5-mark favourite)

5 marks

Show that Rs = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ for three resistors in series.

  1. Same current I flows through R₁, R₂, R₃ (Activity 11.4).
  2. PDs across each: V₁ = IR₁, V₂ = IR₂, V₃ = IR₃
  3. Total PD: V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃  (Activity 11.5)
  4. ⇒ IR = IR₁ + IR₂ + IR₃
  5. Dividing by I: R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃

Rs is greater than any individual resistance.

Derivation: Parallel (5-mark favourite)

5 marks

Show that 1/Rp = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ for three resistors in parallel.

  1. Same PD V across each resistor (Activity 11.6).
  2. Branch currents: I₁ = V/R₁, I₂ = V/R₂, I₃ = V/R₃
  3. Total current: I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃
  4. ⇒ V/Rp = V/R₁ + V/R₂ + V/R₃
  5. Dividing by V: 1/Rp = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃

Rp is smaller than the smallest individual resistance.

Heating Effect & Electric Power

Joule's Law of Heating

In a purely resistive circuit the source energy is dissipated entirely as heat. For a steady current I through resistance R for time t:

H = I2Rt

(also H = VIt, since V = IR)

  • H ∝ I² (for a given R) — double the current, 4× the heat!
  • H ∝ R (for a given I)
  • H ∝ t (time of flow)
Electric power — the rate of dissipation of energy: P = VI = I²R = V²/R. SI unit watt (1 W = 1 V × 1 A); practical unit kilowatt (1 kW = 1000 W).

The Electric Bulb

Filament must retain heat to glow — so it uses tungsten (melting point 3380 °C, does not oxidise). Filament is thermally isolated, and bulbs are filled with nitrogen & argon to prolong filament life. Most power appears as heat; a small part as light.

The Electric Fuse

Placed in series with the device. If a current larger than the rated value (1, 2, 3, 5, 10 A...) flows, the fuse wire heats up and melts, breaking the circuit — protecting appliances. An electric iron (1 kW at 220 V) draws 4.54 A, so a 5 A fuse is used.

Heating Appliances

Electric laundry iron, toaster, oven, kettle, heater — all based on Joule's heating, using high-resistivity alloys (nichrome) that don't oxidise at high temperature.

More to Know (NCERT box)

Many people think electrons are consumed in an electric circuit. This is wrong! We pay the electricity board to provide energy to move electrons through our gadgets — we pay for the energy used, not for electrons.

1 kWh (1 unit) = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 106 J

Quick Revision — Formula Sheet

What you have learnt (NCERT summary)

  • A stream of electrons moving through a conductor constitutes an electric current; conventionally, current direction is opposite to electron flow. SI unit: ampere.
  • A cell/battery generates the potential difference (volts) that sets electrons in motion. Voltmeter in parallel, ammeter in series.
  • Ohm's law: V ∝ I at constant temperature; V = IR. R (ohm) is resistance — it controls current magnitude.
  • R depends directly on length l, inversely on area A, and on the material: R = ρl/A; ρ (resistivity, Ω m) is a material property.
  • Series: Rs = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …  |  Parallel: 1/Rp = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + …
  • Energy dissipated: W = VIt; Joule's law of heating H = I²Rt; power P = VI = I²R = V²/R (watt).
  • Commercial unit of energy: kilowatt hour (kWh); 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
