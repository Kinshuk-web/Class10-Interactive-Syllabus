Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

As we wind up our tour of democracy, it is time to ask a general set of questions: What does democracy do? What outcomes can we reasonably expect of it? Does it fulfil these expectations in real life?

The chapter first asks how to assess democracy’s outcomes. Democracy is seen to be good in principle but not so good in practice — a dilemma that invites careful thinking. The first step is to recognise that democracy is just a form of government: it can only create conditions for achieving something; the citizens have to take advantage of those conditions.

It then examines the record in five areas. On accountable, responsive and legitimate government, democracy is slower but its decisions are more acceptable; it provides transparency and mechanisms of accountability, though it falls short on fair elections, public debate, information-sharing, responsiveness and corruption — but it is undoubtedly more legitimate. On economic growth, dictatorships grew slightly faster between 1950 and 2000, yet democracy is preferred because growth depends on many factors. On inequality and poverty, democracies have not been very successful. On accommodation of social diversity, democracy is best suited to negotiate differences, provided it is not merely majority rule and no one is barred from being in a majority by birth. On dignity and freedom, democracy stands superior to every other form of government.

Accountability

The government answers to citizens for its decisions, through elections, debate and the right to information.

Transparency

A citizen’s right and means to examine the process of decision-making, to know whether decisions followed the correct procedures.

Legitimacy

The quality of being the people’s own government — the one respect in which democracy is certainly better than its alternatives.

Prudential reason

A reason based on outcomes and consequences — democracy is supported because it delivers something.

Rule by majority

Not rule by the majority community; in a democracy different persons and groups may form a majority in different decisions and elections.

Dignity

The respect an individual receives from fellow beings; the passion for respect and freedom is the basis of democracy.

Efficacy of vote

The belief that one’s vote makes a difference to how the government is run and to one’s own self-interest.

How Do We Assess Democracy’s Outcomes?

In Class IX we argued that democracy is a better form of government than dictatorship or any other alternative, because it promotes equality among citizens, enhances the dignity of the individual, improves the quality of decision-making, provides a method to resolve conflicts, and allows room to correct mistakes.

Are these expectations realised? Most people support democracy against alternatives such as rule by a monarch, military or religious leaders — but not so many are satisfied with the democracy in practice. So we face a dilemma: democracy is seen to be good in principle, but felt to be not so good in its practice. This invites us to think hard about the outcomes of democracy — do we prefer it only for moral reasons, or are there prudential reasons too?

Over a hundred countries today claim and practise some kind of democratic politics: they have formal constitutions, hold elections, have parties and guarantee rights. But they differ greatly in their social situations, economic achievements and cultures, so what may be achieved under each will differ. Is there something we can expect from every democracy, just because it is a democracy?

Our fascination for democracy often pushes us into expecting that it can address all socio-economic and political problems; when expectations are not met we start blaming the idea of democracy, or doubting whether we live in one. The first step towards thinking carefully about outcomes is to recognise that democracy is just a form of government. It can only create conditions for achieving something. The citizens have to take advantage of those conditions and achieve those goals.

Accountable, Responsive and Legitimate Government

There are some things democracy must provide. In a democracy we are most concerned with ensuring that people have the right to choose their rulers and have control over them, and that citizens can participate in decision-making whenever possible and necessary. Therefore the most basic outcome of democracy should be that it produces a government that is accountable to the citizens, and responsive to their needs and expectations.

Is democracy efficient?

Some people think democracy produces less effective government. It is true that non-democratic rulers do not have to bother about deliberation in assemblies or worry about majorities and public opinion, so they can be very quick and efficient in decision-making and implementation. Democracy is based on the idea of deliberation and negotiation, so some delay is bound to take place. But think in terms of costs: a government that decides very fast may take decisions that are not accepted by the people and may therefore face problems, while a democratic government takes more time to follow procedures — and because it has followed procedures, its decisions may be both more acceptable to the people and more effective. The cost of time that democracy pays is perhaps worth it.

Democracy also ensures that decision-making is based on norms and procedures. A citizen who wants to know whether a decision was taken through the correct procedures can find out — she has the right and the means to examine the process of decision-making. This is known as transparency, and it is often missing from non-democratic government. So we can expect democracy to produce a government that follows procedures and is accountable, and to develop mechanisms for citizens to hold the government accountable and to take part in decision-making.

If you wanted to measure democracies on this outcome, you would look for: regular, free and fair elections; open public debate on major policies and legislations; and citizens’ right to information about the government and its functioning.

The actual record

The performance of democracies shows a mixed record. They have had greater success in setting up regular and free elections and in creating conditions for open public debate, but most democracies fall short of elections that provide a fair chance to everyone and of subjecting every decision to public debate. Democratic governments do not have a very good record in sharing information with citizens. All one can say in their favour is that they are much better than any non-democratic regime in these respects.

In substantive terms we may expect a government attentive to the needs and demands of the people and largely free of corruption. The record is not impressive on either count: democracies often frustrate the needs of the people and ignore the demands of a majority, and routine tales of corruption are enough to show democracy is not free of that evil. At the same time, there is nothing to show that non-democracies are less corrupt or more sensitive to the people.

There is one respect in which democratic government is certainly better: democratic government is legitimate government. It may be slow, less efficient, not always very responsive or clean — but it is people’s own government. That is why there is overwhelming support for the idea of democracy all over the world, in countries with and without democratic regimes. Democracy’s ability to generate its own support is itself an outcome that cannot be ignored.

Economic Growth and Development

If democracies are expected to produce good governments, is it not fair to expect that they would also produce development? Evidence shows that in practice many democracies did not fulfil this expectation.

If you consider all democracies and all dictatorships for the fifty years between 1950 and 2000, dictatorships have slightly higher rates of economic growth. The inability of democracy to achieve higher economic development worries us — but this alone cannot be the reason to reject democracy.

Why the difference? Because in a democracy, economic development depends on several factors: the size of the country’s population, its global situation, the priorities and cooperation of other countries, and the economic priorities of the government. Democracies are based on political equality — all citizens have equal rights, equal weight in elections and equal opportunity — but political equality does not automatically translate into economic equality. The poor may have the same vote as the rich, but that does not give them the same income, and the government may not pursue policies that reduce economic inequality as fast as it pursues other goals.

The chapter also reminds us that dictatorships have a slightly better record of growth, but they are not able to distribute the benefits of growth equally — which is why growth alone is not a sufficient test of a government.

Inequality and Poverty

The second important outcome is economic equality and the reduction of poverty. In actual life, democracies do not appear to be very successful in reducing economic inequalities. In India a large section of people suffer from poverty, and the poor constitute a large proportion of voters — no party would like to lose their votes — yet democratically elected governments do not appear to be as keen to address poverty as one would expect. Their incomes have been declining, and sometimes they find it difficult to meet the basic needs of life: food, clothing, house, education and health.

The situation is much worse in some other countries. In Bangladesh more than half the population lives in poverty, and people in several poor countries depend on rich countries even for food supplies.

Teacher explanation

Voice of the Poor: “Democracy is a rule of the majority. The poor are in majority. So democracy must be a rule of the poor. How can this not be the case?” — Answer: because majority rule is not the same as rule by the poor. A majority in a democracy is formed afresh on each issue and election, and money, information and organisation weigh heavily in politics. The poor are numerous but not organised or informed enough to act as a majority on economic policy, which is why democracy does not automatically produce redistribution.

Accommodation of Social Diversity

Do democracies lead to peaceful and harmonious life among citizens? It is a fair expectation that democracy should produce a harmonious social life. We have seen how democracies accommodate social divisions — Belgium successfully negotiated differences among ethnic populations. Democracies usually develop a procedure to conduct their competition, which reduces the possibility of tensions becoming explosive or violent.

No society can fully and permanently resolve conflicts among different groups, but we can learn to respect differences and evolve mechanisms to negotiate them. Democracy is best suited to produce this outcome. Non-democratic regimes often turn a blind eye to or suppress internal social differences. The ability to handle social differences, divisions and conflicts is thus a definite plus point of democratic regimes.

But the example of Sri Lanka reminds us that a democracy must fulfil two conditions in order to achieve this outcome:

  1. It is necessary to understand that democracy is not simply rule by majority opinion. The majority always needs to work with the minority, so that governments function to represent the general view. Majority and minority opinions are not permanent.
  2. Rule by majority must not become rule by majority community in terms of religion, race or linguistic group. Rule by majority means that in every decision and every election, different persons and groups may and can form a majority. Democracy remains democracy only as long as every citizen has a chance of being in majority at some point of time. If someone is barred from being in majority on the basis of birth, then democratic rule ceases to be accommodative for that person or group.

Dignity and Freedom of the Citizens

Democracy stands much superior to any other form of government in promoting dignity and freedom of the individual. Every individual wants to receive respect from fellow beings, and conflicts often arise because some feel they are not treated with due respect. The passion for respect and freedom are the basis of democracy, recognised throughout the world at least in principle, and achieved in various degrees. For societies long built on subordination and domination, it is not a simple matter to recognise that all individuals are equal.

The dignity of women

Most societies across the world were historically male-dominated. Long struggles by women have created some sensitivity today that respect for and equal treatment of women are necessary ingredients of a democratic society. That does not mean women are always actually treated with respect — but once the principle is recognised, it becomes easier for women to wage a struggle against what is now unacceptable legally and morally. In a non-democratic set-up, this unacceptability would not have a legal basis, because the principle of individual freedom and dignity would not have legal and moral force there.

Caste inequalities

The same is true of caste inequalities. Democracy in India has strengthened the claims of the disadvantaged and discriminated castes for equal status and equal opportunity. There are still instances of caste-based inequalities and atrocities, but these lack the moral and legal foundations they once had. Perhaps it is this recognition that makes ordinary citizens value their democratic rights.

The examination never gets over

Expectations from democracy also function as the criteria for judging any democratic country. What is most distinctive about democracy is that its examination never gets over: as democracy passes one test, it produces another test. As people get some benefits of democracy they ask for more and want to make democracy even better. That is why, when we ask people about the way democracy functions, they always come up with more expectations — and many complaints.

The fact that people are complaining is itself a testimony to the success of democracy: it shows that people have developed awareness and the ability to expect, and to look critically at power holders and the high and the mighty. A public expression of dissatisfaction with democracy shows the success of the democratic project: it transforms people from the status of a subject into that of a citizen. Most individuals today believe that their vote makes a difference to the way the government is run and to their own self-interest.

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

Did we reach these conclusions in Madam Lyngdoh’s class? (That democracy promotes equality, dignity, better decisions, conflict resolution and correction of mistakes.)

Model answer

Yes — these five were the conclusions of that Class IX discussion. The point of returning to them is to ask whether they are actually realised, and the chapter shows a mixed record: equality and dignity are recognised in principle and defended by law, decisions are better because they follow procedures, conflicts are negotiated, and mistakes can be corrected through elections — but inequality persists and practice falls short of principle.

2.

Is democracy all about coping with multiple pressures and accommodating diverse demands?

Model answer

Partly. Because democracy rests on deliberation and negotiation, it must accommodate diverse demands — which is why it is slower than a dictatorship. But accommodation is not its only business: it must also produce accountable and responsive government, reduce inequality, and protect dignity and freedom. Coping with pressures is the method, not the whole purpose.

3.

What and how does the government know about you and your family? What are the sources of information for you about the government?

Model answer

The government holds records through ration cards, voter identity cards, the census, land records, tax returns, school and hospital records, Aadhaar and welfare scheme registers. Citizens learn about government through newspapers, television, the internet, RTI applications, official gazettes, public meetings and elected representatives. The cartoon on governmental secrecy makes the point that the state knows far more about the citizen than the citizen knows about the state.

4.

“So the best outcome of democracy is that it is a democracy! That is what we have discovered after all this mental gymnastics?”

Model answer

In part, yes — and that is not a trivial conclusion. Democracy’s distinctive outcome is that it makes government legitimate, accountable and correctable, and it makes citizens into citizens rather than subjects. But the chapter does not stop there: it also measures democracy on growth, inequality, social harmony and dignity, and finds a mixed record that gives citizens reasons to keep demanding more.

5.

“Democracy is a rule of the majority. The poor are in majority. So democracy must be a rule of the poor. How can this not be the case?”

Model answer

Because a majority in a democracy is not a fixed social bloc but is formed afresh on each issue and election. The poor are numerous but scattered, less organised, less informed and less able to finance politics, while the rich have resources and access. So political equality of votes does not become economic equality of outcomes — which is exactly why democracies have not been very successful in reducing inequality.

6.

“All you are saying is that democracy ensures that people do not break each other’s head. This is not harmony. Should we be happy about it?”

Model answer

It is more than that, though less than perfect harmony. Democracy develops procedures to conduct competition, which reduces the chance of tensions becoming violent, and it allows differences to be negotiated rather than suppressed — as non-democratic regimes do. No society permanently resolves its conflicts; learning to respect differences and negotiate them is the realistic outcome, and the chapter calls the ability to handle divisions a definite plus point of democracy.

7.

Take one example for each image (“Enemies” and “Greeting”) and write a paragraph on the outcome of democratic politics in both situations.

Model answer

Enemies: a constituency where parties mobilise on religious lines and treat each other as enemies — turnout may rise but trust falls and violence becomes possible, as in communal riots. Greeting: a coalition or a negotiation between communities, as in Belgium or in a village where caste groups bargain over a panchayat budget — differences remain but are handled by talk. The first shows democracy failing to accommodate diversity; the second shows it succeeding. (Student answers will vary.)

8.

Underline the sentences from the section on dignity and freedom that connect to the cartoon or graph. (Rosa Parks cartoon; belief in the efficacy of vote.)

Model answer

Relevant sentences: “Democracy stands much superior to any other form of government in promoting dignity and freedom of the individual”; “once the principle is recognised, it becomes easier for women to wage a struggle against what is now unacceptable legally and morally”; and “Most individuals today believe that their vote makes a difference to the way the government is run and to their own self-interest.” The Rosa Parks cartoon connects to the first two — one person’s refusal becoming a legal and moral turning point — and the graph to the last.

9.

How does democracy produce an accountable, responsive and legitimate government?

Model answer

Through regular, free and fair elections that let citizens choose and remove rulers; through deliberation and negotiation, which make decisions more acceptable and effective even at the cost of speed; through transparency, since decisions must follow known norms and procedures that citizens can examine; through mechanisms such as the right to information and public debate; and through legitimacy — a democratic government, however slow or imperfect, is the people’s own government, which is why support for democracy is overwhelming even where practice disappoints.

NCERT Exercises

Reproduced from the exercise section of the chapter, with model answers. Word limits are the ones the textbook sets.

1.
NCERT exercise5 marks

How does democracy produce an accountable, responsive and legitimate government?

Model answer

Democracy gives citizens the right to choose rulers and control them, and to participate in decisions that affect them. Elections make governments answerable; deliberation and negotiation, though slow, make decisions more acceptable and effective; decisions must follow norms and procedures, so citizens can examine them — transparency; and mechanisms such as public debate and the right to information let citizens hold government to account. Above all, a democratic government is legitimate — the people’s own government — which is why democracy generates overwhelming support even when its performance disappoints.

2.
NCERT exercise5 marks

What are the conditions under which democracies accommodate social diversities?

Model answer

Two conditions. First, democracy must not be understood as simply rule by majority opinion: the majority has to work with the minority so that government represents the general view, and majority and minority opinions are not permanent. Second, rule by majority must not become rule by majority community in terms of religion, race or linguistic group: in every decision and election different persons and groups may form a majority, and democracy remains democracy only as long as every citizen has a chance of being in the majority at some point. If someone is barred from being in the majority on the basis of birth, democratic rule ceases to be accommodative for that person or group.

3.
NCERT exercise5 marks

Give arguments to support or oppose: “Industrialised countries can afford democracy but the poor need dictatorship to become rich.”

Model answer

Oppose. The evidence is that dictatorships had only a slightly higher growth rate between 1950 and 2000, and growth depends on population, the global situation, cooperation from other countries and government priorities — not on the form of government. Meanwhile dictatorships cannot distribute the benefits of growth, do not allow correction of mistakes, and deny dignity and freedom. Democracy’s advantage is that it is legitimate and accountable, and that citizens can change course when a policy fails. (Arguments on the other side may cite the speed of decision-making in non-democracies, but speed is not the same as development.)

4.
NCERT exercise5 marks

Give arguments to support or oppose: “Democracy can’t reduce inequality of incomes between different citizens.”

Model answer

Largely support, with qualification. In practice democracies have not been very successful in reducing economic inequality: in India a large section remains poor despite being a large share of the electorate, and in Bangladesh more than half the population lives in poverty. Political equality does not translate automatically into economic equality. But the statement is too absolute — democracies have created welfare schemes, land reform, reservations and rights-based entitlements, and they allow the poor to organise and demand redistribution, which non-democracies do not.

5.
NCERT exercise5 marks

Give arguments to support or oppose: “Government in poor countries should spend less on poverty reduction, health, education and spend more on industries and infrastructure.”

Model answer

Oppose. Health and education are investments in human capital, not consumption — they raise productivity and are the means by which the poor can use opportunities. Infrastructure and industry matter, but growth without health and education produces growth with inequality, which is precisely democracy’s weakness that the chapter identifies. Public spending on health and education also protects dignity and freedom, which the chapter rates as democracy’s greatest achievement.

6.
NCERT exercise3 marks

Give arguments to support or oppose: “In democracy all citizens have one vote, which means that there is absence of any domination and conflict.”

Model answer

Oppose. One person, one vote establishes political equality, but it does not end domination: economic inequality, caste and gender hierarchy persist, and conflict continues — the chapter says no society can permanently resolve conflicts among groups. What democracy does is provide procedures to negotiate conflict and a legal and moral basis for challenging domination, which is different from its absence.

7.
NCERT exercise5 marks

Identify the challenges to democracy and suggest mechanisms to deepen democracy: (a) following a High Court directive, a temple in Odisha that had separate entry doors for Dalits and non-Dalits allowed entry for all from the same door; (b) a large number of farmers are committing suicide in different States; (c) following an allegation of the killing of three civilians in a fake encounter by Jammu and Kashmir police, an enquiry has been ordered.

Model answer

(a) Challenge: caste-based discrimination and denial of dignity. Mechanism: enforce the prohibition of untouchability and caste discrimination, and support the principle with public awareness and legal remedy — the court order is itself an example of the legal and moral force democracy gives to dignity. (b) Challenge: economic inequality and the failure to address the needs of the poor. Mechanism: debt relief and cheap institutional credit, irrigation and crop insurance, minimum support prices, and effective implementation with local participation. (c) Challenge: abuse of power by state agencies and the need for accountability. Mechanism: independent inquiry, transparency, human rights commissions and a functioning right to information.

8.
NCERT exercise2 marks

In the context of democracies, which idea is correct — democracies have successfully eliminated: A. conflicts among people B. economic inequalities among people C. differences of opinion about how marginalised sections are to be treated D. the idea of political inequality.

Model answer

C. Democracies have not eliminated conflict, economic inequality or political inequality; differences of opinion about how marginalised sections should be treated persist — and that is proper in a democracy, which resolves such differences through debate rather than suppression.

9.
NCERT exercise2 marks

In the context of assessing democracy, which among the following is the odd one out? Democracies need to ensure: A. free and fair elections B. dignity of the individual C. majority rule D. equal treatment before law.

Model answer

C. Majority rule is the odd one out. The chapter insists that democracy is not simply rule by majority opinion — the majority must work with the minority. Free and fair elections, dignity and equal treatment before law are genuine democratic requirements.

10.
NCERT exercise2 marks

Studies on political and social inequalities in democracy show that: A. democracy and development go together B. inequalities exist in democracies C. inequalities do not exist under dictatorship D. dictatorship is better than democracy.

Model answer

B. Inequalities exist in democracies. Democracies have not been very successful in reducing economic inequality, and caste and gender inequalities persist — which is why the chapter measures democracy by outcomes rather than by its form alone.

11.
NCERT exercise3 marks

Read the passage about Nannu, who lost his ration card and used the RTI Act. What does Nannu’s example show? What impact did Nannu’s action have on officials?

Model answer

It shows the value of the right to information as a mechanism of accountability: an ordinary daily-wage earner, ignored for three months by clerks, obtained action within a week of filing an RTI application, was visited by an inspector, and was then treated warmly by the Food and Supply Officer, who asked him to withdraw the application. It also shows how transparency changes the behaviour of officials — the knowledge that their inaction is being recorded and questioned made them act. It is democracy transforming a subject into a citizen. (Ask your parents about their own experiences — student activity.)

Important Questions and Answers

Board-style practice

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

How do we assess the outcomes of democracy? (5 marks)

Model answer

First, we must recognise that democracy is just a form of government: it can only create conditions, and citizens must take advantage of them. Second, expectations must be reasonable — we cannot expect democracy to solve every problem, and when it does not we should not blame the idea of democracy. Third, we judge democracy against what it can reasonably be expected to deliver: an accountable, responsive and legitimate government; economic growth and development; reduction of inequality and poverty; accommodation of social diversity; and the dignity and freedom of the individual. Finally, the record must be compared not with an ideal but with the alternatives — and on each count the chapter asks whether democracy is better than dictatorship.

2.

“Democratic government is legitimate government.” Explain. (3 marks)

Model answer

Because it is the people’s own government. It may be slow, less efficient, not always responsive or clean — democracies do fall short on fair chances to all, on sharing information and on corruption — but it is chosen by the people and answerable to them, and it can be removed by them. That is why support for democracy is overwhelming across South Asia, even where satisfaction with its practice is low: 88 per cent find democracy suitable for their country and 94 per cent want leaders elected by the people.

3.

Explain the relationship between democracy and economic growth. (5 marks)

Model answer

If democracy is expected to produce good government, it is fair to expect development too — but the evidence shows many democracies did not fulfil this expectation: comparing all democracies and dictatorships for the fifty years between 1950 and 2000, dictatorships had slightly higher rates of economic growth. Yet this cannot be a reason to reject democracy, because economic development depends on several factors — the size of the population, the global situation, the priorities and cooperation of other countries and the government’s economic priorities — not on the form of government alone. Moreover, democracies are based on political equality, and political equality does not automatically become economic equality. Dictatorships may grow slightly faster, but they cannot distribute the benefits of growth equally, nor correct their mistakes.

4.

“Democracies have not been very successful in reducing economic inequalities.” Discuss. (5 marks)

Model answer

In actual life democracies do not appear very successful in reducing economic inequalities. In India a large section of people suffer from poverty; the poor are a large proportion of voters and no party wants to lose their votes, yet governments do not seem as keen to address poverty as one would expect. Incomes of the poor have been declining and many find it difficult to meet basic needs of food, clothing, housing, education and health. The situation is worse elsewhere — in Bangladesh more than half the population lives in poverty, and people in several poor countries depend on rich countries even for food. The reason is that political equality does not translate into economic equality: the poor are numerous but unorganised and less able to influence policy, while money and access weigh heavily in politics.

5.

What two conditions must a democracy fulfil to accommodate social diversity? (5 marks)

Model answer

First, democracy must not be understood as simply rule by majority opinion: the majority must work with the minority so that government represents the general view, and majority and minority opinions are not permanent. Second, rule by majority must not become rule by majority community in terms of religion, race or linguistic group. Rule by majority means that in every decision and election different persons and groups may form a majority; democracy remains democracy only as long as every citizen has a chance of being in the majority at some point. If someone is barred from being in the majority on the basis of birth, democratic rule ceases to be accommodative for that person or group — which is precisely what happened to Sri Lankan Tamils.

6.

How does democracy promote the dignity and freedom of the individual? (5 marks)

Model answer

Democracy stands much superior to any other form of government in this respect. Every individual wants respect, and the passion for respect and freedom is the basis of democracy. On women: most societies were historically male-dominated, but long struggles by women have created sensitivity that respect for and equal treatment of women are necessary ingredients of a democratic society; once the principle is recognised it becomes easier to struggle against what is now unacceptable legally and morally, which would not have a legal basis in a non-democratic set-up. On caste: democracy in India has strengthened the claims of disadvantaged and discriminated castes for equal status and opportunity; instances of inequality and atrocity continue but they now lack the moral and legal foundations they once had. It is this recognition that makes ordinary citizens value their democratic rights.

7.

“The examination of democracy never gets over.” Explain. (3 marks)

Model answer

Because expectations from democracy function as the criteria for judging it, and as people gain one benefit they ask for more. As democracy passes one test it produces another. That is why people always come up with more expectations and many complaints — and the fact that they complain is itself a testimony to democracy’s success, since it shows awareness and the ability to look critically at power holders. Public dissatisfaction transforms people from subjects into citizens.

8.

Why is transparency important in a democracy? (3 marks)

Model answer

Because democracy ensures that decision-making is based on norms and procedures, and a citizen who wants to know whether a decision was taken correctly can find out — she has the right and the means to examine the process. This transparency is often missing in non-democratic government. It is why the measures of democratic accountability include the right to information, public debate and free and fair elections, and why Nannu’s RTI application produced action that three months of visits could not.

9.

What does the South Asia survey tell us about support for democracy? (3 marks)

Model answer

That support for the idea is overwhelming even where satisfaction with practice is low. Democracy is preferred over dictatorship everywhere except Pakistan (37 per cent), and in India 69 per cent prefer democracy, 92 per cent find it suitable for the country and 95 per cent want leaders elected by the people. Very few doubt democracy’s suitability — which is why the chapter calls democracy’s ability to generate its own support an outcome in itself.

10.

Is democracy merely a slower form of government? Give the argument about efficiency. (3 marks)

Model answer

Democracy is slower, because it rests on deliberation and negotiation, while non-democratic rulers need not worry about assemblies, majorities or public opinion. But speed is not efficiency: a fast decision that people do not accept may fail in implementation, whereas a democratic decision that follows procedures is more acceptable and therefore more effective. The cost of time that democracy pays is perhaps worth it — and democracy adds transparency, which non-democratic government lacks.

Source 1 — How to think about outcomes

Source extract

Our interest in and fascination for democracy often pushes us into taking a position that democracy can address all socio-economic and political problems. If some of our expectations are not met, we start blaming the idea of democracy. Or, we start doubting if we are living in a democracy. The first step towards thinking carefully about the outcomes of democracy is to recognise that democracy is just a form of government. It can only create conditions for achieving something. The citizens have to take advantage of those conditions and achieve those goals.

1.

What mistake does the passage warn against?

Model answer

Expecting democracy to solve every socio-economic and political problem, and then blaming the idea of democracy or doubting whether we live in one when expectations are not met.

2.

What is the correct way to judge democracy?

Model answer

To recognise that democracy is only a form of government which creates conditions, and then to ask what it can reasonably be expected to deliver — accountability, growth, equality, social harmony and dignity — and whether citizens have used those conditions.

How does this connect to the chapter’s conclusion?

Model answer

It sets up the finding that democracy’s record is mixed on outcomes but superior on legitimacy, dignity and freedom — and that its examination never ends.

Source 3 — Nannu and the RTI Act

Case study

Nannu is a daily wage earner. He lives in Welcome Mazdoor Colony, a slum habitation in East Delhi. He lost his ration card and applied for a duplicate one in January 2004. He made several rounds to the local Food and Civil Supplies office for the next three months. But the clerks and officials would not even look at him, leave alone do his job or bother to tell him the status of his application. Ultimately, he filed an application under the Right to Information Act asking for the daily progress made on his application, names of the officials, who were supposed to act on his application and what action would be taken against these officials for their inaction. Within a week of filing application under the Right to Information Act, he was visited by an inspector from the Food Department, who informed him that the card had been made and he could collect it from the office. When Nannu went to collect his card next day, he was given a very warm treatment by the Food and Supply Officer (FSO), who is the head of a Circle. The FSO offered him tea and requested him to withdraw his application under the Right to Information, since his work had already been done.

1.

What changed for Nannu, and why?

Model answer

Three months of visits produced nothing, but within a week of an RTI application an inspector visited him and his card was ready. The change came because the application made the officials’ inaction visible and recordable, and asked who was responsible and what action would follow.

2.

What does the FSO’s behaviour show?

Model answer

That transparency changes power relations: the officer who had ignored him now offered him tea and asked him to withdraw the application — evidence that accountability was felt.

3.

Which democratic outcome does this illustrate?

Model answer

Accountability and transparency — the right to information as a mechanism for citizens to hold government to account — and, in the chapter’s language, the transformation of a subject into a citizen.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

If dictatorships grow slightly faster, why does the chapter still defend democracy?

Model answer

Because growth is one outcome among several, and even there the difference is slight and depends on many factors other than the form of government. Dictatorships cannot distribute the benefits of growth equally, cannot correct their mistakes, and deny dignity and freedom. Democracy’s distinctive achievements are legitimacy, accountability, transparency and the recognition of equal dignity — which is why 88 per cent in South Asia find democracy suitable for their country despite their complaints. The defence is therefore both prudential and moral. (Model answer.)

2.

“The poor are in a majority, so democracy must be rule of the poor.” Is this a good argument?

Model answer

It is a good hope but a weak prediction. A majority in a democracy is formed afresh on each issue; the poor are numerous but scattered, less organised and less able to finance politics, while money and access weigh heavily. That is why democracies have not been very successful in reducing inequality. The argument becomes stronger only if the poor are organised — through unions, movements, parties and rights-based entitlements. (Model answer.)

3.

Why does the chapter say democracy’s examination never gets over, and is that a strength or a weakness?

Model answer

Because each benefit creates new expectations, and expectations become the criteria for judging democracy. It is a strength: it means democracy is self-correcting and that citizens keep power holders under scrutiny. It can feel like a weakness, because no democratic government ever satisfies its citizens — but the chapter reads complaint as evidence of awareness, and therefore as the transformation of subjects into citizens. (Model answer.)

4.

A country holds regular elections but bans criticism of the government. Does it meet the chapter’s test of democratic outcomes?

Model answer

No. Elections alone are one measure; the chapter also requires open public debate on major policies, the right to information, transparency in decision-making and the ability to correct mistakes. A ban on criticism removes exactly the mechanisms of accountability that make elections meaningful, and it removes the public expression of dissatisfaction that the chapter treats as evidence of democratic success. (Model answer.)

5.

How would you measure whether your own State is delivering democratic outcomes? Suggest three indicators.

Model answer

For example: (i) voter turnout and the incidence of re-polling or violence, as measures of free and fair elections; (ii) the number of RTI applications answered within the statutory period, as a measure of transparency; (iii) human development indicators — literacy, infant mortality, school attendance — plus the poverty rate by social group, as measures of equality and dignity. Each pairs a procedural test with a substantive one, which is how the chapter judges democracy. (Model answer.)

6.

“Democracy has strengthened the claims of the disadvantaged even where it has not removed their disadvantage.” Explain with the examples of women and caste.

Model answer

Because democracy gives a legal and moral basis to equality. On women: long struggles created the recognition that equal treatment is a necessary ingredient of a democratic society, so discrimination is now legally and morally unacceptable even where it persists — which makes further struggle possible in a way it would not be in a non-democratic set-up. On caste: democracy strengthened the claims of disadvantaged castes to equal status and opportunity; atrocities continue but they lack the moral and legal foundations they once had. The recognition is the achievement; the practice is the continuing test. (Model answer.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

To judge democracy we must first recognise that it is only a form of government: it can create conditions, but citizens must take advantage of them. Democracy is seen as good in principle yet disappointing in practice, and the chapter assesses it on five outcomes. On accountable, responsive and legitimate government, democracy is slower because it rests on deliberation, but its decisions are more acceptable and effective, and it offers transparency; its record on fair chances to all, on sharing information and on corruption is poor, yet it is undoubtedly more legitimate — the people’s own government — which is why support for democracy is overwhelming across South Asia (88 per cent find it suitable; 94 per cent want elected leaders), with Pakistan the exception on preference for democracy.

On economic growth, dictatorships grew slightly faster between 1950 and 2000, but growth depends on population, the global situation, cooperation from other countries and government priorities, and dictatorships cannot distribute its benefits. On inequality, democracies have not been very successful: in India a large section remains poor despite being a large share of the electorate, and in Bangladesh more than half the population is poor — because political equality does not become economic equality.

On social diversity, democracy is best suited to negotiate differences, since non-democratic regimes suppress them — but only if democracy is not reduced to rule by majority opinion and if rule by majority does not become rule by a majority community, so that every citizen has a chance of being in the majority at some point. On dignity and freedom, democracy stands much superior to any other form of government: women’s dignity and the claims of disadvantaged castes now rest on legal and moral foundations, even where practice lags. And because expectations keep rising, democracy’s examination never ends — the fact that people complain is itself proof of success, since it shows that subjects have become citizens.
