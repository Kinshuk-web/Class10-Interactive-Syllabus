Chapter 2.1 • Identification

1. How Do We Identify Acids and Bases?

Acids are sour in taste and turn blue litmus red. Bases are bitter, soapy to feel, and turn red litmus blue. Instead of tasting unknown chemical substances, we utilize Chemical & Olfactory Indicators.

Litmus Solution / Paper

Extracted from Lichen (Thallophyta). Neutral color is purple.

In Acid: Turns RED
In Base: Turns BLUE

Phenolphthalein

Synthetic indicator used in titrations and neutralization tests.

In Acid: Colorless
In Base: Deep Pink

Methyl Orange

Synthetic indicator giving crisp, clear color change.

In Acid: Red
In Base: Yellow

Olfactory Indicators

Substances whose odour changes in acidic/basic media.

In Acid: Retains Odour
In Base: Loses Odour

Other Natural Indicators (NCERT Page 17 & Group Activity)

Turmeric (Haldi): Remains yellow in acid. Turns reddish-brown in basic solutions (like soap on curry stains!).
Red Cabbage Extract: Red/pink in acid, green in basic media.
Flower Petals: Hydrangea, Petunia, and Geranium change color depending on acidity or alkalinity.

1. How do we identify them?

Before diving into equations, we need a way to test whether a substance is acidic or basic. Acids are generally sour to taste and turn blue litmus red. Bases are bitter, soapy to touch, and turn red litmus blue. We use Indicators to test them safely.

Litmus Solution

A natural indicator extracted from lichens. Neutral color is purple.

Acid: Red Base: Blue

Phenolphthalein

A synthetic indicator often used in acid-base titrations.

Acid: Colorless Base: Pink

Methyl Orange

Another synthetic indicator. Shows clear distinct colors.

Acid: Red Base: Yellow

Olfactory

Substances whose odour changes in acidic or basic media (Vanilla, Onion).

Acid: Retains Smell Base: Loses Smell

Note for Students: Arrhenius concept states that Acids produce H⁺ (aq) ions in water, while Bases produce OH⁻ (aq) ions. Dry HCl gas does not change the color of dry litmus paper because H⁺ ions are only produced in the presence of water!

5 Critical Applications of pH in Daily Life (NCERT Chapter 2.3.1)

🌱

1. Plant Growth & Soil pH

Plants require a specific pH range (~6.0–7.5). If soil is too acidic (pH < 6), farmers treat it with Quicklime (CaO), Slaked Lime ($Ca(OH)_2$), or Chalk ($CaCO_3$).

🩺

2. pH in Our Digestive System

Stomach produces dilute $HCl$ (pH ~1.2) for digestion. Overeating causes excess acid and indigestion pain. Antacids like Milk of Magnesia ($Mg(OH)_2$) neutralise excess acid.

🦷

3. Tooth Decay Prevention

Tooth enamel is made of Calcium Hydroxyapatite (hardest body substance). Tooth decay starts when mouth pH drops below 5.5 due to bacterial acid production. Toothpaste (basic) neutralizes acid.

🐝

4. Animal & Plant Warfare

Bee sting and Nettle plant leaves inject Methanoic Acid (HCOOH) causing pain. Mild bases like baking soda or rubbing basic Dock plant leaves neutralizes the sting!

🌧️

5. Aquatic Life & Acid Rain

When rainwater pH drops below 5.6, it is called Acid Rain. When it flows into rivers, it lowers aquatic water pH, making fish survival difficult.

🪐

Atmosphere of Venus

Venus atmosphere is made up of thick white and yellowish clouds of concentrated Sulfuric Acid ($H_2SO_4$). Life cannot exist under such hyper-acidic conditions!

Chapter 2.4 • Industrial Chemistry

5. Chlor-Alkali Process & Important Salts

Common Salt ($NaCl$) is the key raw material for manufacturing essential industrial chemicals. Explore the Chlor-Alkali electrolysis cell and master 5 key salts.

Electrolysis of Brine: 2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g)

Three Vital Industrial Products

1. Chlorine Gas (Cl₂) — At Anode

Used for water treatment, swimming pools, PVC pipes, disinfectants, CFCs, and pesticides.

2. Hydrogen Gas (H₂) — At Cathode

Rocket fuel, margarine manufacturing, and ammonia production for agricultural fertilizers.

3. Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) — Near Cathode

Degreasing metals, soap and detergent manufacturing, paper mills, artificial fibers (rayon).

Caustic Soda

Sodium Hydroxide

NaOH

Formed via Chlor-Alkali process electrolysis of brine. Highly basic alkali.

2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂
Key Board Uses:

Soaps, paper making, metal degreasing.

Calcium Oxychloride

Bleaching Powder

CaOCl₂

Action of chlorine gas on dry slaked lime [$Ca(OH)_2$].

Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O
Key Board Uses:

Textile cotton bleaching, oxidizing agent, disinfecting drinking water.

Sodium Hydrogen Carbonate

Baking Soda

NaHCO₃

Mild non-corrosive basic salt. On heating during baking, liberates $CO_2$ gas bubbles.

2NaHCO₃ --Δ--> Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑
Key Board Uses:

Antacids, crispy pakoras, fire extinguishers. (Mixed with tartaric acid = Baking Powder!).

Hydrated Sodium Carbonate

Washing Soda

Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Recrystallization of anhydrous sodium carbonate with 10 water molecules of crystallization.

Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O
Key Board Uses:

Glass, soap & paper industry, removing permanent hardness of water, Borax compound.

Calcium Sulphate Hemihydrate

Plaster of Paris (POP)

CaSO₄·½H₂O

Formed by heating Gypsum ($CaSO_4 \cdot 2H_2O$) at 373 K (100°C). On adding water, sets back into hard solid Gypsum.

CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O
Uses:

Fractured bone plasters, decorative statues, smooth ceiling designs.

🚨 Critical Board Question Caution

Why 373 K? If Gypsum is heated above 373 K, all water of crystallization is lost, yielding anhydrous $CaSO_4$ (known as Dead Burnt Plaster), which loses its setting property with water!

Water of Crystallization: Copper Sulphate = CuSO₄·5H₂O (Blue)
