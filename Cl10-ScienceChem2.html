Acids, Bases & Salts

Explore the fundamental nature of chemical compounds. Understand how they dissociate in water, learn to predict their reactions, observe indicators, and discover the vital roles these chemicals play in our daily lives.

1. How do we identify them?

Before diving into equations, we need a way to test whether a substance is acidic or basic. Acids are generally sour to taste and turn blue litmus red. Bases are bitter, soapy to touch, and turn red litmus blue. We use Indicators to test them safely.

Litmus Solution

A natural indicator extracted from lichens. Neutral color is purple.

Acid: Red Base: Blue

Phenolphthalein

A synthetic indicator often used in acid-base titrations.

Acid: Colorless Base: Pink

Methyl Orange

Another synthetic indicator. Shows clear distinct colors.

Acid: Red Base: Yellow

Olfactory

Substances whose odour changes in acidic or basic media (Vanilla, Onion).

Acid: Retains Smell Base: Loses Smell

Note for Students: Arrhenius concept states that Acids produce H⁺ (aq) ions in water, while Bases produce OH⁻ (aq) ions. Dry HCl gas does not change the color of dry litmus paper because H⁺ ions are only produced in the presence of water!

3. The pH Scale (Universal Indicator)

pH stands for 'potenz' in German, meaning power. It measures the H⁺ ion concentration. Slide to explore pH values of common substances. pH < 7 is acidic, pH = 7 is neutral, pH > 7 is basic.

0 (Strong Acid) 7 (Neutral) 14 (Strong Base)
7

Neutral

[H⁺] = [OH⁻]

Common Substance

Pure Water, Blood

Tooth Decay

Tooth decay starts when the pH of the mouth is lower than 5.5. Bacteria degrade sugar to produce acids.

Soil & Plants

Most plants grow best when soil pH is close to 7. If too acidic, farmers add quick lime (CaO).

4. Chemicals from Common Salt

NaCl (Sodium Chloride) is a crucial raw material for various everyday chemicals. Here are the essential salts you need to know for your boards.

Sodium Hydroxide

NaOH

Prepared by the Chlor-alkali process. Electricity is passed through aqueous NaCl (brine), producing NaOH, Cl₂, and H₂.

2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g)

Uses: Degreasing metals, soaps, paper making.

Bleaching Powder

CaOCl₂

Produced by the action of chlorine on dry slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂]. Cl₂ from chlor-alkali process is used here.

Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O

Uses: Bleaching cotton/linen, purifying drinking water.

Baking Soda

NaHCO₃

Mild, non-corrosive basic salt. Used in cooking. When heated during baking, it releases CO₂ making bread fluffy.

2NaHCO₃ (heat) → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑

Uses: Antacids, fire extinguishers, baking powder.

Washing Soda

Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Recrystallization of sodium carbonate gives washing soda. It is a basic salt containing 10 molecules of water of crystallization.

Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Uses: Glass, soap, paper industries, removing permanent hardness of water.

Plaster of Paris (POP)

CaSO₄·½H₂O (Calcium Sulphate Hemihydrate)

On heating Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) at exactly 373 K, it loses water molecules and becomes POP. If mixed with water again, it sets into hard solid Gypsum.

CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (Gypsum)

Uses: Doctors use for fractured bones, making toys, smooth surfaces.

Caution: Heating above 373 K produces dead burnt plaster (anhydrous CaSO₄), which doesn't set with water!
