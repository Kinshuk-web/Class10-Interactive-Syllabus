Forest and Wildlife Resources

We share this planet with millions of other living beings, and we depend on them for the air we breathe, the water we drink and the soil that grows our food. This chapter looks at India’s forest and wildlife wealth, why it is under stress, how the state protects it, and how ordinary villagers and tribal communities protect it too.

Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

We share this planet with millions of other living beings, starting from micro-organisms and bacteria, lichens to banyan trees, elephants and blue whales. This entire habitat that we live in has immense biodiversity. We humans, along with all living organisms, form a complex web of ecological system in which we are only a part and very much dependent on this system for our own existence.

For example, the plants, animals and micro-organisms re-create the quality of the air we breathe, the water we drink and the soil that produces our food, without which we cannot survive. Forests play a key role in the ecological system as these are also the primary producers on which all other living beings depend.

The chapter describes India’s flora and fauna, explains why conservation became essential, and traces the legal and project-based response: the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 and Project Tiger, 1973. It then classifies forests as reserved, protected and unclassed, and finally shows how communities — through sacred groves, the Chipko movement, Beej Bachao Andolan, Navdanya and joint forest management — conserve forests and wildlife.

Flora and Fauna in India

We share this planet with millions of other living beings, starting from micro-organisms and bacteria, lichens to banyan trees, elephants and blue whales. This entire habitat that we live in has immense biodiversity. We humans along with all living organisms form a complex web of ecological system in which we are only a part and very much dependent on this system for our own existence. For example, the plants, animals and micro-organisms re-create the quality of the air we breathe, the water we drink and the soil that produces our food without which we cannot survive. Forests play a key role in the ecological system as these are also the primary producers on which all other living beings depend.

If you look around, you will be able to find that there are some animals and plants which are unique in your area. In fact, India is one of the world’s richest countries in terms of its vast array of biological diversity. This is possibly twice or thrice the number yet to be discovered. These diverse flora and fauna are so well integrated in our daily life that we take these for granted. But, lately, they are under great stress mainly due to insensitivity to our environment.

Conservation of Forest and Wildlife in India

Conservation, in the background of rapid decline in wildlife population and forestry, has become essential. But why do we need to conserve our forests and wildlife?

  • Conservation preserves the ecological diversity and our life support systems — water, air and soil.
  • It also preserves the genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth of species and breeding.
  • For example, in agriculture we are still dependent on traditional crop varieties. Fisheries too are heavily dependent on the maintenance of aquatic biodiversity.

In the 1960s and 1970s, conservationists demanded a national wildlife protection programme. The Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act was implemented in 1972, with various provisions for protecting habitats. An all-India list of protected species was also published.

The thrust of the programme was towards protecting the remaining population of certain endangered species by:

  • banning hunting,
  • giving legal protection to their habitats, and
  • restricting trade in wildlife.

Subsequently, central and many state governments established national parks and wildlife sanctuaries. The central government also announced several projects for protecting specific animals which were gravely threatened, including the tiger, the one-horned rhinoceros, the Kashmir stag or hangul, three types of crocodiles — fresh water crocodile, saltwater crocodile and the Gharial — the Asiatic lion, and others.

Most recently, the Indian elephant, black buck (chinkara), the great Indian bustard (godawan) and the snow leopard, etc., have been given full or partial legal protection against hunting and trade throughout India.

Project Tiger

Tiger is one of the key wildlife species in the faunal web. In 1973, the authorities realised that the tiger population had dwindled to 1,827 from an estimated 55,000 at the turn of the century.

The major threats to the tiger population are numerous, such as poaching for trade, shrinking habitat, depletion of prey base species, growing human population, etc. The trade of tiger skins and the use of their bones in traditional medicines, especially in the Asian countries, left the tiger population on the verge of extinction. Since India and Nepal provide habitat to about two-thirds of the surviving tiger population in the world, these two nations became prime targets for poaching and illegal trading.

“Project Tiger”, one of the well-publicised wildlife campaigns in the world, was launched in 1973. Tiger conservation has been viewed not only as an effort to save an endangered species, but with equal importance as a means of preserving biotypes of sizeable magnitude.

Types and Distribution of Forest and Wildlife Resources

Even if we want to conserve our vast forest and wildlife resources, it is rather difficult to manage, control and regulate them. In India, much of its forest and wildlife resources are either owned or managed by the government through the Forest Department or other government departments. These are classified under the following categories.

Community and Conservation

Conservation strategies are not new in our country. We often ignore that in India, forests are also home to some of the traditional communities. In some areas of India, local communities are struggling to conserve these habitats along with government officials, recognising that only this will secure their own long-term livelihood.

In Sariska Tiger Reserve, Rajasthan, villagers have fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act. In many areas, villagers themselves are protecting habitats and explicitly rejecting government involvement. The inhabitants of five villages in the Alwar district of Rajasthan have declared 1,200 hectares of forest as the Bhairodev Dakav ‘Sonchuri’, declaring their own set of rules and regulations which do not allow hunting, and are protecting the wildlife against any outside encroachments.

The famous Chipko movement in the Himalayas has not only successfully resisted deforestation in several areas but has also shown that community afforestation with indigenous species can be enormously successful.

Attempts to revive the traditional conservation methods or developing new methods of ecological farming are now widespread. Farmers and citizens’ groups like the Beej Bachao Andolan in Tehri and Navdanya have shown that adequate levels of diversified crop production without the use of synthetic chemicals are possible and economically viable.

In India, the joint forest management (JFM) programme furnishes a good example for involving local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests. The programme has been in formal existence since 1988, when the state of Odisha passed the first resolution for joint forest management. JFM depends on the formation of local (village) institutions that undertake protection activities mostly on degraded forest land managed by the forest department. In return, the members of these communities are entitled to intermediary benefits like non-timber forest produce and a share in the timber harvested by “successful protection”.

The clear lesson from the dynamics of both environmental destruction and reconstruction in India is that local communities everywhere have to be involved in some kind of natural resource management. But there is still a long way to go before local communities are at the centre-stage in decision-making. Accept only those economic or developmental activities that are people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding.

Box: Sacred groves — a wealth of diverse and rare species

Nature worship is an age-old tribal belief based on the premise that all creations of nature have to be protected. Such beliefs have preserved several virgin forests in pristine form called Sacred Groves (the forests of Gods and Goddesses). These patches of forest or parts of large forests have been left untouched by the local people and any interference with them is banned.

Certain societies revere a particular tree which they have preserved from time immemorial:

  • The Mundas and the Santhal of the Chota Nagpur region worship mahua (Bassia latifolia) and kadamba (Anthocaphalus cadamba) trees.
  • The tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship the tamarind (Tamarindus indica) and mango (Mangifera indica) trees during weddings.
  • To many of us, peepal and banyan trees are considered sacred.

Indian society comprises several cultures, each with its own set of traditional methods of conserving nature and its creations. Sacred qualities are often ascribed to springs, mountain peaks, plants and animals which are closely protected. You will find troops of macaques and langurs around many temples. They are fed daily and treated as a part of temple devotees. In and around Bishnoi villages in Rajasthan, herds of blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai and peacocks can be seen as an integral part of the community and nobody harms them.

What is biodiversity? Why is biodiversity important for human lives? (about 30 words)

4.
NCERT exercise3 marks

How have human activities affected the depletion of flora and fauna? Explain. (about 30 words)

Model answer

Human insensitivity to the environment has put flora and fauna under great stress: habitats shrink as forests are cleared for agriculture, settlements and industry; poaching and trade in wildlife products such as tiger skins and bones push species to the verge of extinction; pollution and growing human population deplete prey species and degrade ecosystems.

5.
NCERT exercise5 marks

Describe how communities have conserved and protected forests and wildlife in India? (about 120 words)

Model answer

Communities have conserved forests in many ways. In Sariska Tiger Reserve, Rajasthan, villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act. In Alwar district, five villages declared 1,200 hectares as the Bhairodev Dakav “Sonchuri”, framing their own rules that ban hunting and protect wildlife from outside encroachment. The Chipko movement in the Himalayas resisted deforestation and showed that community afforestation with indigenous species can succeed. Farmers’ groups like the Beej Bachao Andolan in Tehri and Navdanya demonstrated ecologically sound, chemical-free farming. Under joint forest management, formal since Odisha’s 1988 resolution, village institutions protect degraded forest land and receive non-timber forest produce and a share of timber. 6.
NCERT exercise5 marks

Write a note on good practices towards conserving forest and wildlife. (about 120 words)

Model answer

Good practices include legal protection of habitats through the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, with an all-India list of protected species, a ban on hunting and restriction of trade; species projects such as Project Tiger (1973) and protection for the rhino, hangul, crocodiles, Asiatic lion, elephant, black buck, great Indian bustard and snow leopard; and national parks and sanctuaries. Equally important are community practices: sacred groves, the Sonchuri of Alwar, Chipko, Beej Bachao Andolan, Navdanya and joint forest management, which give local people a stake in protection. Conservation planning is now shifting from a few species to biodiversity as a whole — insects, butterflies, moths, beetles and, since 1991, plants are also protected. The lesson is that people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding activities work best.

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

Why is it necessary to conserve forests and wildlife? Give three reasons. (3 marks)

Model answer

(i) Conservation preserves ecological diversity and our life-support systems — water, air and soil. (ii) It preserves the genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth of species and breeding. (iii) Agriculture still depends on traditional crop varieties and fisheries on aquatic biodiversity, so conservation protects our food security and livelihoods.

2.

What was the thrust of the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972? (3 marks)

Model answer

Implemented in 1972 with provisions for protecting habitats, it published an all-India list of protected species. Its thrust was to protect the remaining population of certain endangered species by banning hunting, giving legal protection to their habitats and restricting trade in wildlife.

3.

Describe the threats to the tiger population and the response of the government. (5 marks)

Model answer

Threats: poaching for trade, shrinking habitat, depletion of prey base species and a growing human population. The trade in tiger skins and the use of tiger bones in traditional medicines, especially in Asian countries, left the tiger on the verge of extinction; since India and Nepal hold about two-thirds of the surviving tiger population, they became prime targets for poaching and illegal trade. Response: Project Tiger was launched in 1973, when numbers had fallen to 1,827 from about 55,000 at the turn of the century. It is one of the world’s best-publicised wildlife campaigns, and tiger conservation is seen not only as saving an endangered species but as preserving biotypes of sizeable magnitude. Tiger reserves include Corbett, Sunderbans, Bandhavgarh, Sariska, Manas and Periyar.

4.

Distinguish between reserved, protected and unclassed forests. (3 marks)

Model answer

Reserved forests: more than half the total forest land; the most valuable for conservation of forest and wildlife. Protected forests: almost one-third of the total forest area, declared by the Forest Department and protected from any further depletion. Unclassed forests: other forests and wastelands belonging to both government and private individuals and communities; found in large proportion in all north-eastern states and parts of Gujarat. Reserved and protected forests together are called permanent forest estates.

5.

Which state has the largest area under permanent forests? What do such forests produce? (2 marks)

Model answer

Madhya Pradesh has the largest area under permanent forests, constituting 75 per cent of its total forest area. Permanent forest estates are maintained for producing timber and other forest produce, and for protective reasons.

6.

What is joint forest management? How does it benefit local communities? (3 marks)

Model answer

JFM is a programme for involving local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests, formally in existence since 1988 when Odisha passed the first resolution. It depends on local (village) institutions that undertake protection activities mostly on degraded forest land managed by the forest department. In return, community members are entitled to intermediary benefits such as non-timber forest produce and a share in the timber harvested by “successful protection”.

7.

What are sacred groves? Give two examples of trees worshipped by communities. (3 marks)

Model answer

Sacred groves are virgin forests preserved in pristine form through nature worship — “the forests of Gods and Goddesses”. Patches of forest are left untouched by local people and any interference is banned. Examples: the Mundas and Santhals of Chota Nagpur worship mahua and kadamba; tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship tamarind and mango during weddings; peepal and banyan are widely considered sacred.

8.

How has conservation planning changed in recent years? (3 marks)

Model answer

Conservation projects now focus on biodiversity rather than on a few of its components. There is a more intensive search for different conservation measures, and even insects are finding a place in conservation planning. In the notifications under the Wildlife Act of 1980 and 1986, several hundred butterflies, moths, beetles and one dragonfly were added to the list of protected species; in 1991 plants were added for the first time, starting with six species.

9.

“Local communities must be involved in natural resource management.” Justify. (5 marks)

Model answer

Communities that live near forests depend on them for their long-term livelihood, so they have the strongest reason to protect them. The chapter gives several proofs: villagers of Sariska fought mining using the Wildlife Protection Act; five Alwar villages created the Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri with their own rules; the Chipko movement resisted deforestation and proved community afforestation works; Beej Bachao Andolan and Navdanya showed chemical-free diversified farming is viable; joint forest management gives villagers a share of produce in return for protection; and sacred groves preserve virgin forests through belief. The clear lesson is that local communities must be involved — though much remains to be done before they are at the centre-stage of decision-making.

10.

What kind of economic and developmental activities should be accepted, according to the chapter? (2 marks)

Model answer

Only those economic or developmental activities that are people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding.

1.
CBSE pattern

Explain the importance of the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, in conserving biodiversity. (CBSE pattern, 3 marks)

Model answer

The Act, implemented in 1972, provided for protecting habitats and published an all-India list of protected species. It banned hunting, gave legal protection to habitats of endangered species and restricted trade in wildlife. It also enabled the creation of national parks and wildlife sanctuaries and species projects such as Project Tiger, and later brought insects (1980, 1986) and plants (1991) into the protected list.

2.
CBSE pattern

Why did the government launch Project Tiger in 1973? (2 marks)

Model answer

Because the tiger population had dwindled to 1,827 from an estimated 55,000 at the turn of the century, mainly due to poaching for trade, shrinking habitat, depletion of prey species and growing human population. Project Tiger aimed to save the species and preserve biotypes of sizeable magnitude.

3.
CBSE pattern

“Conservation strategies are not new in India.” Support the statement with examples. (3 marks)

Model answer

Nature worship is an age-old tribal belief that all creations of nature must be protected, which preserved virgin forests as sacred groves. Communities revere particular trees — mahua and kadamba among the Mundas and Santhals, tamarind and mango among tribals of Odisha and Bihar. Temples feed macaques and langurs, and Bishnoi villages protect blackbuck, nilgai and peacocks. Movements such as Chipko and joint forest management continue this tradition.

4.
CBSE pattern

How does joint forest management involve local communities? (2 marks)

Model answer

Village institutions undertake protection activities on degraded forest land managed by the forest department and, in return, receive intermediary benefits such as non-timber forest produce and a share in timber harvested through successful protection. It began formally in 1988 with Odisha’s resolution.

5.
CBSE pattern

Name any three states with a large percentage of reserved forests. (1 mark)

Model answer

Any three of: Jammu and Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal and Maharashtra.

6.
CBSE pattern

Which states have a bulk of their forest area under protected forests? (1 mark)

Model answer

Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha and Rajasthan.

7.
CBSE pattern

Why are the north-eastern states important for unclassed forests? (2 marks)

Model answer

All the north-eastern states, and parts of Gujarat, have a very high percentage of their forests as unclassed forests managed by local communities, reflecting community ownership and traditional management.

8.
CBSE pattern

Give two examples of community-led conservation in Rajasthan. (2 marks)

Model answer

(i) In Sariska Tiger Reserve, villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act. (ii) Five villages in Alwar district declared 1,200 hectares of forest as the Bhairodev Dakav “Sonchuri”, with their own rules banning hunting and protecting wildlife from outside encroachment.

9.
CBSE pattern

What is meant by biodiversity? Why is India important in this context? (3 marks)

Model answer

Biodiversity is the rich variety of wildlife and cultivated species, diverse in form and function but closely integrated through networks of interdependence. India is one of the world’s richest countries in terms of its vast array of biological diversity, and possibly twice or thrice the number of species is still to be discovered.

10.
CBSE pattern

Mention any two animals that have recently been given full or partial legal protection against hunting and trade. (1 mark)

Model answer

The Indian elephant and the black buck (chinkara); also the great Indian bustard (godawan) and the snow leopard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick clarifications

1.

What is the difference between a national park and a wildlife sanctuary?

Model answer

The chapter names both as protected areas established by central and state governments after 1972, without distinguishing them. In practice a national park has stricter protection, but for this chapter you only need to know that both were created to protect habitats, and to be able to name examples such as Corbett National Park and Sariska Wildlife Sanctuary.

2.

Is Project Tiger only about the tiger?

Model answer

No. Tiger conservation has been viewed not only as an effort to save an endangered species, but with equal importance as a means of preserving biotypes of sizeable magnitude — that is, whole habitats and the species that live in them.

3.

Why are unclassed forests important?

Model answer

They are other forests and wastelands belonging to government, private individuals and communities, and in the north-east and parts of Gujarat they are managed by local communities — so they show community-based forest management in practice.

4.

What is a sacred grove?

Model answer

A patch of virgin forest left untouched by local people because of nature worship — “forests of Gods and Goddesses” — where any interference is banned.

5.

Who started the Chipko movement and when?

Model answer

The chapter names the Chipko movement in the Himalayas and its achievements but does not give a date or a leader. Answer only what is in the chapter: it resisted deforestation in several areas and showed that community afforestation with indigenous species can be enormously successful.

6.

What is the difference between reserved and protected forests?

Model answer

Reserved forests cover more than half the total forest land and are the most valuable for conservation. Protected forests cover almost one-third and are protected from any further depletion.

7.

Which is the first state to pass a joint forest management resolution?

Model answer

Odisha, in 1988, when JFM came into formal existence.

Source 1 — Buddha on the tree

Source extract
“The tree is a peculiar organism of unlimited kindness and benevolence and makes no demand for its sustenance, and extends generously the products of its life activity. It affords protection to all beings, offering shade even to the axemen who destroy it.”

— Gautama Buddha (487 B.C.)

1.

What quality of the tree does the quotation highlight?

Model answer

Its selfless generosity: it demands nothing for its own sustenance and gives shade even to those who cut it.

2.

How is this idea reflected in the chapter’s argument about conservation?

Model answer

The chapter argues that forests are primary producers on which all life depends and that human insensitivity has put them under great stress; conservation is therefore a moral as well as an ecological necessity, and communities that revere trees have preserved them.

3.

Name two trees considered sacred in India.

Model answer

Peepal and banyan; also mahua, kadamba, tamarind and mango in particular communities.

Source 2 — Newspaper report: “Gharial on the brink”

Case study (Fig. 2.3)

The chapter reproduces two newspaper cuttings. The first, “Gharial on the brink” by Romulus Whitaker and Janaki Lenin, reports that the gharial population has been at its lowest since the 1970s and asks what went wrong and what can be done, describing the five-kilometre stretch of the Girwa River in Katerniaghat Wildlife Sanctuary as one of the only three wild breeding sites left in the world. The second, “Bird deaths blamed on dirty Yamuna”, reports that recent bird deaths were blamed on pollution and contaminated fish.

1.

Which species is described as critically endangered in the first cutting?

Model answer

The gharial — one of the three types of crocodiles protected under the central government’s species projects (freshwater crocodile, saltwater crocodile and gharial).

2.

What reason is given for bird deaths in the second cutting?

Model answer

Pollution of the Yamuna and contaminated fish — that is, dirty water and a poisoned food chain.

3.

What does this pair of reports tell us about the threats to wildlife?

Model answer

That habitat loss and pollution, not only hunting, are major threats: river species lose clean water, prey and breeding sites, which is why conservation must protect whole habitats and biotypes, not just single animals.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

A forest is legally “protected” but local people are still cutting firewood from it. Suggest a solution using the chapter’s ideas.

Model answer

Protection on paper fails without community involvement. The chapter’s answer is joint forest management: form a village institution that protects the degraded forest and, in return, receives non-timber forest produce and a share of timber. Along with this, provide alternatives such as biogas or improved stoves to reduce firewood dependence. Where communities revere the forest — as with sacred groves — protection is even stronger. (Model answer.)

2.

“Conservation of a single species automatically conserves the whole forest.” Do you agree?

Model answer

Partly. Project Tiger shows that saving a large predator requires preserving biotypes of sizeable magnitude, so it does protect habitat and prey. But the chapter notes that conservation projects are now focusing on biodiversity rather than a few components, because insects and plants also matter and because a species can survive in a reserve while its wider habitat is degraded. Single-species projects are useful entry points, not a complete strategy. (Model answer.)

3.

Why should a city student care about sacred groves in Chota Nagpur?

Model answer

Because the ecological services of forests — air, water, soil — are shared. Sacred groves preserve virgin forest and genetic diversity that may be useful in agriculture and medicine, and they show that conservation works when people value nature culturally. Urban demand for timber, paper and land is itself one of the pressures on such forests, so city dwellers are part of the cause and must be part of the solution.

4.

Compare the government’s approach and the community’s approach to conservation as presented in the chapter. Which is more effective?

Model answer

The government approach is legal and administrative: the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, protected species lists, national parks and sanctuaries, and species projects such as Project Tiger. The community approach is local and cultural: sacred groves, Sonchuri rules, Chipko, seed movements and joint forest management. The chapter’s conclusion is that both are needed, and that local communities must be at the centre of decision-making because only people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding activities succeed in the long run.

5.

The chapter says “there is still a long way to go before local communities are at the centre-stage in decision-making.” What practical steps would bring them there?

Model answer

Giving village institutions legal authority and a share of revenue from forest produce; recording community rights over unclassed forests; consulting gram sabhas before projects that divert forest land; training local youth as forest guards and eco-guides; and linking conservation with livelihood through non-timber produce, eco-tourism and agro-forestry. (Model answer.)

6.

Use the two newspaper cuttings to argue that pollution is as serious a threat to wildlife as hunting.

Model answer

Hunting directly removes animals, but pollution removes the conditions for life. The gharial report shows a species reduced to three wild breeding sites; the Yamuna report shows birds dying because the river is polluted and fish are contaminated. Poisoned water and a contaminated food chain kill far more individuals, and over a wider area, than poaching does, and they cannot be reversed by guarding a single nest. Hence habitat protection must include cleaning rivers and controlling industrial and domestic waste. (Model answer.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

India is one of the world’s richest countries in biological diversity. Biodiversity — the immensely rich variety of wildlife and cultivated species, integrated through networks of interdependence — supports the air, water and soil on which we depend, and forests are the primary producers of the ecological system. Yet flora and fauna are under great stress because of human insensitivity to the environment.

Conservation is needed to preserve ecological diversity, life-support systems and genetic diversity. The Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act of 1972 created an all-India list of protected species and provided for protecting habitats, banning hunting and restricting trade in wildlife. National parks, sanctuaries and species projects followed, including Project Tiger (1973), which began when the tiger population had fallen to 1,827 from about 55,000 at the turn of the century.

Forest land is classified as reserved (more than half, the most valuable), protected (almost one-third) and unclassed (the rest, dominant in the north-east and parts of Gujarat); reserved and protected forests together are permanent forest estates, and Madhya Pradesh has the largest area under them.

Community conservation has a long history in India: sacred groves preserved through nature worship, villagers of Sariska resisting mining, the Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri of Alwar, the Chipko movement, Beej Bachao Andolan, Navdanya and joint forest management since 1988. The lesson is that local communities must be involved in natural resource management, and that only people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding activities should be accepted.
