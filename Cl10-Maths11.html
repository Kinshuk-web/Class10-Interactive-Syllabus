11.1 · The two cuts

02Sector and segment

Picture a circular disc of dough. You can cut a piece in two completely different ways.

Cut with two radii. Put the knife point at the centre O and cut outwards twice. The piece between the two cuts — bounded by two radii and the arc between them — is a sector. The angle ∠AOB at the centre is called the angle of the sector. A sector is the shape of a pizza slice.

Cut with a chord. Now join two points A and B on the circle with a straight line (a chord) and cut along it. The piece between the chord and the arc is a segment. A segment is the shape of a piece broken off the rim of a biscuit.

Sector

Bounded by 2 radii + 1 arc

Two sectors exist for any two radii: the small one (minor) and the big one (major).

angle of major sector = 360° − θ

Segment

Bounded by 1 chord + 1 arc

The chord cuts the disc into a minor segment (small cap) and a major segment (the rest).

segment = sector − triangle OAB

11.1 · Where the formula comes from

03Why θ/360? The unitary method

You never have to memorise the sector formula. You only have to believe one thing: a sector is a fraction of the whole circle, and that fraction is θ/360.

Step-through · Build the formula
Click “Next step”. Each line is one small, obvious move.
Step 1 — the whole thing. The full circle is itself a sector with angle 360°. Its area is πr² and its boundary (circumference) is 2πr.
Step 2 — one degree. Chop the circle into 360 identical slivers. One sliver has area πr²/360 and arc 2πr/360.
Step 3 — take θ of them. θ slivers give θ × πr²/360 for area, and θ × 2πr/360 for the arc.
Step 4 — tidy up. Area = θ360πr², arc = θ3602πr.
Step 5 — say it in English. “Take the fraction θ/360 of the whole circle.” That sentence solves every problem in this chapter, including the ones where you have forgotten the formula.

Two things that scale together

Because both formulae carry the same factor θ/360, the arc and the area grow in lockstep with the angle. Double the angle, double both. That is why you can also write:

Area of sector = ½ × (arc length) × r

It is exactly the triangle formula ½ × base × height, with the curved arc playing the part of the base. Useful as a shortcut when a question hands you the arc length instead of the angle.

Same idea, three questions

Given θ, find area → use the formula directly.

Given area, find θ → rearrange: θ = 360 × area / (πr²).

Given area and θ, find r → r² = 360 × area / (θπ).

Formula 1

04Arc length, area and perimeter

The arc is just the curved edge of the sector. Its length is the same fraction of the whole circumference, 2πr. The perimeter of a sector is the arc plus the two straight radii — forgetting those two radii is the single most common error in this chapter.

Speed trick If θ is a friendly fraction of 360 (30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 180), convert it to a fraction first: 60° → 1/6, 90° → 1/4, 120° → 1/3, 45° → 1/8, 180° → 1/2. Then you are multiplying one simple fraction by πr² instead of doing long division.

05Area of a segment

A segment has a straight edge, so no single formula covers it. Instead you take the sector and remove the isosceles triangle OAB that sits inside it.

Area of segment = Area of sector − Area of ΔOAB

And the triangle? With two sides r and included angle θ, its area is ½ r² sinθ. For the three angles that examiners actually use, that collapses into something you can do in your head:

The other piece

06Major sector and major segment

You rarely need a fresh formula for the big piece. The circle is a whole, so the big piece is “everything that is left”.

Major sector

= πr² − minor sector
or, with the angle: (360° − θ) × πr²/360

Both give the same number — the second one is faster when θ is small.

Major segment

= πr² − minor segment

Do not try to use (360°−θ) in the segment formula — the triangle for the reflex angle is a different triangle. Subtract from the whole circle instead.

Worked examples

08The two textbook examples, line by line

Example 1 · Sector of radius 4 cm, angle 30° (π = 3.14)

Step-through · Example 1
Find the minor sector, then the major sector two different ways.
Write what is given. r = 4 cm, θ = 30°, π = 3.14.
Area of sector = θ/360 × πr² = (30/360) × 3.14 × 16.
Simplify. 30/360 = 1/12, and 3.14 × 16 = 50.24, so area = 50.24/12 = 4.19 cm².
Major sector, route 1: πr² − 4.19 = 50.24 − 4.19 = 46.05 cm².
Major sector, route 2: (360 − 30)/360 × 3.14 × 16 = 330/360 × 50.24 = 46.05 cm². Same answer — as it must be.

Example 2 · Segment of radius 21 cm, angle 120° (π = 22/7)

Step-through · Example 2
The full “sector minus triangle” routine, exactly as the board expects it.
Plan. Segment AYB = sector OAYB − triangle OAB. Two pieces to find.
Sector. (120/360) × 22/7 × 21 × 21 = (1/3) × 22 × 63 = 462 cm².
Drop the perpendicular OM to the chord. It bisects AB and bisects the angle, so ∠AOM = 60°.
In right triangle OMA: OM = 21 cos 60° = 10.5 cm, and AM = 21 sin 60° = 21√3/2, so AB = 21√3 cm.
Triangle area = ½ × AB × OM = ½ × 21√3 × 10.5 = 441√3/4 cm².
Subtract. Segment = 462 − 441√3/4 = (21/4)(88 − 21√3) cm². With √3 = 1.73 this is about 271.1 cm².
Why the perpendicular appears The triangle OAB is isosceles with two sides r. Dropping the perpendicular from O gives two congruent right triangles, and the 60° halves come free. This is the same construction every time — only the angle changes.

Before you write the exam

10Six traps that cost marks

01

Perimeter ≠ arc

Perimeter of a sector = arc + 2r. If a question says “boundary of the sector”, it wants the radii too.

02

Degrees, not radians

θ in θ/360 must be in degrees. If the question gives π/3, convert: π/3 rad = 60°.

03

Units squared

Area is cm², m², km². Arc and perimeter are cm, m, km. Mixing them is an easy lost mark.

04

π value as given

Use 22/7 or 3.14 exactly as the question says. Do not silently swap one for the other.

05

Major piece

Always get it by subtracting from πr² (or from the whole for a segment), never by stuffing 360−θ into the segment formula.

06

Chord vs arc

A chord is straight; an arc is curved. A segment is bounded by both. A sector is bounded by two radii and an arc.

Marking habit that works Write the formula, then substitute, then simplify — three lines. Examiners award method marks even when the arithmetic slips, but only if the formula is visible.

13Frequently asked questions

A sector is cut by two radii — its point is at the centre, like a pizza slice. A segment is cut by one chord — its straight edge is away from the centre, like a broken piece of biscuit rim. A sector always contains the centre; a minor segment never does.

Because both area and arc length are proportional to the angle. The circle is the 360° case, so any smaller angle takes that fraction of it. It is the unitary method: find the value for 1°, then multiply by θ.

Whatever the question states. NCERT exercises say “unless stated otherwise, use π = 22/7”. If a question gives 3.14, use 3.14 — and if it gives nothing, 22/7 is the safe default for radii that are multiples of 7.

Area of circle − area of minor segment. Do not try to reuse the sector formula with 360° − θ; the triangle inside a reflex sector is a different shape and the usual 60/90/120 shortcuts do not apply.

Use area = ½ × arc × r. To get the angle, θ = (arc × 360)/(2πr). Both come from the same fraction, so nothing new to memorise.

Not compulsory, but a rough circle with the sector shaded takes ten seconds and prevents the classic mistake of subtracting the wrong piece. Examiners also find it easier to follow your method.

Constantly: the area of a circular arc appears in fan and turbine design, the wiper problem is real automotive geometry, and the same “fraction of a whole” logic powers pie charts and speedometer dials.

Chapter summary

16Five lines that contain the chapter

  • Length of arc of angle θ = (θ/360) × 2πr
  • Area of sector of angle θ = (θ/360) × πr²
  • Area of segment = area of sector − area of ΔOAB
  • Major sector = πr² − minor sector; major segment = πr² − minor segment
  • Perimeter of a sector = arc + 2r, and area = ½ × arc × r
