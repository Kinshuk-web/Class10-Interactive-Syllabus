Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

With this chapter we resume the tour of democracy. Last year we noted that in a democracy all power does not rest with any one organ of the government. An intelligent sharing of power among legislature, executive and judiciary is very important to the design of a democracy.

The chapter begins with two stories — Belgium and Sri Lanka. Both are democracies facing demands for power-sharing, and they handled them in opposite ways. Belgium recognised regional and cultural differences and, between 1970 and 1993, amended its constitution four times to work out an innovative arrangement: equal Dutch- and French-speaking ministers in the central government, powers given to state governments of the two regions, equal representation in Brussels, and a third “community government” elected by each language group.

Sri Lanka took the majoritarian path: Sinhala was made the only official language in 1956, preferential policies favoured Sinhala applicants for university places and government jobs, and the constitution stipulated that the state would protect and foster Buddhism. Tamils felt alienated, demanded autonomy and then an independent Tamil Eelam, and the distrust turned into a civil war that ended only in 2009.

From these two stories the chapter draws two sets of reasons for power sharing — prudential and moral — and then describes the four forms it takes: among organs of government (horizontal), among levels of government (vertical or federal), among social groups, and among political parties, pressure groups and movements.

Ethnic

A social division based on shared culture; people of the same ethnic group believe in their common descent because of similarities of physical type or culture, or both.

Majoritarianism

A belief that the majority community should be able to rule a country in whichever way it wants, disregarding the wishes and needs of the minority.

Civil war

A violent conflict between opposing groups within a country that becomes so intense that it appears like a war.

Prudential reasons

Reasons based on prudence — a careful calculation of gains and losses; here, that power sharing brings better outcomes.

Moral reasons

Reasons that emphasise the very act of power sharing as valuable — it is the spirit of democracy.

Horizontal distribution

Power shared among organs of government at the same level — legislature, executive, judiciary — through checks and balances.

Vertical distribution

Power shared among governments at different levels — central, state and local.

Community government

The Belgian third kind of government, elected by people of one language community — Dutch, French or German-speaking — wherever they live, with power over cultural, educational and language issues.

Belgium and Sri Lanka — Two Democracies, Two Paths

Belgium is a small country in Europe, smaller in area than the state of Haryana. It has borders with France, the Netherlands, Germany and Luxembourg. Its population is a little over one crore, about half that of Haryana — and its ethnic composition is very complex.

French-speaking40%Wallonia region
German-speaking1%—
In Brussels (the capital)80% French-speaking, 20% Dutch-speakingBrussels-Capital Region

The minority French-speaking community was relatively rich and powerful. This was resented by the Dutch-speaking community, who got the benefit of economic development and education much later. This led to tensions between the two communities during the 1950s and 1960s, and the tension was more acute in Brussels. Brussels presented a special problem: the Dutch-speaking people constituted a majority in the country, but a minority in the capital.

Sri Lanka is an island nation, just a few kilometres off the southern coast of Tamil Nadu, with about two crore people — about the same as Haryana. Its major social groups are the Sinhala-speakers (74 per cent) and the Tamil-speakers (18 per cent). Among Tamils there are two sub-groups: Tamil natives of the country are called “Sri Lankan Tamils” (13 per cent), and the rest, whose forefathers came from India as plantation workers during the colonial period, are called “Indian Tamils”. Sri Lankan Tamils are concentrated in the north and east of the country. Most Sinhala-speaking people are Buddhists, while most Tamils are Hindus or Muslims; about 7 per cent are Christians, both Tamil and Sinhala.

What could have happened

Imagine what could happen. In Belgium, the Dutch community could take advantage of its numeric majority and force its will on the French- and German-speaking population, pushing the conflict further and perhaps leading to a messy partition in which both sides claim Brussels. In Sri Lanka, the Sinhala community enjoyed an even bigger majority and could impose its will on the entire country.

Majoritarianism in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka emerged as an independent country in 1948. The leaders of the Sinhala community sought to secure dominance over government by virtue of their majority. As a result, the democratically elected government adopted a series of majoritarian measures to establish Sinhala supremacy.

  • In 1956, an Act was passed to recognise Sinhala as the only official language, thus disregarding Tamil.
  • Governments followed preferential policies that favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs.
  • A new constitution stipulated that the state shall protect and foster Buddhism.

All these measures, coming one after the other, gradually increased the feeling of alienation among the Sri Lankan Tamils. They felt that none of the major political parties led by Buddhist Sinhala leaders was sensitive to their language and culture; that the constitution and government policies denied them equal political rights, discriminated against them in getting jobs and other opportunities, and ignored their interests. As a result, relations between the Sinhala and Tamil communities strained over time.

The Sri Lankan Tamils launched parties and struggles for the recognition of Tamil as an official language, for regional autonomy and equality of opportunity in securing education and jobs. But their demand for more autonomy to provinces populated by Tamils was repeatedly denied. By the 1980s, several political organisations were formed demanding an independent Tamil Eelam (state) in the northern and eastern parts of Sri Lanka.

The distrust between the two communities turned into widespread conflict. It soon turned into a civil war. As a result, thousands of people of both communities were killed, many families were forced to leave the country as refugees and many more lost their livelihoods. The civil war caused a terrible setback to the social, cultural and economic life of the country. It ended in 2009.

Accommodation in Belgium

The Belgian leaders took a different path. They recognised the existence of regional differences and cultural diversities. Between 1970 and 1993 they amended their constitution four times so as to work out an arrangement that would enable everyone to live together within the same country. The arrangement is different from any other country and is very innovative.

The four elements of the Belgian model
ElementWhat it provides
Equal ministers in the central governmentThe constitution prescribes that the number of Dutch- and French-speaking ministers shall be equal. Some special laws require the support of a majority of members from each linguistic group, so no single community can make decisions unilaterally.
Powers to the regionsMany powers of the central government have been given to state governments of the two regions. The state governments are not subordinate to the central government.
BrusselsA separate government in which both communities have equal representation. The French-speaking people accepted equal representation in Brussels because the Dutch-speaking community accepted equal representation in the central government.
Community governmentA third kind of government, elected by people belonging to one language community — Dutch, French and German-speaking — no matter where they live. It has power regarding cultural, educational and language-related issues.

You might find the Belgian model very complicated — it indeed is, even for people living in Belgium. But these arrangements have worked well so far. They helped to avoid civic strife between the two major communities and a possible division of the country on linguistic lines. When many countries of Europe came together to form the European Union, Brussels was chosen as its headquarters.

Let’s think

Annette and Selvi: Annette studies in a Dutch-medium school in the northern region of Belgium, where many French-speaking students want the medium of instruction to be French. Selvi studies in a school in the northern region of Sri Lanka, where all students are Tamil-speaking and want the medium of instruction to be Tamil. If their parents approached their respective governments, who is more likely to succeed? — Annette, because Belgium’s power-sharing arrangements give language communities control over educational and language-related issues, whereas Sri Lanka’s majoritarian system has consistently denied such demands.

What Do the Two Stories Teach Us?

Both countries are democracies, yet they dealt with the question of power sharing differently. In Belgium, the leaders realised that the unity of the country is possible only by respecting the feelings and interests of different communities and regions — and that realisation produced mutually acceptable arrangements for sharing power.

Sri Lanka shows the opposite. It shows that if a majority community wants to force its dominance over others and refuses to share power, it can undermine the unity of the country. The comparison is the heart of the chapter.

Why Power Sharing Is Desirable

Two different sets of reasons can be given in favour of power sharing.

Prudential reasons

Firstly, power sharing is good because it helps to reduce the possibility of conflict between social groups. Since social conflict often leads to violence and political instability, power sharing is a good way to ensure the stability of political order. Imposing the will of the majority community over others may look like an attractive option in the short run, but in the long run it undermines the unity of the nation. Tyranny of the majority is not just oppressive for the minority; it often affects the majority adversely as well.

Moral reasons

There is a second, deeper reason why power sharing is good for democracies. Power sharing is the very spirit of democracy. A democratic rule involves sharing power with those affected by its exercise, and who have to live with its effects. People have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed. A legitimate government is one where citizens, through participation, acquire a stake in the system.

Key distinction

Let us call the first set of reasons prudential and the second moral. While prudential reasons stress that power sharing will bring out better outcomes, moral reasons emphasise the very act of power sharing as valuable.

Forms of Power Sharing

The idea of power-sharing has emerged in opposition to the notions of undivided political power. For a long time it was believed that all power of a government must reside in one person or group of persons located at one place, and that if the power to decide is dispersed it would not be possible to take quick decisions and enforce them. But these notions have changed with the emergence of democracy. One basic principle of democracy is that people are the source of all political power; in a democracy people rule themselves through institutions of self-government, due respect is given to diverse groups and views, and everyone has a voice in the shaping of public policies. Therefore, in a democracy political power should be distributed among as many citizens as possible.

1. Among organs of government — horizontal distribution

Power is shared among different organs of government, such as the legislature, executive and judiciary. This is called horizontal distribution of power because it allows different organs of government placed at the same level to exercise different powers. Such a separation ensures that none of the organs can exercise unlimited power — each organ checks the others, resulting in a balance of power among institutions. Ministers and government officials exercise power but are responsible to Parliament or the State Assemblies; judges are appointed by the executive but can check the functioning of the executive or the laws made by the legislatures. This arrangement is called a system of checks and balances.

2. Among governments at different levels — vertical distribution

Power can be shared among governments at different levels — a general government for the entire country (usually called the federal government, and in India the Central or Union Government) and governments at the provincial or regional level (in India, State Governments). In countries with different levels of government the constitution clearly lays down the powers of each level. This is called federal division of power, and the same principle can extend to levels lower than the State — municipality and panchayat. Division of powers involving higher and lower levels of government is called vertical division of power.

3. Among social groups

Power may also be shared among different social groups, such as religious and linguistic groups. The “community government” in Belgium is a good example. In some countries there are constitutional and legal arrangements whereby socially weaker sections and women are represented in the legislatures and administration — for example the system of reserved constituencies in our assemblies and Parliament. This arrangement gives space in government and administration to diverse social groups who would otherwise feel alienated, and gives minority communities a fair share in power.

4. Among political parties, pressure groups and movements

Power-sharing arrangements can also be seen in the way political parties, pressure groups and movements control or influence those in power. In a democracy citizens must have freedom to choose among various contenders for power, and in contemporary democracies this takes the form of competition among different parties, which ensures that power does not remain in one hand. In the long run, power is shared among different political parties representing different ideologies and social groups. Sometimes this sharing is direct — when two or more parties form an alliance to contest elections, and if elected form a coalition government. Interest groups of traders, businessmen, industrialists, farmers and industrial workers also have a share in governmental power, either through participation in governmental committees or by influencing decision-making.

Khalil’s Dilemma — Applying the Lesson

Vetal’s story: in Beirut lived Khalil, whose father was an Orthodox Christian and mother a Sunni Muslim. Lebanon fought a bitter civil war, in which one of Khalil’s uncles was killed. At the end of the war Lebanon’s leaders agreed on basic rules for power sharing: the President must belong to the Maronite sect of Catholic Christians, the Prime Minister must be from the Sunni Muslim community, the Deputy Prime Minister’s post is fixed for the Orthodox Christian sect and the Speaker’s for Shi’a Muslims. Under this pact the Christians agreed not to seek French protection and the Muslims not to seek unification with Syria. When they agreed, the two communities were nearly equal in population; both sides have continued to respect the agreement though the Muslims are now in a clear majority.

Khalil dislikes the system: he is popular and ambitious, but the top position is out of his reach because he practises neither his father’s nor his mother’s religion. He asks why Lebanon cannot be a “normal” democracy — hold an election, let everyone contest, and whoever wins the most votes becomes president, whatever community he comes from. His elders, who have seen the bloodshed, say the present system is the best guarantee of peace.

Khalil’s dilemma

Vetal’s question: if you had the power to rewrite the rules in Lebanon, what would you do — adopt the “regular” rules as Khalil suggests, stick to the old rules, or do something else? Model answer: the chapter’s lesson is that power sharing matters more than a purely numerical majority when a society has deep divisions. A defensible answer keeps the principle of shared power but makes it flexible — for example, a proportional system in which all communities are represented in proportion to their numbers rather than by fixed posts, combined with a requirement of cross-community consent for key decisions. Simply holding a winner-takes-all election could repeat Sri Lanka’s mistake. (Student answers may vary if reasoned.)

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

Look at the maps of Belgium and Sri Lanka. In which regions do you find concentration of different communities?

Model answer

In Belgium the Dutch-speaking Flemish are concentrated in the northern region and the French-speaking Walloons in the south, with Brussels a largely French-speaking island inside the Flemish north and a small German-speaking area in the east. In Sri Lanka the Sinhala-speakers are concentrated in the south, west and centre, while Sri Lankan Tamils are concentrated in the north and east; Indian Tamils live mainly in the central plantation areas.

2.

What could happen if the majority community in either country forced its will on the others?

Model answer

In Belgium, the Dutch majority could have pushed its will on the French- and German-speaking populations, deepening conflict and possibly leading to a messy partition in which both sides claimed Brussels. In Sri Lanka, the Sinhala majority did impose its will — and the result was alienation, a demand for Tamil Eelam and a long civil war.

3.

Annette studies in a Dutch-medium school in Belgium and Selvi in a Tamil-speaking school in northern Sri Lanka. Both want a different medium of instruction. Whose parents are more likely to succeed, and why?

Model answer

Annette’s. Belgium’s power-sharing arrangements include a community government with power over cultural, educational and language-related issues, so a language community can act on such a demand. Sri Lanka’s majoritarian constitution and language policy have consistently denied Tamil demands.

4.

What is the main difference between a federal form of government and a unitary one? (Asked again in Chapter 2, answered here for the Belgian case.)

Model answer

In a unitary system there is either only one level of government or the sub-units are subordinate to the central government, which can pass orders to them. In a federal system the central government cannot order the state government to do something; state governments have powers of their own for which they are not answerable to the centre, and both levels are separately answerable to the people. Belgium moved from unitary to federal in 1993; Sri Lanka remains unitary in practice.

5.

What are the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies? Give an example of each.

Model answer

(i) Among organs of government — horizontal distribution with checks and balances, as between the legislature, executive and judiciary in India. (ii) Among governments at different levels — vertical or federal division, as between the Union, States and panchayats in India, or the Belgian state governments. (iii) Among social groups — the community government in Belgium, or reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes and Tribes in India. (iv) Among political parties, pressure groups and movements — coalition governments and alliances, and the influence of traders’ and farmers’ organisations.

6.

State one prudential reason and one moral reason for power sharing with an example from the Indian context.

Model answer

Prudential: power sharing reduces the possibility of conflict between social groups and ensures political stability — India’s linguistic reorganisation of states and its language policy helped avoid the conflict Sri Lanka suffered. Moral: power sharing is the very spirit of democracy, since people have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed — the panchayati raj system, where villagers directly participate in decisions, gives citizens a stake in the system.

7.

Three students drew different conclusions: Thomman — power sharing is necessary only in societies with religious, linguistic or ethnic divisions; Mathayi — it is suitable only for big countries with regional divisions; Ouseph — every society needs some form of power sharing even if it is small or has no social divisions. Which do you agree with and why?

Model answer

Ouseph. Power sharing is not merely a remedy for division; it is the normal design of a democracy. Even a small, homogeneous society needs the horizontal separation of legislature, executive and judiciary so that no organ has unlimited power, and needs competition among parties and the influence of interest groups so that power does not stay in one hand. Belgium adopted federalism though it is small — which also answers Mathayi.

8.

The Mayor of Merchtem, a town near Brussels, defended a ban on speaking French in the town’s schools, saying it would help non-Dutch speakers integrate in the Flemish town. Is this in keeping with the spirit of Belgium’s power-sharing arrangements?

Model answer

No. Belgium’s arrangements rest on recognising linguistic diversity and giving each community control over its own language and education, and on equal representation rather than assimilation. A ban on French in schools denies the French-speaking community exactly the cultural and educational space the model was built to protect, and is the kind of majoritarian measure that caused Sri Lanka’s conflict.

9.

Read the passage on panchayats and pick out any one prudential reason for power sharing offered in it.

Model answer

“Giving power to Panchayats is also a way to reduce corruption and increase administrative efficiency… When people participate in the planning and implementation of developmental schemes, they would naturally exercise greater control over these schemes. This would eliminate the corrupt middlemen.” This is prudential — it argues from the gains that follow, not from the rightness of participation.

10.

Which arguments are in favour of power sharing: A. reduces conflict among different communities, B. decreases the possibility of arbitrariness, C. delays decision making, D. accommodates diversities, E. increases instability and divisiveness, F. promotes people’s participation in government, G. undermines the unity of a country?

Model answer

In favour: A, B, D and F — so the correct code is (a). C, E and G are arguments against power sharing.

12.

Important Questions and Answers

Board-style practice

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

Describe the ethnic composition of Belgium and the steps taken by its leaders to accommodate diversity. (5 marks)

Model answer

Belgium is smaller in area than Haryana, with a little over one crore people. 59 per cent live in the Flemish region and speak Dutch, 40 per cent in Wallonia speak French and 1 per cent speak German; in Brussels 80 per cent speak French and 20 per cent Dutch. The French-speaking minority was relatively rich and powerful, which the Dutch-speaking community resented because it benefited from development and education later — producing tensions in the 1950s and 1960s, sharpest in Brussels, where the Dutch were a majority nationally but a minority in the capital. The leaders recognised regional and cultural diversity and amended the constitution four times between 1970 and 1993: Dutch- and French-speaking ministers were made equal in the central government, with some laws requiring a majority from each linguistic group; many central powers were given to the state governments of the two regions, which are not subordinate to the centre; Brussels got a separate government with equal representation; and a community government elected by each language group was given power over cultural, educational and language issues. These arrangements avoided civic strife and a possible division of the country.

2.

How did majoritarianism in Sri Lanka lead to a civil war? (5 marks)

Model answer

Sri Lanka became independent in 1948, and Sinhala leaders sought dominance by virtue of their 74 per cent majority. The elected government adopted a series of majoritarian measures: in 1956 an Act made Sinhala the only official language, disregarding Tamil; preferential policies favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs; and a new constitution stipulated that the state would protect and foster Buddhism. These measures increased the Tamils’ sense of alienation — they felt denied equal political rights, discriminated against in jobs and opportunities, and ignored by the major parties. They launched parties and struggles for Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy and equality of opportunity, but demands for autonomy to Tamil-populated provinces were repeatedly denied. By the 1980s organisations demanded an independent Tamil Eelam, distrust turned into widespread conflict, and it became a civil war in which thousands died, families became refugees and livelihoods were lost. It ended only in 2009, leaving a terrible setback to the country’s social, cultural and economic life.

3.

Explain the prudential and moral reasons for power sharing. (5 marks)

Model answer

Prudential: power sharing reduces the possibility of conflict between social groups, and since social conflict leads to violence and political instability, it ensures the stability of political order. Imposing the majority’s will may look attractive in the short run but in the long run undermines the unity of the nation — tyranny of the majority oppresses the minority and ultimately harms the majority too. Sri Lanka is the proof. Moral: power sharing is the very spirit of democracy. Democratic rule involves sharing power with those affected by its exercise and who must live with its effects; people have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed, and a legitimate government is one in which citizens acquire a stake in the system through participation. Prudential reasons stress better outcomes; moral reasons value the act of sharing itself.

4.

Explain the four forms of power sharing with examples. (5 marks)

Model answer

(i) Horizontal — among the legislature, executive and judiciary, which are placed at the same level and check each other, producing a balance of power; ministers are responsible to Parliament and judges, though appointed by the executive, can check its actions. (ii) Vertical or federal — among governments at different levels, with powers laid down in the constitution; India’s Union, State and local governments, and Belgium’s state governments. (iii) Among social groups — the community government in Belgium, and reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women in India. (iv) Among political parties and pressure groups — competition among parties, alliances and coalition governments, and the influence of traders’, farmers’ and workers’ organisations.

5.

“Sharing of power makes us more powerful.” Explain. (3 marks)

Model answer

Because power that is shared is power that is accepted. Where communities and regions feel they have a stake, they stay within the system rather than fight it — Belgium avoided civic strife and partition, and Brussels became the headquarters of the European Union. Where power is concentrated, as in Sri Lanka, resistance grows, conflict consumes resources and the country is weakened. Sharing therefore strengthens both legitimacy and stability.

6.

What is majoritarianism? How is it different from democracy? (3 marks)

Model answer

Majoritarianism is the belief that the majority community should be able to rule a country in whichever way it wants, disregarding the wishes and needs of the minority. It is not the same as democracy: democracy requires that everyone affected by power has a voice and a share, and that the majority’s decisions respect minority rights. Sri Lanka’s measures were adopted by a democratically elected government, yet they were majoritarian and, in effect, undemocratic in spirit.

7.

Why did the Belgian leaders choose accommodation rather than majority rule? (3 marks)

Model answer

Because they recognised that the country’s regional and cultural differences were real and could not be wished away. They concluded that the unity of the country was possible only by respecting the feelings and interests of different communities and regions — the alternative, forcing the Dutch majority’s will, risked a messy partition in which both sides would claim Brussels.

8.

What is a community government? Why is it important? (3 marks)

Model answer

It is the third kind of government in Belgium, elected by people belonging to one language community — Dutch, French or German-speaking — no matter where they live, and it has power over cultural, educational and language-related issues. It is important because it gives each linguistic community control over what matters most to it, which is the heart of Belgium’s accommodation, and it shows how power can be shared among social groups rather than only among territories.

9.

Give two examples of checks and balances in the Indian system. (2 marks)

Model answer

Ministers and officials exercise power but are answerable to Parliament and the State Assemblies; and judges, though appointed by the executive, can review the actions of the executive and the laws made by the legislature.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick clarifications

1.

Is power sharing the same as federalism?

Model answer

No. Federalism is only one form of power sharing — the vertical division of power among levels of government. Power sharing also takes place among organs of government, among social groups, and among political parties and pressure groups.

2.

Why is Belgium called an example of accommodation?

Model answer

Because instead of majority rule it recognised diversity and built arrangements — equal ministers, devolved powers, equal representation in Brussels, a community government — that let both major communities live together.

3.

Did Sri Lanka have a democratic government when it adopted majoritarian measures?

Model answer

Yes — the measures were adopted by a democratically elected government. That is the chapter’s point: elections alone do not make a system democratic in spirit if the majority disregards the minority.

4.

What was the demand for Tamil Eelam?

Model answer

An independent Tamil state in the northern and eastern parts of Sri Lanka, demanded by political organisations formed in the 1980s after repeated denial of autonomy and language rights.

5.

Is power sharing a sign of weakness?

Model answer

No. The chapter argues the opposite: sharing power makes a country more powerful because it keeps communities within the system and prevents the conflict that weakens a nation.

6.

What does “checks and balances” mean in practice?

Model answer

Each organ of government can limit the others — the legislature can question the executive, the judiciary can review laws and executive action, and the executive implements what the legislature enacts.

7.

Why did Brussels become a special problem in Belgium?

Model answer

Because the Dutch-speaking people were a majority in the country but a minority in the capital, so any arrangement for the capital had to protect both communities at once.

Source 1 — The Belgian model

Source extract

The Belgian leaders took a different path. They recognised the existence of regional differences and cultural diversities. Between 1970 and 1993, they amended their constitution four times so as to work out an arrangement that would enable everyone to live together within the same country. The arrangement they worked out is different from any other country and is very innovative. The constitution prescribes that the number of Dutch and French-speaking ministers shall be equal in the central government. Some special laws require the support of majority of members from each linguistic group. Thus, no single community can make decisions unilaterally. Many powers of the Central Government have been given to State Governments of the two regions of the country. The State Governments are not subordinate to the Central Government.

1.

Which two principles underlie the Belgian arrangement described here?

Model answer

Equality between the two major language communities in the central government, so that neither can decide unilaterally; and genuine devolution, so that the state governments of the two regions have powers of their own and are not subordinate to the centre.

2.

Why does the chapter call the arrangement “innovative”?

Model answer

Because it is unlike any other country’s: it combines equal representation in the central government, a requirement of majority support from each linguistic group for special laws, strong regional governments, equal representation in Brussels and a community government — a package designed for a very specific ethnic arithmetic.

3.

What does “no single community can make decisions unilaterally” mean in practice?

Model answer

That some special laws need the support of a majority of members from each linguistic group, so the French-speaking minority cannot be outvoted by the Dutch-speaking majority on those matters.

Source 2 — The two stories compared

Case study

Both are democracies. Yet, they dealt with the question of power sharing differently. In Belgium, the leaders have realised that the unity of the country is possible only by respecting the feelings and interests of different communities and regions. Such a realisation resulted in mutually acceptable arrangements for sharing power. Sri Lanka shows us a contrasting example. It shows us that if a majority community wants to force its dominance over others and refuses to share power, it can undermine the unity of the country.

1.

What is the single variable that differs between the two cases?

Model answer

The attitude of the majority and its leaders towards sharing power — accommodation in Belgium, majoritarian imposition in Sri Lanka. Both countries were democracies with a large majority community.

2.

What evidence supports the claim that Sri Lanka’s path “undermined the unity of the country”?

Model answer

The alienation of the Tamil community, the demand for an independent Tamil Eelam in the 1980s, and a civil war in which thousands were killed, families became refugees and livelihoods were lost — ending only in 2009.

3.

Which of the two reasons for power sharing does this passage illustrate?

Model answer

The prudential one — that power sharing reduces conflict and protects the unity and stability of the country.

Source 3 — Khalil’s dilemma

Case study

At the end of this civil war, Lebanon’s leaders came together and agreed to some basic rules for power sharing among different communities. As per these rules, the country’s President must belong to the Maronite sect of Catholic Christians. The Prime Minister must be from the Sunni Muslim community. The post of Deputy Prime Minister is fixed for Orthodox Christian sect and that of the Speaker for Shi’a Muslims… When the Christians and Muslims came to this agreement, they were nearly equal in population. Both sides have continued to respect this agreement though now the Muslims are in clear majority. Khalil does not like this system one bit… He cannot understand why Lebanon can’t be like any other ‘normal’ democracy. “Just hold an election, allow everyone to contest and whoever wins maximum votes becomes the president, no matter which community he comes from.”

1.

Why was the Lebanese arrangement created?

Model answer

To end a bitter civil war and guarantee each community a share of power, at a time when Christians and Muslims were nearly equal in population.

2.

What is the weakness Khalil points to?

Model answer

That the system is fixed by community rather than by votes: a popular candidate who belongs to neither community, or whose community is not allotted that post, can never hold it — which offends the democratic idea that anyone should be able to contest and win.

3.

What is the strongest argument of Khalil’s elders?

Model answer

That they have seen the bloodshed of the civil war, and the fixed arrangement has kept the peace — even though the Muslims are now a clear majority. On the chapter’s reasoning, that is a prudential argument for power sharing.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

A country has a 70 per cent majority community and a 30 per cent minority that speaks a different language. The majority offers the minority reserved seats in parliament but refuses to let it run schools in its own language. Is this power sharing? What would you advise?

Model answer

It is partial power sharing: reserved seats give the minority a voice in the legislature, but refusing control over its own language and schools denies it the cultural security that the Belgian community government was designed to provide. On the chapter’s evidence, such a half-measure keeps the grievance alive, because language and education are exactly what minorities feel most strongly about. I would advise devolving control of education and language policy to the minority region or community, alongside the reserved seats, so that the minority has both voice and autonomy. (Model answer.)

2.

“Power sharing delays decisions, and a country at war with itself needs quick decisions.” Evaluate this argument using Sri Lanka.

Model answer

The argument has force in an emergency — quick, unified command can matter in a war. But Sri Lanka’s quick majoritarian decisions did not end conflict; they created it. The 1956 language Act and the preferential policies were decisive and quick, and their consequence was alienation, a demand for a separate state and a civil war that lasted decades and cost far more than any delay in decision-making. Speed without consent produces resistance, which is slower and costlier in the end. The better reading is that power sharing trades a little speed for legitimacy. (Model answer.)

3.

Why might a minority prefer a “community government” to reserved seats in a national legislature?

Model answer

Because reserved seats give representation inside a body where the majority still outnumbers it, whereas a community government gives the minority actual decision-making power over the matters it cares most about — culture, education and language. Voice is not the same as power: one lets you speak, the other lets you decide. (Model answer.)

4.

Does India’s experience support the prudential or the moral case for power sharing? Give two examples.

Model answer

Both. The prudential case: the creation of linguistic states, feared as a step towards disintegration, actually made the country more united and administration easier, and the cautious language policy avoided Sri Lanka’s conflict. The moral case: panchayati raj and the gram sabha let people participate directly in decisions that affect them, giving citizens a stake in the system — which is power sharing as the spirit of democracy. (Model answer.)

5.

A student says: “Belgium’s model is undemocratic because ministers are chosen by language, not merit.” How would you respond?

Model answer

The criticism has weight — fixed community shares can override merit and freeze identities. But the alternative in a deeply divided society is not a neutral meritocracy; it is majority capture of all posts, which is what Sri Lanka experienced. Belgium’s arrangement is a constitutional guarantee that neither community can be excluded, not a rule that individuals cannot rise. The chapter treats it as a workable compromise, and notes that it has kept the peace and even attracted the European Union’s headquarters. (Model answer.)

6.

Identify one situation in your own school or locality where power is shared, and one where it is concentrated. What are the consequences?

Model answer

For example: a class monitor elected monthly, with duties divided among monitors for attendance, cleanliness and sports, shares responsibility and gets more done; a single person deciding everything in a housing society tends to produce grievances and poor information. The chapter’s point applies at every scale: sharing power builds legitimacy, concentrating it invites resistance. (Student answers will vary.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

All power in a democracy does not rest with one organ. Belgium and Sri Lanka, both democracies, show what happens when a country does and does not share power. Belgium’s population is 59 per cent Dutch-speaking, 40 per cent French-speaking and 1 per cent German-speaking, with Brussels 80 per cent French-speaking — so the Dutch were a majority in the country but a minority in the capital. Its leaders recognised regional and cultural diversity and amended the constitution four times between 1970 and 1993: equal numbers of Dutch- and French-speaking ministers with a requirement of majority support from each linguistic group for special laws, real powers for the state governments of the two regions, equal representation in Brussels, and a community government for cultural, educational and language issues. The arrangements avoided civic strife and a possible partition, and Brussels became the headquarters of the European Union.

Sri Lanka took the opposite road. After independence in 1948 the Sinhala majority sought dominance: Sinhala became the only official language in 1956, preferential policies favoured Sinhala applicants for university places and government jobs, and the constitution required the state to protect and foster Buddhism. Tamils felt their language, culture and equal opportunity were denied; their demands for autonomy were refused, and by the 1980s organisations demanded an independent Tamil Eelam. Distrust became a civil war in which thousands died and families became refugees, ending only in 2009.

Two sets of reasons follow. Prudential reasons stress that power sharing reduces conflict, ensures political stability and protects the unity of the country — tyranny of the majority harms the majority too. Moral reasons hold that power sharing is the very spirit of democracy, since people have a right to be consulted on how they are governed and a legitimate government gives citizens a stake. Power sharing takes four forms: among organs of government (horizontal, with checks and balances), among levels of government (vertical or federal), among social groups (community government, reserved constituencies), and among political parties, pressure groups and movements (alliances and coalition governments).
