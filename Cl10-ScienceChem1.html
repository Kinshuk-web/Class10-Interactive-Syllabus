Chemical Reactions & Equations

Explore the fundamental processes where substances transform. Understand how bonds break and form, learn to balance the scales of mass, and discover how these reactions shape our daily world.

1. What is a Chemical Reaction?

This section introduces the core definition of a chemical change. A chemical reaction is a process where reactants transform into products with entirely different properties through the breaking and forming of bonds. Interact with the cards below to see the physical indicators that tell us a reaction has occurred.

🧊

Change in State

Solid to liquid, or liquid to gas.

Example: Burning of wax.
🎨

Change in Colour

Visual transformation of substances.

Example: Rusting of iron (turns reddish-brown).
🫧

Evolution of Gas

Bubbles or fumes released.

Example: Zinc + dilute H2SO4 releasing H2 gas.
🌡️

Change in Temp.

Environment gets hotter or colder.

Example: Adding water to Quick Lime.

2. Balancing Chemical Equations

Equations must be balanced to satisfy the Law of Conservation of Mass (mass can neither be created nor destroyed). The number of atoms of each element must be equal on both the Left Hand Side (Reactants) and Right Hand Side (Products).

3. Types of Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions are classified based on how atoms rearrange. Explore the tabs below to understand the five main types of reactions, their formulas, and real-world examples. The interactive chart provides a visualization of energy changes during these reactions.

➕

Combination Reaction

Two or more reactants combine to form a single product.

A + B → AB

Example 1: Burning of Coal

C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g)

Example 2: Slaked Lime Formation

CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq) + Heat

* This is an Exothermic reaction.

⚡

Decomposition Reaction

A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products. These require energy.

1. Thermal (Heat)

CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

2. Electrolytic (Electricity)

2H2O → 2H2 + O2

3. Photolytic (Sunlight)

2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl2(g)

🔀

Displacement Reaction

A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its salt solution.

A + BC → AC + B

Example: Iron and Copper Sulphate

Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s)

Blue Solution
→ fades to →
Light Green
🔄

Double Displacement

Exchange of ions between two compounds to form new compounds. Often results in a Precipitate (insoluble solid).

AB + CD → AD + CB

Example: Precipitation Reaction

Na2SO4(aq) + BaCl2(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

BaSO4 forms a white precipitate.

⚖️

Redox (Oxidation & Reduction)

Reactions where oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously.

Oxidation

  • Gain of Oxygen
  • Loss of Hydrogen

Reduction

  • Loss of Oxygen
  • Gain of Hydrogen

Example Reaction

CuO + H2 →(Δ) Cu + H2O

Reduced: CuO → Cu
Oxidized: H2 → H2O

4. Effects of Oxidation in Daily Life

Oxidation isn't just a textbook concept; it continuously affects the materials and food around us. Explore the two primary real-world effects: Corrosion of metals and Rancidity in foods. Click on the cards to reveal prevention methods.

Corrosion

Gradual destruction of metals.

🛡️
  • Iron: Rusting (Reddish-brown layer).
  • Silver: Black coating (Silver sulphide).
  • Copper: Green coating (Copper carbonate).

Prevention Methods

Painting Oiling/Greasing Galvanization (Zinc coating)
Click to toggle prevention methods

Rancidity

Oxidation of fats and oils in food.

🍟

When fats and oils are oxidized, they become rancid and their smell and taste change, making food unpalatable and spoiled.

Prevention Methods

  • Adding antioxidants.
  • Flushing packets with Nitrogen gas (inert).
  • Storing in airtight containers.
  • Refrigeration.
