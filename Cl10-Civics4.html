Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

Political parties are easily one of the most visible institutions in a democracy. For most ordinary citizens, democracy is equal to political parties — people who know nothing about the Constitution may still know something about the parties. But visibility does not mean popularity: most people are very critical of parties and blame them for all that is wrong with our democracy, because parties have become identified with social and political divisions.

About a hundred years ago few countries had political parties; now few do not. A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in the government, agreeing on some policies and programmes for society with a view to promoting the collective good. A party has three components — leaders, active members and followers — and performs seven functions, from contesting elections to shaping public opinion and giving people access to government machinery.

The chapter then asks how many parties a democracy should have: one-party, two-party (USA, UK) or multiparty (India) systems. The answer is that a party system is not chosen — it evolves with a society’s divisions, history and electoral system. India has more than 750 registered parties and, per the Election Commission’s 2023 notification, six recognised national parties, along with many State parties whose rise has made coalitions normal since 1996.

Finally come the four challenges — lack of internal democracy, dynastic succession, money and muscle power, and the absence of meaningful choice — and the reforms: the anti-defection law, the Supreme Court’s affidavit order, the Election Commission’s orders, and suggestions for a law on internal affairs, one-third tickets to women and state funding of elections. Legal fixes alone are not enough: pressure from citizens and joining parties matter more.

Why Do We Need Political Parties?

Political parties are easily one of the most visible institutions in a democracy. For most ordinary citizens, democracy is equal to political parties — in remote parts of the country you may meet people who know nothing about the Constitution but who know something about the parties. Yet this visibility does not mean popularity: most people are very critical of parties, blaming them for all that is wrong with our democracy and political life, because parties have become identified with social and political divisions.

About a hundred years ago there were few countries with any political party; now there are few without them. Why did parties become so omnipresent in democracies all over the world?

Meaning

Definition

A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in the government. They agree on some policies and programmes for the society with a view to promote the collective good. Since there can be different views on what is good for all, parties try to persuade people why their policies are better than others, and seek to implement them by winning popular support through elections.

Thus parties reflect fundamental political divisions in a society. Parties are about a part of the society and thus involve partisanship — a party is known by which part it stands for, which policies it supports and whose interests it upholds. A political party has three components: the leaders, the active members and the followers.

Functions of Political Parties

Basically, political parties fill political offices and exercise political power. Parties do so by performing a series of functions:

  1. Parties contest elections. In most democracies elections are fought mainly among candidates put up by parties, which select their candidates in different ways — in the USA members and supporters of a party choose its candidates (a method more and more countries are following); in India top party leaders choose candidates.
  2. Parties put forward different policies and programmes and voters choose from them. No government can handle the vast variety of views, so a large number of similar opinions must be grouped together to give direction to policy — a party reduces a vast multitude of opinions into a few basic positions, and a government is expected to base its policies on the line taken by the ruling party.
  3. Parties play a decisive role in making laws. Formally laws are debated and passed in the legislature, but since most members belong to a party, they go by the direction of the party leadership, irrespective of personal opinions.
  4. Parties form and run governments. Big policy decisions are taken by the political executive that comes from parties; parties recruit leaders, train them and make them ministers to run the government the way they want.
  5. Those that lose elections play the role of opposition, voicing different views and criticising the government for its failures or wrong policies, and mobilising opposition to the government.
  6. Parties shape public opinion. They raise and highlight issues; with lakhs of members and activists spread across the country, and with many pressure groups being extensions of parties, opinions in society often crystallise on the lines parties take. Parties sometimes also launch movements for the resolution of problems faced by people.
  7. Parties provide people access to government machinery and welfare schemes. For an ordinary citizen it is easier to approach a local party leader than a government officer, which is why people feel close to parties even when they do not fully trust them.

Parties have to be responsive to people’s needs and demands, otherwise people can reject them in the next elections.

Necessity

This list of functions answers the question: we need parties because they perform these functions. But why can modern democracies not exist without parties? Imagine a situation without parties. Every candidate would be independent, so no one could make promises to the people about major policy changes; a government might be formed, but its utility would remain ever uncertain. Elected representatives would be accountable to their constituency for what they do in the locality, but no one would be responsible for how the country is run.

Look also at the non-party based elections to panchayats in many States: although parties do not contest formally, the village generally splits into more than one faction, each putting up a “panel” of its candidates — which is exactly what a party does. That is why we find political parties in almost all countries, big or small, old or new, developed or developing. The rise of political parties is directly linked to the emergence of representative democracies: as societies became large and complex they needed an agency to gather different views and present them to government, to bring representatives together so that a responsible government could be formed, and to support or restrain the government, make policies and justify or oppose them. Parties are a necessary condition for a democracy.

How Many Parties Should We Have?

In a democracy any group of citizens is free to form a political party. In this formal sense there are a large number of parties in each country — more than 750 parties are registered with the Election Commission of India — but not all are serious contenders; usually only a handful are effectively in the race to win elections and form the government.

The three party systems
SystemWhat it meansExamplesAssessment
One-partyOnly one party is allowed to control and run the governmentChina, and formerly many communist statesNot a democratic option: legally people may form parties, but the electoral system does not permit free competition for power
Two-partyPower usually changes between two main parties; several other parties may exist, contest and win a few seats, but only the two have a serious chance of winning a majorityUnited States of America, United KingdomGives voters a clear choice of government, but excludes many views
MultipartySeveral parties compete and more than two have a reasonable chance of coming to power on their own or in alliance; government is usually formed by a coalitionIndiaOften appears messy and can lead to political instability, but allows a variety of interests and opinions to enjoy representation

When several parties in a multiparty system join hands for the purpose of contesting elections and winning power, it is called an alliance or a front — for example, in India there were three such major alliances in the 2004 parliamentary elections: the National Democratic Alliance, the United Progressive Alliance and the Left Front.

So which is better? Perhaps the best answer is that this is not a very good question. Party system is not something any country can choose — it evolves over a long time, depending on the nature of society, its social and regional divisions, its history of politics and its system of elections, and these cannot be changed very quickly. Each country develops a party system conditioned by its special circumstances: if India has evolved a multiparty system, it is because the social and geographical diversity of so large a country is not easily absorbed by two or even three parties. No system is ideal for all countries and all situations.

Popular Participation in Political Parties

It is often said that political parties are facing a crisis because they are very unpopular and citizens are indifferent to them. The available evidence shows that this belief is only partly true for India. Based on a series of large sample surveys conducted over several decades:

  • Political parties do not enjoy much trust among people in South Asia: the proportion saying their trust is “not much” or “not at all” is greater than those with “some” or “great” trust.
  • The same is true of most other democracies — parties are among the least trusted institutions all over the world.
  • Yet participation in the activities of parties was fairly high: the proportion saying they were members of a party was higher in India than in many advanced countries like Canada, Japan, Spain and South Korea.
  • Over the last three decades the proportion reporting membership of a political party in India has gone up steadily.
  • The proportion saying they feel “close to a political party” has also gone up in this period.
National Parties and State Parties

Democracies that follow a federal system tend to have two kinds of parties: parties present in only one of the federal units, and parties present in several or all units. India is no exception: there are country-wide parties, called “national parties”, which have units in various States that by and large follow the same policies, programmes and strategy decided at the national level.

Every party has to register with the Election Commission. While the Commission treats all parties equally, it offers special facilities to large and established parties: they are given a unique symbol that only their official candidates may use. Parties that get this privilege and other facilities are “recognised” by the Commission.

State parties

Other than these, most of the major parties are classified by the Election Commission as “State parties”, commonly referred to as regional parties — though they need not be regional in ideology or outlook. Some are all-India parties that have succeeded only in some States: the Samajwadi Party and Rashtriya Janata Dal have national-level organisation with units in several States. Some, like the Biju Janata Dal, Sikkim Democratic Front, Mizo National Front and Telangana Rashtra Samithi, are conscious of their State identity.

Over the last three decades the number and strength of these parties has expanded, making Parliament politically more diverse. No national party was able to secure a majority in the Lok Sabha on its own until 2014, so national parties were compelled to form alliances with State parties. Since 1996 nearly every State party has had an opportunity to be part of one or another national-level coalition government, which has contributed to the strengthening of federalism and democracy in our country.

Challenges to Political Parties

Since parties are the most visible face of democracy, it is natural that people blame them for whatever is wrong with the working of democracy. All over the world people express strong dissatisfaction with the failure of parties to perform their functions well, and popular criticism has focused on four problem areas.

  1. Lack of internal democracy. All over the world there is a tendency towards the concentration of power in one or a few leaders at the top. Parties do not keep membership registers, do not hold organisational meetings and do not conduct internal elections regularly; ordinary members do not get sufficient information about what happens inside the party, and do not have the means or connections to influence decisions. Leaders therefore assume greater power to make decisions in the name of the party, and those who disagree find it difficult to continue — personal loyalty to the leader becomes more important than loyalty to party principles and policies.
  2. Dynastic succession. Since most parties do not practise open and transparent procedures, there are very few ways for an ordinary worker to rise to the top. Those who happen to be leaders are in a position of unfair advantage to favour people close to them or even family members; in many parties the top positions are always controlled by members of one family. This is unfair to other members, and bad for democracy, since people without adequate experience or popular support come to occupy positions of power. The tendency is present in some measure all over the world, including in older democracies.
  3. Growing role of money and muscle power, especially during elections. Since parties are focused only on winning, they take short-cuts: they nominate candidates who have or can raise lots of money; rich people and companies that fund parties tend to influence their policies and decisions; and in some cases parties support criminals who can win elections. Democrats all over the world are worried about the increasing role of rich people and big companies in democratic politics.
  4. Parties do not seem to offer a meaningful choice. To offer meaningful choice, parties must be significantly different, but in recent years there has been a decline in ideological differences among parties in most parts of the world — for example the difference between the Labour and Conservative parties in Britain is very little; they agree on fundamental aspects and differ only in details. In India too the differences among major parties on economic policy have reduced, and sometimes people cannot even elect very different leaders, because the same set of leaders keep shifting from one party to another.
How Can Parties Be Reformed?

In order to face these challenges, political parties need to be reformed. But are parties willing to reform? If they are not, is it possible to force them? This is not a simple question: in a democracy the final decision is made by leaders who represent political parties, and people can replace them only by another set of party leaders.

Efforts already made in India

  • Anti-defection law. The Constitution was amended to prevent elected MLAs and MPs from changing parties, because many elected representatives were indulging in defection to become ministers or for cash rewards. The law now says that if an MLA or MP changes parties, he or she will lose the seat in the legislature. This has brought defection down — but it has also made dissent even more difficult, since MPs and MLAs must accept whatever party leaders decide.
  • Supreme Court order on affidavits. The Court passed an order to reduce the influence of money and criminals: it is now mandatory for every candidate to file an affidavit giving details of property and criminal cases pending. This has made a lot of information available to the public, but there is no system to check whether the information is true, and it is not yet clear whether it has reduced the influence of the rich and criminals.
  • Election Commission order. The Commission made it necessary for parties to hold organisational elections and file income tax returns. Parties have started doing so, but sometimes it is a mere formality, and it is not clear that this has led to greater internal democracy.

Suggestions often made

  • A law to regulate the internal affairs of parties: compulsory maintenance of a register of members, following the party’s own constitution, an independent authority to judge party disputes, and open elections to the highest posts.
  • Making it mandatory to give about one-third of tickets to women candidates, with a similar quota for women in the decision-making bodies of the party.
  • State funding of elections: the government should give parties money for election expenses, in kind (petrol, paper, telephone) or in cash on the basis of votes secured in the last election.

These suggestions have not yet been accepted by political parties. But we must be very careful about legal solutions to political problems: over-regulation of parties can be counter-productive, forcing all parties to find ways to cheat the law, and parties will not agree to pass a law they do not like.

Two other ways

  1. People can put pressure on political parties — through petitions, publicity and agitations. Ordinary citizens, pressure groups and movements and the media can play an important role; if parties feel they would lose public support by not reforming, they will become more serious about it.
  2. Political parties can improve if those who want this join political parties. The quality of democracy depends on the degree of public participation; it is difficult to reform politics if ordinary citizens do not take part in it and simply criticise it from outside. The problem of bad politics can be solved by more and better politics.

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

Categorise the photographs of wall writing by the functions of political parties they illustrate.

Model answer

Wall writing is mainly about contesting elections and shaping public opinion — it advertises the party and its symbol before a vote, and tries to persuade voters. Some slogans also illustrate putting forward policies and programmes, when they make a promise or claim. (Student activity.)

2.

Okay, granted that we cannot live without political parties. But on what grounds do people support a political party?

Model answer

Broadly on the party’s policies and programmes and the direction it gives to government; on its ideology and the interests it is seen to uphold; on the record of its government and its leaders’ popularity; on the social groups and regions it represents; and, for many voters, on long-standing family and community loyalties — which is why the chapter says voters’ attachment to parties is often stronger than their attachment to caste or community.

3.

So, you agree with me. Parties are partial, partisan and lead to partitions. Parties do nothing but divide people.

Model answer

No — partisanship is not the same as dividing people. Because society has genuine differences of opinion and interest, someone must group those opinions into programmes; that is what parties do. Without them, every candidate would be independent, no one could promise policy change, and no one would be responsible for how the country is run. Parties can become identified with divisions, which is why people criticise them — but the remedy is better parties, not no parties.

4.

How many major or effective parties are good for a democracy?

Model answer

There is no single answer. A one-party system is not democratic; a two-party system gives a clear choice but excludes many views; a multiparty system represents diversity but can look messy and produce instability. The chapter’s conclusion is that a party system is not chosen but evolves with a society’s divisions, history and electoral system — no system is ideal for all countries.

5.

I wonder how politicians manage these coalitions. I can’t even remember the names of all the parties.

Model answer

Through alliances built before and after elections, a common minimum programme, and the distribution of ministries and policy concessions. In India, three major alliances contested in 2004 — the NDA, the UPA and the Left Front — and since 1996 nearly every State party has been part of a national coalition. Managing them requires constant negotiation, which is precisely the “new culture of power sharing” the previous chapter described.

6.

Can you find the names of at least two States for each type of party system at the State level — two-party, multiparty with two alliances, and multiparty?

Model answer

Two-party: Kerala and Tamil Nadu, where two fronts have alternated for decades. Multiparty with two alliances: Maharashtra and Bihar, where blocs form around two poles. Multiparty: West Bengal, Odisha and Assam, where three or more parties have had a real chance at different times. (Answers may vary by period.)

7.

Does the cartoon reflect the data graphics on popular participation in parties?

Model answer

Partly not. The cartoon suggests citizens are indifferent and parties unpopular, and it is true that parties are among the least trusted institutions. But the data show participation is fairly high and rising in India — membership above the global average and growing over three decades. Distrust and participation coexist: people join and vote while criticising parties.

8.

Why don’t parties give enough tickets to women? Is that also due to lack of internal democracy?

Model answer

Largely yes. When a few leaders at the top decide tickets without transparent procedures, they tend to choose candidates from their own networks, and those networks are overwhelmingly male. That is why the suggested reform is a mandatory quota of about one-third of tickets for women, along with a quota in the party’s decision-making bodies.

9.

Can you identify which challenges are highlighted in the cartoons on pages 57 to 59? What are the ways to curb the misuse of money and muscle power in politics?

Model answer

The cartoons highlight the role of money and muscle power — a businessman-politician owning media and a football club, and corporate money controlling institutions. Ways to curb it: mandatory affidavits (already ordered by the Supreme Court), state funding of elections, stricter disclosure and audit of party funds, curbs on corporate donations, faster trial of criminal cases against candidates and disqualification on conviction.

10.

Does this suggest that in democracies people contest elections only to make money? But isn’t it true that there are politicians committed to the well-being of the people?

Model answer

Both are true, and the chapter holds them together. The role of money has grown and some candidates are chosen for their wealth, but many politicians are committed to public well-being — the chapter’s own argument is that the problem of bad politics can be solved by more and better politics, that is, by more committed people joining parties.

11.

Do you agree that this form of reforming political parties will be acceptable to them? (On state funding and regulation)

Model answer

Not automatically. Parties will resist a law that regulates their internal affairs or caps their spending, because the final decision on such a law rests with party leaders themselves. That is why the chapter warns that legal solutions must be carefully designed, that over-regulation can be counter-productive, and that pressure from citizens and media — not law alone — is what makes parties reform.

1.
NCERT exercise5 marks

State the various functions political parties perform in a democracy.

Model answer

(i) They contest elections, selecting candidates — by members in the USA, by top leaders in India. (ii) They put forward different policies and programmes, reducing a multitude of opinions to a few basic positions. (iii) They play a decisive role in making laws, since members follow party direction. (iv) They form and run governments, recruiting and training leaders as ministers. 2.
NCERT exercise5 marks

What are the various challenges faced by political parties?

Model answer

Lack of internal democracy — power concentrates in one or a few leaders, membership registers are not kept, meetings and internal elections are irregular, and ordinary members lack information and influence. Dynastic succession — top positions tend to stay with one family, giving unfair advantage and bringing inexperienced people to power. Money and muscle power — candidates are chosen for their ability to raise funds, donors influence policy, and parties sometimes back criminals. No meaningful choice — ideological differences among parties have declined, so voters cannot choose really different policies or leaders.

Important Questions and Answers

Board-style practice

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

Why do we need political parties in a democracy? (5 marks)

Model answer

Because parties perform functions no other institution performs: they contest elections and select candidates; they group a vast multitude of opinions into a few policy positions; they make laws, since members follow party direction; they form and run governments and train leaders; they provide opposition that criticises and restrains the government; they shape public opinion by raising issues and running movements; and they give ordinary citizens access to government machinery and welfare schemes. Imagine a situation without parties: every candidate would be independent, no one could promise major policy change, and although representatives would be accountable to their locality, no one would be responsible for how the country is run. Even non-party panchayat elections produce “panels” that behave like parties. Parties are therefore a necessary condition for democracy, and their rise is directly linked to the rise of representative democracy.

2.

Compare one-party, two-party and multiparty systems. Which is best? (5 marks)

Model answer

In a one-party system only one party may control and run the government; it is not a democratic option, because although people are legally free to form parties the electoral system does not permit free competition for power. In a two-party system power usually changes between two main parties while others exist and win a few seats — as in the USA and UK; it offers a clear choice of government but excludes many views. In a multiparty system several parties compete and more than two have a real chance of power, usually through coalitions — as in India; it can look messy and produce instability, but it allows a variety of interests and opinions to be represented. No system is best: a party system is not chosen but evolves with the nature of society, its social and regional divisions, its political history and its electoral system — which is why India’s diversity produced a multiparty system.

3.

What are the criteria for recognition as a State party and a national party? (3 marks)

Model answer

A party that secures at least 6 per cent of the total votes in an election to the Legislative Assembly of a State and wins at least two seats is recognised as a State party. A party that secures at least 6 per cent of the total votes in Lok Sabha elections, or in Assembly elections in four States, and wins at least four seats in the Lok Sabha is recognised as a national party. Recognised parties get a unique election symbol and other facilities.

4.

Describe the four challenges faced by political parties. (5 marks)

Model answer

Lack of internal democracy: power concentrates in a few leaders; parties do not keep membership registers, hold organisational meetings or conduct internal elections regularly; ordinary members lack information and influence; personal loyalty to the leader replaces loyalty to principles. Dynastic succession: without open procedures an ordinary worker cannot rise, so leaders favour relatives and in many parties top posts stay with one family, bringing inexperienced people to power. Money and muscle power: parties focus only on winning and nominate those who can raise money; donors influence policy; some parties back criminals who can win. No meaningful choice: ideological differences have declined — Labour and Conservative in Britain differ only in details, Indian parties differ little on economic policy — and the same leaders keep shifting parties, so voters cannot choose very different policies or people.

5.

What efforts have been made in India to reform political parties, and how far have they succeeded? (5 marks)

Model answer

Anti-defection law: the Constitution was amended so that an MLA or MP who changes parties loses the seat — defection has come down, but dissent has become harder because members must accept what party leaders decide. Supreme Court order: every candidate must file an affidavit disclosing property and pending criminal cases — much information is now public, but there is no system to verify it and no clear evidence that the influence of money and criminals has declined. Election Commission order: parties must hold organisational elections and file income tax returns — they now do so, but often as a formality, with no clear gain in internal democracy.

6.

What reforms have been suggested for political parties? Why have they not been adopted? (5 marks)

Model answer

Suggested: a law regulating parties’ internal affairs (compulsory membership register, following the party’s own constitution, an independent authority for disputes, open elections to the highest posts); a mandatory minimum of about one-third of tickets for women, with a quota in decision-making bodies; and state funding of elections, in kind (petrol, paper, telephone) or cash based on votes secured. They have not been adopted because the final decision on such laws rests with party leaders themselves, who would be regulated by them. The chapter warns that over-regulation can be counter-productive, forcing parties to find ways to cheat the law.

7.

“The problem of bad politics can be solved by more and better politics.” Explain. (3 marks)

Model answer

Because reform cannot come only from outside. Citizens who simply criticise parties leave the field to those already in them; if people who want reform join parties, contest internal elections and put pressure through petitions, publicity, movements and the media, parties will change because they fear losing public support. The quality of democracy depends on the degree of public participation.

8.

What is defection? How has the law dealt with it, and what is the cost? (3 marks)

Model answer

Defection is changing party allegiance from the party on which a person was elected to a different party. The Constitution was amended so that an MLA or MP who defects loses the seat, which reduced defection — but the cost is that it has made dissent within parties even more difficult, since members must accept whatever party leaders decide.

9.

Why has the number of State parties grown, and what has been its effect? (3 marks)

Model answer

Over the last three decades the number and strength of State parties has expanded, making Parliament more politically diverse. No national party won a Lok Sabha majority on its own until 2014, so national parties had to ally with State parties, and since 1996 nearly every State party has been part of a national coalition — which has strengthened federalism and democracy.

10.

Distinguish between a national party and a State party with examples. (3 marks)

Model answer

A national party has units in various States following policies decided at the national level and meets the higher recognition criteria — for example the INC, BJP, BSP, CPI-M, AAP and NPP. A State party is recognised within a State on the lower criteria and is commonly called a regional party — for example the Samajwadi Party, Rashtriya Janata Dal, Biju Janata Dal, Sikkim Democratic Front, Mizo National Front and Telangana Rashtra Samithi — though such parties need not be regional in ideology.

Source 1 — Why parties are necessary

Source extract

We can understand the necessity of political parties by imagining a situation without parties. Every candidate in the elections will be independent. So no one will be able to make any promises to the people about any major policy changes. The government may be formed, but its utility will remain ever uncertain. Elected representatives will be accountable to their constituency for what they do in the locality. But no one will be responsible for how the country will be run. We can also think about it by looking at the non-party based elections to the panchayat in many states. Although, the parties do not contest formally, it is generally noticed that the village gets split into more than one faction, each of which puts up a ‘panel’ of its candidates. This is exactly what the party does.

1.

What three problems arise in a democracy without parties?

Model answer

No one can promise major policy changes; the government’s utility remains uncertain; and although representatives are accountable to their locality, no one is responsible for how the country is run.

2.

What does the panchayat example show?

Model answer

That even where parties do not contest formally, factions put up panels of candidates — which is exactly what a party does. The party function is inherent in representative politics, not an artificial addition.

3.

What conclusion does the chapter draw?

Model answer

That parties are a necessary condition for democracy, and their rise is directly linked to the emergence of representative democracy in large, complex societies.

Source extract

The first challenge is lack of internal democracy within parties… Parties do not keep membership registers, do not hold organisational meetings, and do not conduct internal elections regularly. Ordinary members of the party do not get sufficient information on what happens inside the party… The second challenge of dynastic succession is related to the first one… In many parties, the top positions are always controlled by members of one family. Because there are no open and transparent procedures, an ordinary worker has no route to the top, so leaders can favour relatives — which is how dynastic succession follows from the lack of internal democracy.

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

If parties are so unpopular, why do people keep joining them?

Model answer

Because distrust of an institution is not the same as indifference to politics. Parties are the only available route to power, to a say in who governs and to access to the state; membership in India (11.4 per cent) is above the global average (9.1) and has risen over three decades. People criticise parties in general and participate in particular — the chapter treats this as evidence that the crisis of parties is real but partial. (Model answer.)

2.

Would a law banning criminal candidates solve the problem of money and muscle power?

Model answer

Only partly. A ban is necessary, but the deeper problem is that parties select candidates for their ability to raise funds and win, so the incentive to field tainted but winnable candidates remains. Effective reform combines disclosure (affidavits), verification of the disclosures, speedy trial and disqualification on conviction, state funding of elections to reduce dependence on donors, and internal party democracy so that candidates are not chosen by a few leaders. (Model answer.)

3.

Why does India have a multiparty system while the USA has two parties?

Model answer

Because a party system evolves with a society’s divisions, history and electoral rules. India’s social and geographical diversity is not easily absorbed by two or even three parties, and its first-past-the-post, multi-member-diversity politics rewards regional formations that can then bargain in coalitions. The USA’s nationalised, two-pole competition and its electoral rules squeeze out third parties. Neither is a choice; both are outcomes. (Model answer.)

4.

“The anti-defection law strengthened parties and weakened democracy.” Discuss.

Model answer

It strengthened parties by making the legislator’s mandate belong to the party that won it, ending the spectacle of wholesale floor-crossing for ministerial posts. But it weakened the legislator’s independence: an MP who disagrees with the leadership risks disqualification, so dissent — which is the substance of deliberation — is suppressed, and party leaders gain more control. A defensible answer distinguishes defection (changing sides for reward) from dissent (disagreeing while staying), and suggests protecting the second while punishing the first. (Model answer.)

5.

Should India move to state funding of elections? What would it change, and what could go wrong?

Model answer

It would reduce dependence on rich donors and companies, lower the advantage of wealthy candidates and make the cost of contesting less of a barrier — which matters especially for women and first-time candidates. But it could cost the exchequer heavily, be captured by established parties that design the formula, and fail if parties continue to raise undeclared funds alongside. It works best combined with spending caps, audit and disclosure. (Model answer.)

6.

Why does the chapter end with “the problem of bad politics can be solved by more and better politics” rather than with a list of laws?

Model answer

Because laws regulating parties must be passed by the very leaders they would regulate, so legal reform alone will always be partial; and over-regulation pushes parties to evade rather than change. The chapter therefore locates the remedy in participation: citizens, media, movements and members pressing parties from within and outside, so that reform becomes the price of public support. It is a claim about where power really lies in a democracy. (Model answer.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

Political parties are the most visible institutions of a democracy, though not the most trusted. A party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in the government, agreeing on policies and programmes for the collective good; it has leaders, active members and followers, and it reflects the fundamental political divisions of a society. Parties perform seven functions — contesting elections, putting forward policies, making laws, forming and running governments, playing the role of opposition, shaping public opinion, and giving people access to government. Without them, every candidate would be independent, no one could promise policy change, and no one would be responsible for how the country is run. Parties are therefore a necessary condition for democracy.

Party systems are not chosen but evolve: one-party systems are not democratic; two-party systems (USA, UK) offer a clear choice; multiparty systems (India) represent diversity through coalitions and alliances such as the NDA, UPA and Left Front in 2004. India has more than 750 registered parties and, per the Election Commission’s 2023 notification, six recognised national parties, alongside many State parties whose growth has made coalition politics normal since 1996 and strengthened federalism.

Parties face four challenges: lack of internal democracy, dynastic succession, the growing role of money and muscle power, and the absence of meaningful choice between them. Reforms attempted include the anti-defection law, the Supreme Court’s order making affidavits mandatory, and the Election Commission’s requirement of organisational elections and income tax returns — each useful but partial, and sometimes with side effects. Suggested reforms — a law regulating parties’ internal affairs, one-third of tickets for women, and state funding of elections — have not been accepted. The chapter ends by arguing that legal solutions must be handled carefully, and that the strongest forces for reform are public pressure and the participation of citizens who join parties: the problem of bad politics can be solved by more and better politics.
