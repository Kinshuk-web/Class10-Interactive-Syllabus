Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

We participate in the market both as producers and consumers. As producers we may work in agriculture, industry or services; as consumers we participate when we purchase goods and services that we need — the final goods that people as consumers use.

In the preceding chapters we discussed the need for rules and regulations that promote development — protecting workers in the unorganised sector, protecting borrowers from high interest rates, protecting the environment. Similarly, rules and regulations are required for the protection of consumers in the marketplace, because an individual consumer often finds herself in a weak position: when there is a complaint, the seller tries to shift all responsibility on to the buyer — “If you didn’t like what you bought, please go elsewhere”.

Exploitation takes many forms: weighing less than what one should, adding charges that were not mentioned, selling adulterated or defective goods. Markets do not work fairly when producers are few and powerful while consumers purchase in small amounts and are scattered; such companies can manipulate the market and pass on false information through the media — as with powdered milk sold as better than mother’s milk, or cigarettes whose link with cancer took years of court cases to be accepted.

The consumer movement arose out of this dissatisfaction. In India it began in an organised form in the 1960s against food shortages, hoarding, black marketing and adulteration, and led to the Consumer Protection Act 1986 (COPRA), amended in 2019. The chapter then sets out the rights — safety, information, choice, seeking redressal, representation and consumer education — with the case studies of Reji Mathew, Amritha, Abirami and Prakash, and closes with quality marks and the unfinished work of the movement.

Consumer

One who participates in the market to purchase final goods and services for personal use.

Consumer movement

The organised effort of consumers against unethical and unfair trade practices, which shifted the responsibility for quality from buyers to sellers.

COPRA

The Consumer Protection Act 1986, amended in 2019; it gives consumers the right to redressal through a three-tier machinery.

District Commission

District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission — deals with claims up to Rs 1 crore.

State Commission

Deals with claims between Rs 1 crore and Rs 10 crore; also hears appeals against District Commission orders.

National Commission

Deals with claims exceeding Rs 10 crore; sits in New Delhi.

MRP

Maximum Retail Price — the highest price printed on a packet; consumers may bargain for less.

Quality marks

ISI, Agmark, Hallmark and +F — logos that assure quality; certification is mandatory for some products such as LPG cylinders, food colours and additives, cement and packaged drinking water.

National Consumers’ Day

24 December — the day the Indian Parliament enacted the Consumer Protection Act in 1986.

The Consumer in the Marketplace

We participate in the market both as producers and consumers. As producers of goods and services we could be working in any of the sectors discussed earlier — agriculture, industry or services. Consumers participate in the market when they purchase goods and services that they need. These are the final goods that people as consumers use.

In the preceding chapters we discussed the need for rules and regulations or steps that would promote development — for the protection of workers in the unorganised sector, to protect people from high interest rates charged by moneylenders in the informal sector, and for protecting the environment. For example, moneylenders adopt various tricks to bind the borrower: they could make the producer sell the produce to them at a low rate in return for a timely loan, or force a small farmer like Swapna to sell her land to pay back the loan. Similarly, many people who work in the unorganised sector have to work at low wages and accept conditions that are not fair and are often harmful to their health. To prevent such exploitation we have talked of rules and regulations for their protection, and there are organisations that have struggled for long to ensure that these rules are followed.

Likewise, rules and regulations are required for the protection of the consumers in the marketplace. Individual consumers often find themselves in a weak position. Whenever there is a complaint regarding a good or service that had been bought, the seller tries to shift all the responsibility on to the buyer. Their position usually is — “If you didn’t like what you bought, please go elsewhere.” As if the seller has no responsibility once a sale is completed! The consumer movement, as we shall discuss later, is an effort to change this situation.

How Consumers Are Exploited

Exploitation in the marketplace happens in various ways. For example, sometimes traders indulge in unfair trade practices — when shopkeepers weigh less than what they should, or when traders add charges that were not mentioned before, or when adulterated or defective goods are sold.

Markets do not work in a fair manner when producers are few and powerful whereas consumers purchase in small amounts and are scattered. This happens especially when large companies are producing these goods. These companies with huge wealth, power and reach can manipulate the market in various ways. At times false information is passed on through the media and other sources to attract consumers.

Source extract

Two examples of false claims: a company for years sold powdered milk for babies all over the world as the most scientific product, claiming it to be better than mother’s milk. It took years of struggle before the company was forced to accept that it had been making false claims. Similarly, a long battle had to be fought with court cases to make cigarette-manufacturing companies accept that their product could cause cancer.

Hence there is a need for rules and regulations to ensure protection for consumers.

The Consumer Movement

The consumer movement arose out of the dissatisfaction of consumers, as many unfair practices were being indulged in by sellers. There was no legal system available to consumers to protect them from exploitation in the marketplace. For a long time, when a consumer was not happy with a particular brand or shop, he or she generally avoided buying that brand or would stop purchasing from that shop. It was presumed that it was the responsibility of consumers to be careful while buying a commodity or service.

It took many years for organisations in India, and around the world, to create awareness amongst people. This has also shifted the responsibility of ensuring the quality of goods and services on to the sellers.

In India, the consumer movement as a “social force” originated with the necessity of protecting and promoting the interests of consumers against unethical and unfair trade practices. Rampant food shortages, hoarding, black marketing, adulteration of food and edible oil gave birth to the consumer movement in an organised form in the 1960s. Till the 1970s, consumer organisations were largely engaged in writing articles and holding exhibitions; they formed consumer groups to look into the malpractices in ration shops and overcrowding in road passenger transport. More recently, India has witnessed an upsurge in the number of consumer groups.

Consumers International: in 1985 the United Nations adopted the UN Guidelines for Consumer Protection. This was a tool for nations to adopt measures to protect consumers and for consumer advocacy groups to press their governments to do so. At the international level this has become the foundation for the consumer movement. Today, Consumers International has become an umbrella body to over 200 member organisations from over 100 countries.

Because of all these efforts, the movement succeeded in bringing pressure on business firms as well as government to correct business conduct which may be unfair and against the interests of consumers at large. A major step taken in 1986 by the Indian government was the enactment of the Consumer Protection Act 1986, popularly known as COPRA. This Act was amended in 2019.

Right to Safety

While using many goods and services, we as consumers have the right to be protected against the marketing of goods and delivery of services that are hazardous to life and property. Producers need to strictly follow the required safety rules and regulations.

There are many goods and services that we purchase which require special attention to safety. For example, pressure cookers have a safety valve which, if it is defective, can cause a serious accident. The manufacturers of the safety valve have to ensure high quality. You also need public or government action to see that this quality is maintained. However, we do find bad quality products in the market because the supervision of these rules is weak and the consumer movement is also not strong enough.

Case study

Reji’s suffering: Reji Mathew, a healthy boy studying in Class IX, was admitted in a private clinic in Kerala for removal of tonsils. An ENT surgeon performed the tonsillectomy operation under general anaesthesia. As a result of improper anaesthesia Reji showed symptoms of some brain abnormalities because of which he was crippled for life. His father filed a complaint in the State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission claiming compensation of Rs 5,00,000 for medical negligence and deficiency in service. The State Commission, saying that the evidence was not sufficient, dismissed it. Reji’s father appealed again in the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission located in New Delhi. The National Commission, after looking into the complaint, held the hospital responsible for medical negligence and directed it to pay the compensation.

Right to Information

When you buy any commodity, you will find certain details given on the packing. These details are about ingredients used, price, batch number, date of manufacture, expiry date and the address of the manufacturer. When we buy medicines, on the packets you might find “directions for proper use” and information relating to side effects and risks associated with usage of that medicine. When you buy garments, you will find information on “instructions for washing”.

Why is it that rules have been made so that the manufacturer displays this information? Because consumers have the right to be informed about the particulars of goods and services that they purchase. Consumers can then complain and ask for compensation or replacement if the product proves to be defective in any manner.

For example, if we buy a product and find it defective well within the expiry period, we can ask for a replacement. If the expiry period was not printed, the manufacturer would blame the shopkeeper and would not accept responsibility. If people sell medicines that have expired, severe action can be taken against them. Similarly, one can protest and complain if someone sells a good at more than the printed price on the packet. This is indicated by MRP — maximum retail price. In fact consumers can bargain with the seller to sell at less than the MRP.

In recent times the right to information has been expanded to cover various services provided by the government. In October 2005 the Government of India enacted a law, popularly known as the RTI (Right to Information) Act, which ensures its citizens all the information about the functions of government departments.

Case study

Amritha’s case: Amritha, an engineering graduate, after submitting all the certificates and attending the interview for a job in a government department, did not receive any news of the result. The officials also refused to comply with her queries. She therefore filed an application using the RTI Act, saying that it was her right to know the result in a reasonable time so that she could plan her future. She was not only informed about the reasons for delay in the declaration of results but also got her call letter for appointment, as she performed well in the interview.

Right to Choose

Any consumer who receives a service in whatever capacity, regardless of age, gender and nature of service, has the right to choose whether to continue to receive the service.

Suppose you want to buy toothpaste and the shop owner says that she can sell the toothpaste only if you buy a toothbrush. If you are not interested in buying the brush, your right to choice is denied. Similarly, sometimes gas supply dealers insist that you have to buy the stove from them when you take a new connection. In this way many a time you are forced to buy things that you may not wish to and are left with no choice.

Case study

A refund — Abirami’s case: Abirami, a student of Ansari Nagar, joined a two-year course at a local coaching institute for professional courses in New Delhi. At the time of joining she paid the fees of Rs 61,020 as lump sum for the entire course of two years. However, she decided to opt out of the course at the end of one year as she found that the quality of teaching was not up to the mark. When she asked for a refund of the fee for one year, it was denied to her. When she filed the case in the District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, the Commission directed the Institute to refund Rs 28,000, saying that she had the right to choose. The Institute again appealed in the State Consumer Commission. The State Commission upheld the District Commission’s direction and further fined the institute Rs 25,000 for a frivolous appeal. It also directed the institute to pay Rs 7,000 as compensation and litigation cost. The State Commission also restrained all the educational and professional institutions in the state from charging fees from students for the entire duration of the course in advance and that too at one go. Any violation of this order may invite penalties and imprisonment.

Right to Seek Redressal

Consumers have the right to seek redressal against unfair trade practices and exploitation. If any damage is done to a consumer, she has the right to get compensation depending on the degree of damage. There is a need to provide an easy and effective public system by which this can be done.

The consumer can file a complaint before the appropriate consumer forum on his or her own, with or without the services of lawyers. These days a consumer, as an individual or as a group (called a class action suit), can file a complaint both physically or through the internet, and conduct the case through video conferencing.

Case study

Prakash’s case, step by step: Prakash had sent a money order to his village for his daughter’s marriage. The money did not reach his daughter when she needed it, nor did it reach months later. He (1) goes to the post office to send the money order; (2) comes to know the money has not reached his daughter; (3) enquires about the money order at the post office; (4) the post office does not respond satisfactorily; (5) goes to the local Consumer Protection Council for advice; (6) goes to a Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission to file a case, fills a registration form, and the Commission sends notice to the other party; (7) pleads the case himself in the Commission office; (8) the Commission’s judge verifies the documents and hears the arguments of both the aggrieved party and the other party; (9) the judge announces the Disputes Redressal Commission’s verdict.

Right to Seek Redressal — the Three-Tier Machinery

The consumer movement in India has led to the formation of various organisations, locally known as consumer forums or consumer protection councils. They guide consumers on how to file cases in the Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions and, on many occasions, represent individual consumers in these commissions. These voluntary organisations also receive financial support from the government for creating awareness among people. If you live in a residential colony you might have noticed boards of Residents’ Welfare Associations — if there is any unfair trade practice meted out to their members, they take up the case on their behalf.

Under COPRA, a three-tier quasi-judicial machinery at the district, state and national levels was set up for redressal of consumer disputes.

The three-tier redressal machinery under COPRA

DistrictDistrict Consumer Disputes Redressal CommissionCases involving claims up to Rs 1 crore
StateState Consumer Disputes Redressal CommissionClaims between Rs 1 crore and Rs 10 crore
NationalNational Commission (New Delhi)Claims exceeding Rs 10 crore

If a case is dismissed in the district-level commission, a consumer can also appeal in the state and then in the national-level commissions. Thus the Act has enabled us as consumers to have the right to represent in the Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions.

Learning to Become Well-Informed Consumers

When we as consumers become conscious of our rights, while purchasing various goods and services, we will be able to distinguish and make informed choices. This calls for acquiring the knowledge and skill to become a well-informed consumer. How do we become conscious of our rights? The posters and advertisements that governments and consumer organisations bring out are one means: the chapter reproduces a government poster that urges consumers to “ensure consumer rights to healthy environment as against air pollution, water pollution, noise pollution” and to “assert rights, pursue remedies”.

The enactment of COPRA has led to the setting up of separate Departments of Consumer Affairs in central and state governments. The posters that we see are one example through which government spreads information about the legal process which people can use. You might also be seeing such advertisements on television channels.

ISI and Agmark

While buying many commodities, on the cover you might have seen a logo with the letters ISI, Agmark, Hallmark or +F. These logos and certification help consumers get assured of quality while purchasing goods and services. The organisations that monitor and issue these certificates allow producers to use their logos provided they follow certain quality standards.

Though these organisations develop quality standards for many products, it is not compulsory for all the producers to follow standards. However, for some products that affect the health and safety of consumers, or products of mass consumption like LPG cylinders, food colours and additives, cement and packaged drinking water, it is mandatory on the part of the producers to get certified by these organisations.

Taking the Consumer Movement Forward

India has been observing 24 December as National Consumers’ Day. It was on this day that the Indian Parliament enacted the Consumer Protection Act in 1986. India is one of the countries that have exclusive authority for consumer redressal.

The consumer movement in India has made some progress in terms of numbers of organised groups and their activities. There are today more than 2,000 consumer groups in the country, of which only about 50–60 are well organised and recognised for their work.

However, the consumer redressal process is becoming cumbersome, expensive and time-consuming. Many a time consumers are required to engage lawyers; these cases require time for filing and attending the commission proceedings; in most purchases cash memos are not issued, hence evidence is not easy to gather; and most purchases in the market are small retail sales.

The COPRA was amended in 2019 to further strengthen consumers in India:

  • Buying through the internet is now included.
  • If there is any service deficiency or defective product, the service provider or manufacturer is also held responsible and would be penalised or even imprisoned.
  • Settlement of disputes with the help of a neutral intermediary outside the Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, called a mediator, is now encouraged at all three tiers.

After more than 30 years of the enactment of COPRA, consumer awareness in India is spreading but slowly. Besides this, the enforcement of laws that protect workers, especially in the unorganised sectors, is weak; similarly, rules and regulations for the working of markets are often not followed. Nevertheless, there is scope for consumers to realise their role and importance. It is often said that consumer movements can be effective only with the consumers’ active involvement. It requires a voluntary effort and struggle involving the participation of one and all.

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

What are the various ways by which people may be exploited in the market?

Model answer

Under-weighing, adding charges that were not mentioned before, selling adulterated or defective goods, passing on false information through advertisements, denying choice by forcing additional purchases, and refusing responsibility once a sale is complete. Large companies with wealth, power and reach can manipulate the market because consumers buy in small amounts and are scattered.

2.

Think of one example from your experience where you thought that there was some “cheating” in the market. Discuss in the classroom.

Model answer

For example: a shop charging more than the MRP; a vegetable seller using a faulty or tampered weight; a packet of biscuits past its expiry date; a “free gift” offer that raises the price; or a repair shop replacing a part that was not faulty. Discuss what evidence would be needed and which right applies. (Student answers will vary.)

3.

What do you think should be the role of government to protect consumers?

Model answer

To make and enforce laws such as COPRA and the RTI Act; to set and monitor quality standards and make certification mandatory for health- and safety-related products; to provide accessible consumer courts; to spread awareness through Departments of Consumer Affairs, posters and advertisements; to check hoarding, black marketing and adulteration; and to support consumer organisations.

4.

What could have been the steps taken by consumer groups?

Model answer

Writing articles and holding exhibitions to spread awareness; forming groups to check malpractices in ration shops and overcrowding in road transport; guiding consumers on how to file cases and representing them before the commissions; campaigning against false advertising; pressing the government for a law, which led to COPRA in 1986; and joining international bodies such as Consumers International.

5.

There may be rules and regulations but they are often not followed. Why? Discuss.

Model answer

Because supervision is weak and the consumer movement is not strong enough; many consumers do not know their rights or do not complain; purchases are small and cash memos are often not issued, so evidence is hard to gather; cases are cumbersome, expensive and time-consuming; and traders know that most complaints go no further.

6.

For the following products and services, discuss what safety rules should be observed by the producer: LPG cylinder, cinema theatre, circus, medicines, edible oil, marriage pandal, high-rise building.

Model answer

LPG cylinder: tested valves, no leakage, safety instructions printed. Cinema theatre: fire exits, extinguishers, safe wiring and seating capacity limits. Circus: safe enclosures, trained animals, medical help on site. Medicines: correct composition, expiry dates, side-effect warnings, no banned drugs. Edible oil: purity and Agmark standard, no adulteration. Marriage pandal: fire-retardant material, stable structure, safe electrical connections. High-rise building: structural safety, fire escapes, lifts and water systems to code.

7.

Find out any case of accident or negligence from people around you where you think the responsibility lay with the producer. Discuss.

Model answer

For example: a pressure cooker whose safety valve failed; a mobile phone charger that overheated; a lift that stopped between floors; a food product that caused illness. Ask: was the design, manufacture or warning defective? What evidence is needed? Which right — safety or information — was violated? (Student activity.)

8.

When we buy commodities we find that the price charged is sometimes higher or lower than the MRP printed on the pack. Discuss the possible reasons. Should consumer groups do something about this?

Model answer

Higher than MRP: shops in remote areas or stations adding transport and convenience margins, or plain overcharging — which is illegal and should be complained about. Lower than MRP: discounts, clearance sales, competition, or goods nearing expiry. Consumer groups should publicise the rule that selling above MRP is an offence, encourage bills, and take up repeated violations with the authorities.

9.

Pick up a few packaged goods and examine the information given. In what ways are they useful? Is there some information you think should be given but is not?

Model answer

Useful: ingredients (for allergies and preferences), date of manufacture and expiry (for safety), MRP (to check overcharging), batch number (for recalls), manufacturer’s address (for complaints), and usage instructions. Often missing or unclear: the quantity of each ingredient, country of origin, detailed warnings, and a toll-free complaint number. (Student activity.)

10.

People make complaints about the lack of civic amenities such as bad roads or poor water and health facilities but no one listens. Now the RTI Act gives you the power to question. Do you agree? Discuss.

Model answer

Yes. The RTI Act lets a citizen ask government departments for information about their functions — how much was sanctioned and spent on a road or water scheme, when work was scheduled, why it is delayed. With that information, a complaint becomes a specific, verifiable question, and officials must reply. Amritha’s case shows how an RTI application produced both the reason for delay and her appointment letter.

11.

Which of the following offers would really benefit consumers? (a) 15 gm more in every 500 gm pack (b) subscribe for a newspaper with a gift at the end of a year (c) scratch and win gifts worth Rs 10 lakhs (d) a milk chocolate inside a 500 gram glucose box (e) win a gold coin inside a pack (f) buy shoes worth Rs 2,000 and get one pair worth Rs 500 free.

Model answer

Genuine benefits: (a) more quantity for the same price, and (f) a real free pair if the shoes are what you need at a fair price. The others — (b) a distant gift, (c) a lottery-like scratch card, (d) and (e) a small extra inside a pack — are mostly marketing devices that raise the price or the appeal rather than the value; the chance of winning is tiny. Read the price per unit, not the offer.

12.

Arrange the following in the correct order: (a) Arita files a case in the District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (b) She engages a professional person (c) She realises that the dealer has given her defective material (d) She starts attending the commission proceedings (e) She goes and complains to the dealer and the branch office, to no effect (f) She is asked to produce the bill and warranty before the commission (g) She purchases a wall clock from a retail outlet (h) Within a few months, the dealer was ordered by the commission to replace her old wall clock with a brand new one at no extra cost.

Model answer

Correct order: (g) she purchases a wall clock → (c) she realises the material is defective → (e) she complains to the dealer and branch office with no effect → (b) she engages a professional person → (a) she files a case in the District Commission → (f) she is asked to produce the bill and warranty → (d) she attends the proceedings → (h) the dealer is ordered to replace the clock at no extra cost.

13.

What is the difference between a consumer protection council and a Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission?

Model answer

A consumer protection council (consumer forum) is a voluntary organisation that guides consumers, helps them file cases and sometimes represents them, and receives government support for awareness work. A Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission is a quasi-judicial body set up under COPRA at district, state and national levels which actually hears cases and passes orders for compensation or replacement.

14.

Categorise these cases under the six consumer rights: (a) Lata got an electric shock from a newly purchased iron and complained immediately (b) John is dissatisfied with MTNL/BSNL/TATA INDICOM services and files a case in the District Commission (c) your friend has been sold a medicine past its expiry date and you advise her to complain (d) Iqbal scans all the particulars given on the pack of any item he buys (e) you are not satisfied with your cable operator but cannot switch to anybody else (f) you realise you have received a defective camera and are complaining to the head office persistently.

Model answer

(a) Right to safety. (b) Right to seek redressal (and to represent). (c) Right to information (expiry date) leading to redressal. (d) Right to information. (e) Right to choose. (f) Right to seek redressal (and to be heard).

15.

If standardisation ensures the quality of a commodity, why are many goods available in the market without ISI or Agmark certification?

Model answer

Because certification is voluntary for most products — organisations develop standards but it is not compulsory for all producers to follow them. It is mandatory only for products that affect health and safety or are of mass consumption, such as LPG cylinders, food colours and additives, cement and packaged drinking water. For other goods, producers may skip certification to save cost, and enforcement is weak.

16.

Find out the details of who provides Hallmark and ISO certification.

Model answer

Hallmark: issued by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) for gold and silver jewellery, indicating purity. ISO certification: given by the International Organization for Standardization through accredited certifying bodies, for quality management systems (ISO 9001) and environmental management (ISO 14001), among others. ISI is also a BIS mark, and Agmark is issued under the Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) scheme for agricultural products.

1.
NCERT exercise5 marks

Why are rules and regulations required in the marketplace? Illustrate with a few examples.

Model answer

Because the individual consumer is in a weak position: once a sale is complete the seller tries to shift all responsibility on to the buyer. Without rules, traders can weigh less than they should, add charges not mentioned before, sell adulterated or defective goods, and make false claims through advertisements — as with powdered milk sold as better than mother’s milk, or cigarettes whose link with cancer took years of court cases. When producers are few and powerful and consumers are scattered, the market itself cannot correct this, so rules and a redressal system are needed.

2.
NCERT exercise5 marks

What factors gave birth to the consumer movement in India? Trace its evolution.

Model answer

Factors: dissatisfaction with unfair trade practices and the absence of any legal system to protect consumers; rampant food shortages, hoarding, black marketing and adulteration of food and edible oil; the presumption that it was the consumer’s own responsibility to be careful. Evolution: an organised movement began in the 1960s; till the 1970s consumer organisations wrote articles and held exhibitions and formed groups to check malpractices in ration shops and overcrowding in road transport; pressure on business and government grew; the UN adopted the Guidelines for Consumer Protection in 1985; India enacted COPRA in 1986 and amended it in 2019; more recently there has been an upsurge in the number of consumer groups, with more than 2,000 today.

Important Questions and Answers

Board-style practice

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

Why do consumers need protection? Explain any three reasons. (3 marks)

Model answer

(i) The individual consumer is in a weak position — once a sale is complete the seller shifts all responsibility on to the buyer. (ii) Traders can exploit consumers through under-weighing, hidden charges, adulteration and defective goods. (iii) When producers are few and powerful and consumers are scattered and buy in small amounts, companies can manipulate the market and pass on false information through the media. Rules and a redressal system are therefore needed.

2.

Explain the six consumer rights given by the Consumer Protection Act. (6 marks)

Model answer

Right to safety — protection against goods and services hazardous to life and property (Reji Mathew’s case). Right to information — particulars of goods and services: ingredients, price, batch number, dates, manufacturer, and the RTI Act for government services (Amritha’s case). Right to choose — the right to decide whether to continue a service, and not to be forced to buy extras (Abirami’s refund). Right to seek redressal — the right to complain and get compensation according to the degree of damage (Prakash’s money order case). Right to represent — the right to be heard in the Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions, individually or as a group. Right to consumer education — the right to be made aware of these rights.

3.

Explain the three-tier machinery set up under COPRA. (3 marks)

Model answer

A three-tier quasi-judicial machinery exists at district, state and national levels. The District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission deals with claims up to Rs 1 crore, the State Commission with claims between Rs 1 crore and Rs 10 crore, and the National Commission, in New Delhi, with claims exceeding Rs 10 crore. If a case is dismissed at the district level, a consumer may appeal to the state and then to the national commission.

4.

What is the right to information? How has it been expanded? (3 marks)

Model answer

It is the right to be informed about the particulars of goods and services purchased — ingredients, price, batch number, date of manufacture and expiry, and the manufacturer’s address — so that a consumer can complain and claim compensation or replacement. It has been expanded to cover government services by the RTI Act, enacted in October 2005, which gives citizens access to information about the functions of government departments, as Amritha found when she learnt why her result was delayed.

5.

Explain the case of Reji Mathew. Which right was violated? (3 marks)

Model answer

Reji Mathew, a Class IX student, was admitted to a private clinic in Kerala for removal of tonsils. Because of improper anaesthesia he suffered brain abnormalities and was crippled for life. His father claimed Rs 5,00,000 before the State Commission for medical negligence, was dismissed for insufficient evidence, appealed to the National Commission, and the hospital was held responsible and ordered to pay compensation. The right to safety was violated — the service delivered was hazardous to life.

6.

What does the 2019 amendment to COPRA provide? (3 marks)

Model answer

It includes buying through the internet (e-commerce); it holds the service provider or manufacturer responsible and liable to penalty or imprisonment for service deficiency or a defective product (product liability); and it encourages settlement of disputes through a neutral intermediary, a mediator, outside the Commission, at all three tiers.

7.

Explain the quality certification marks mentioned in the chapter. When is certification mandatory? (5 marks)

Model answer

ISI (Bureau of Indian Standards) for industrial and consumer goods, Agmark for agricultural products such as edible oil and cereals, Hallmark for gold and silver jewellery, and +F for fortified foods. The organisations that monitor and issue these certificates allow producers to use the logos provided they follow prescribed quality standards. Certification is voluntary for most products, but mandatory for products that affect health and safety or are of mass consumption — LPG cylinders, food colours and additives, cement and packaged drinking water.

8.

“Consumer movements can be effective only with the consumers’ active involvement.” Discuss. (3 marks)

Model answer

Because rights are of no use unless they are used. A consumer who does not ask for a bill, check the MRP or complain when cheated allows unfair practices to continue; the machinery works only when complaints are filed — as Prakash, Abirami and Reji’s father did. Consumer organisations can create awareness, but the movement requires a voluntary effort and struggle involving the participation of one and all.

9.

Describe the difficulties faced in the consumer redressal process. (5 marks)

Model answer

The process is cumbersome, expensive and time-consuming: consumers are often required to engage lawyers; cases require time for filing and attending proceedings; in most purchases cash memos are not issued, so evidence is hard to gather; and most purchases are small retail sales, so the effort may seem greater than the loss. Awareness is spreading only slowly, enforcement of related laws is weak, and of more than 2,000 consumer groups only about 50–60 are well organised and recognised. The 2019 amendment — e-commerce coverage, product liability and mediation — is an attempt to ease these problems.

10.

What is MRP? Why is it important for consumers? (2 marks)

Model answer

MRP is the Maximum Retail Price printed on the packet — the highest price at which the good may be sold. It protects consumers from overcharging, and consumers may even bargain for less than the MRP.

Source-Based and Case-Based Questions

Read the passage or table, then answer

Source 1 — Reji’s suffering

Case study

Reji Mathew, a healthy boy studying in Class IX, was admitted in a private clinic in Kerala for removal of tonsils. An ENT surgeon performed the tonsillectomy operation under general anaesthesia. As a result of improper anaesthesia Reji showed symptoms of some brain abnormalities because of which he was crippled for life. His father filed a complaint in the State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission claiming compensation of Rs 5,00,000 for medical negligence and deficiency, in service. The State Commission, saying that the evidence was not sufficient, dismissed it. Reji’s father appealed again in the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission located in New Delhi. The National Commission after looking into the complaint, held the hospital responsible for medical negligence and directed it to pay the compensation.

1.

Which consumer right was violated, and how?

Model answer

The right to safety — a service hazardous to life was delivered through improper anaesthesia, leaving a healthy boy crippled for life.

2.

Trace the route of the complaint and explain what it shows about the redressal system.

Model answer

State Commission first — dismissed for insufficient evidence; then appeal to the National Commission — hospital held responsible and compensation ordered. It shows that the three-tier system allows appeal, and that persistence can produce justice even after an initial setback.

3.

Why does the chapter use this case under “safety” rather than “redressal”?

Model answer

Because the substance of the complaint is that the service itself was unsafe; redressal is the remedy that followed. The chapter pairs each right with a case, and safety is the right at stake.

Source 2 — Abirami’s refund

Case study

Abirami, a student of Ansari Nagar, joined a two-year course at a local coaching institute for professional courses in New Delhi. At the time of joining the course, she paid the fees Rs 61,020 as lumpsum for the entire course of two years. However, she decided to opt out of the course at the end of one year as she found that the quality of teaching was not up to the mark. When she asked for a refund of the fee for one year, it was denied to her. When she filed the case in the District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, the Commission directed the Institute to refund Rs 28,000 saying that she had the right to choose. The Institute again appealed in the State Consumer Commission. The State Commission upheld the District Commission’s direction and further fined the institute Rs 25,000 for a frivolous appeal. It also directed the institute to pay Rs 7000 as compensation and litigation cost.

1.

Which right did the Commission rely on?

Model answer

The right to choose — a consumer who receives a service has the right to decide whether to continue to receive it, regardless of age, gender or nature of service.

2.

What total amount did the institute have to bear?

Model answer

Rs 28,000 as refund, plus Rs 25,000 fine for a frivolous appeal, plus Rs 7,000 compensation and litigation cost — Rs 60,000 in all.

3.

What wider change did the State Commission’s order bring?

Model answer

It restrained all educational and professional institutions in the state from charging fees for the entire duration of a course in advance and at one go, with penalties and imprisonment for violation — so one consumer’s case changed practice for everyone.

Source extract

The poster reproduced in the chapter urges consumers to “Ensure consumer rights to healthy environment as against air pollution, water pollution, noise pollution” and lists, in small print, the causes — for example pollution from vehicles and industry, burning of waste, use of loudspeakers and generators — and the remedies available, ending with the slogan “Assert rights, pursue remedies”.

1.

What is the poster trying to achieve?

Model answer

To make consumers conscious of their rights, and to tell them that pollution of air, water and noise is a consumer issue with legal remedies — so that people assert their rights and pursue remedies rather than accept the harm.

2.

Model answer

Because COPRA led to Departments of Consumer Affairs in central and state governments whose task is to spread information about the legal process people can use; posters and television advertisements are how awareness is created.

3.

Model answer

Primarily the right to safety — protection against goods and services hazardous to life and property — and the right to be informed about them.

1.

This chapter sets no map work. Instead, draw a flow chart of the redressal process from buying a defective good to the Commission’s verdict.

Model answer

Buy the good → discover the defect → complain to the seller (keep the bill and warranty) → if no response, approach a Consumer Protection Council for advice → file a complaint before the appropriate Commission (District up to Rs 1 crore) → the Commission sends notice to the other party → documents verified and both sides heard → verdict: replacement, refund or compensation → appeal to State, then National, if dissatisfied.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

A shopkeeper says: “If I have to give a bill for every sale and follow every rule, my small shop will close.” How would you answer him?

Model answer

A bill protects him too: it is proof of a genuine sale, it prevents disputes, and it builds the trust that brings customers back. Following rules — correct weights, no adulteration, printed prices — is the minimum for staying in business, and consumer law applies to small shops as much as large ones. At the same time, the state should make compliance cheap and simple for small traders: readily available bills, standard weights, easy registration and awareness, rather than only penalties. (Model answer.)

2.

Why is the right to information often more useful to a poor consumer than the right to seek redressal?

Model answer

Because redressal is slow, costly and needs evidence, while information is immediate and preventive: checking the MRP, the expiry date, the weight and the ingredients stops the loss before it happens. For a poor consumer, a small loss is not worth a long case, so the cheapest protection is knowledge at the point of purchase — which is also why consumer education is itself a right. (Model answer.)

3.

“India has strong consumer laws but weak consumer protection.” Discuss.

Model answer

The laws are strong: COPRA 1986 with a 2019 amendment covering e-commerce, product liability and mediation; the RTI Act; mandatory certification for health-related products; and a three-tier court system with wide jurisdiction. Protection is weaker in practice: only about 50–60 of more than 2,000 consumer groups are well organised; cases are cumbersome, expensive and slow; bills are rarely issued so evidence is scarce; most purchases are small; and enforcement of related laws is weak. Awareness is spreading but slowly. (Model answer.)

4.

A company sells a food supplement claiming it “builds immunity and prevents all disease”. What can a consumer do, and which rights are involved?

Model answer

The claim is false information, so the right to information is violated — labels must state ingredients and cannot mislead; the right to safety is involved if the product is hazardous or its claims lead people to abandon treatment; and the consumer can seek redressal for compensation. Practically: keep the bill and the advertisement, complain to the seller and the manufacturer, and file before the District Commission; the advertisement itself can also be reported to the authorities. (Model answer.)

5.

Should consumer courts handle very small claims at all? Argue both ways and give your view.

Model answer

For: small losses matter greatly to poor households, and if small claims are excluded, traders are free to cheat a little from everyone — the total loss is huge. Against: the cost of a hearing may exceed the claim, clogging the system. The chapter’s answer is the three-tier structure with an accessible district forum and, since 2019, mediation, so that small claims can be settled cheaply and quickly. My view: keep small claims in, but make the process simple and free of lawyers where possible. (Model answer.)

6.

Why does the chapter place consumer rights in an Economics textbook rather than a Civics one?

Model answer

Because the consumer is an economic actor: markets allocate goods, and the balance of power between producers and consumers determines whether exchange is fair. Consumer rights correct a market failure — when producers are few and powerful and consumers are scattered, prices, quality and information are distorted. The chapter is also about development: fair markets, safe goods and effective redressal are part of how development reaches ordinary people. (Model answer.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

We participate in the market both as producers and as consumers, and the individual consumer is in a weak position: once a sale is made, the seller tries to shift all responsibility on to the buyer. Exploitation takes the form of under-weighing, hidden charges, adulterated or defective goods, and false information passed on through advertising — as when powdered milk was sold for years as better than mother’s milk. Markets do not work fairly when producers are few and powerful while consumers are scattered and buy in small amounts, so rules and regulations are needed.

The consumer movement arose from this dissatisfaction, and shifted the responsibility for quality from buyers to sellers. In India it began in an organised form in the 1960s against food shortages, hoarding, black marketing and adulteration; the UN adopted the Guidelines for Consumer Protection in 1985, and India enacted the Consumer Protection Act (COPRA) in 1986, amended in 2019. Consumers International now links over 200 organisations in more than 100 countries.

COPRA gives six rights: safety (Reji Mathew, crippled by improper anaesthesia, finally won compensation from the National Commission); information (labels, MRP, expiry dates, and the RTI Act of 2005, which helped Amritha learn why her job result was delayed); choice (Abirami won a refund of fees when she left a course); redressal (Prakash pursued a missing money order through the District Commission himself); representation (consumer forums and the three-tier commissions — District up to Rs 1 crore, State up to Rs 10 crore, National above that); and consumer education. Quality marks — ISI, Agmark, Hallmark and +F — assure standards, and certification is mandatory for products affecting health and safety such as LPG cylinders, food colours and additives, cement and packaged drinking water.

The movement has progressed — more than 2,000 consumer groups, exclusive redressal authorities, National Consumers’ Day on 24 December — but redressal remains cumbersome, expensive and slow, few consumers keep bills, and only about 50–60 groups are well organised. The 2019 amendment brought e-commerce, product liability and mediation. Ultimately, consumer movements can be effective only with the consumers’ active involvement: it requires a voluntary effort and struggle involving the participation of one and all.
