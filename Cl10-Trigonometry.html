Introduction: What is Trigonometry?

Trigonometry is the branch of mathematics that studies the relationships between the sides and angles of triangles, especially right-angled triangles. The word "trigonometry" comes from the Greek words trigonon (triangle) and metron (measure). In Class 10, you will learn how trigonometry helps us solve real-world problems, such as finding the height of a building or the distance across a river, without directly measuring them.

Why is trigonometry important? Trigonometry is widely used in engineering, architecture, navigation, astronomy, and many other fields. It forms the foundation for advanced mathematics and science studies.

Chapter 8: Introduction to Trigonometry

This chapter introduces the basic concepts of trigonometry, focusing on right-angled triangles. You will learn about the six trigonometric ratios, their relationships, standard values, and fundamental identities. All explanations are aligned with the latest NCERT and CBSE curriculum for Class 10.

1. Understanding the Right-Angled Triangle

Consider a triangle ABC that is right-angled at B. The sides of the triangle are named based on the angle you are considering:

  • Hypotenuse: The side opposite the right angle (the longest side).
  • Opposite side: The side opposite to the angle in question.
  • Adjacent side: The side next to the angle in question (but not the hypotenuse).

2. The Six Trigonometric Ratios

For any acute angle (less than 90°) in a right-angled triangle, we define six trigonometric ratios:

For angle A in triangle ABC (right-angled at B):
sin A = Opposite / Hypotenuse = BC / AC
cos A = Adjacent / Hypotenuse = AB / AC
tan A = Opposite / Adjacent = BC / AB
cosec A = Hypotenuse / Opposite = AC / BC
sec A = Hypotenuse / Adjacent = AC / AB
cot A = Adjacent / Opposite = AB / BC

sin, cos, and tan are called the primary trigonometric ratios. The other three (cosec, sec, cot) are their reciprocals.

Memory Tip: Use the phrase SOH-CAH-TOA to remember:
Sin = Opposite / Hypotenuse
Cos = Adjacent / Hypotenuse
Tan = Opposite / Adjacent
Example: In triangle ABC, right-angled at B, if AB = 24 cm and BC = 7 cm, find sin A and cos A.
Solution: First, find the hypotenuse AC using the Pythagoras theorem:
AC = √(AB² + BC²) = √(24² + 7²) = √(576 + 49) = √625 = 25 cm.
sin A = BC / AC = 7 / 25
cos A = AB / AC = 24 / 25

3. Standard Values of Trigonometric Ratios

The values of trigonometric ratios for certain standard angles (0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°) are frequently used in problems and proofs. Memorizing this table is essential.

Angle sin cos tan cosec sec cot
0° 0 1 0 Not defined 1 Not defined
30° 1/2 √3/2 1/√3 2 2/√3 √3
45° 1/√2 1/√2 1 √2 √2 1
60° √3/2 1/2 √3 2/√3 2 1/√3
90° 1 0 Not defined 1 Not defined 0

Notice: The values of sin increase from 0 to 1 as the angle increases from 0° to 90°, while the values of cos decrease from 1 to 0. The tan values can become very large or undefined (at 90°).

4. Fundamental Trigonometric Identities

Trigonometric identities are equations that are true for all values of the variables involved. The three most important identities in Class 10 are:

sin² θ + cos² θ = 1
1 + tan² θ = sec² θ
1 + cot² θ = cosec² θ

These identities are derived from the Pythagoras theorem and are used to simplify and solve trigonometric expressions and equations.

Example: If sin θ = 3/5, find cos θ.
Solution: Use the identity sin² θ + cos² θ = 1.
(3/5)² + cos² θ = 1 ⇒ 9/25 + cos² θ = 1 ⇒ cos² θ = 1 - 9/25 = 16/25 ⇒ cos θ = 4/5

5. Trigonometric Ratios of Complementary Angles

Two angles are called complementary if their sum is 90°. In a right-angled triangle, the two acute angles are always complementary. The following relationships hold:

sin (90° − A) = cos A
cos (90° − A) = sin A
tan (90° − A) = cot A
cot (90° − A) = tan A
sec (90° − A) = cosec A
cosec (90° − A) = sec A
Tip: The value of a trigonometric ratio for an angle is equal to the value of its complementary ratio for the complementary angle.

Chapter 9: Some Applications of Trigonometry

This chapter focuses on how trigonometry is used to solve real-life problems involving heights and distances. You will learn about the concepts of angle of elevation and angle of depression, and how to apply trigonometric ratios to find unknown heights or distances without direct measurement.

1. Angle of Elevation and Angle of Depression

Animation: Angle of Elevation and Depression

Drag to change the angle. Watch how the line of sight and triangle change.

Angle of Elevation: The angle formed by the line of sight when looking up at an object above the horizontal level.
Angle of Depression: The angle formed by the line of sight when looking down at an object below the horizontal level.

In both cases, the horizontal line is always drawn from the observer's eye. The angle is measured upwards (elevation) or downwards (depression) from the horizontal.

2. Solving Heights and Distances Problems

To solve real-life problems using trigonometry, follow these steps:

  1. Draw a diagram representing the situation.
  2. Mark all known and unknown lengths and angles.
  3. Identify the right-angled triangle(s) involved.
  4. Choose the appropriate trigonometric ratio based on the given information.
  5. Write the equation and solve for the unknown.
Example: From a point 15 m away from the foot of a tower, the angle of elevation of the top of the tower is 60°. Find the height of the tower.
Solution: Let the height of the tower be h m.
tan 60° = h / 15 ⇒ √3 = h / 15 ⇒ h = 15 × √3 ≈ 25.98 m
