How do we know something is alive?

Movement is the first thing we notice — but it is not enough. Tap each case below to test the idea.

🔬
The real definition: invisible molecular movement

Living organisms are well-organised structures — tissues made of cells, cells made of smaller components. Because of environmental effects, this order keeps breaking down. If order breaks down completely, the organism dies. So living things must keep repairing and maintaining their structures — and since structures are made of molecules, molecules must keep moving. That invisible movement is the most reliable sign of life.

Repair needed

Section 5.1

What are Life Processes?

Maintenance functions that must go on even when you are doing nothing at all — even while asleep. Tap any box in the chain.

Tap a box above

Each of these four is a maintenance job your body runs right now, without asking you.

Why food must come from outside

Energy is needed to prevent damage and break-down. That energy is not made from nothing — it is transferred in from outside. Life on earth is carbon-based, so most food is carbon-based too.

Why oxygen appears

Breaking molecules down usually means oxidising-reducing reactions. Many organisms take oxygen from outside to do this — that is respiration.

Why waste must leave

Reactions that release energy also create by-products that are useless — or harmful — to cells. They must be removed: excretion.

NCERT In-text Questions (5.1)
In a multicellular body most cells are far from the surface and not in direct contact with the environment. Diffusion can move molecules only over very short distances and too slowly, so oxygen would never reach deep tissues in time. A transport system (blood) plus specialised surfaces (alveoli) are needed instead.
Visible movement is not reliable. The dependable criterion is molecular movement — the constant repair and maintenance of the organised structure. Growth, breathing, responsiveness and reproduction are supporting signs, but the invisible molecular activity is the real one.
They serve two purposes: (i) as a source of energy for maintenance and activity, and (ii) as raw material for growth and repair — making proteins and other substances the body needs.
Nutrition, respiration, transportation of materials within the body, and excretion of wastes — together these keep the organised structure of the body repaired and running.
Section 5.2

Nutrition — how organisms get food

Two strategies, one goal: energy + building material.

🌿Autotrophic

Uses simple inorganic material (CO₂ + water) and an external energy source (sunlight) to build complex high-energy food.

Examples: green plants, some bacteria.

🍖Heterotrophic

Takes in complex material prepared by other organisms; it must be broken into simpler molecules using enzymes (bio-catalysts).

Examples: animals, fungi. Survival depends directly or indirectly on autotrophs.

Photosynthesis — watch the raw materials become food

Third event: reduction of CO₂ to carbohydrate. These need not happen immediately one after another — desert plants take CO₂ at night and use daytime light energy on the stored intermediate.

Stomata: open or closed?
What controls the pore

Opening: water flows into the guard cells - they swell - the pore opens.

Closing: guard cells shrink (lose water) - the pore closes.

Why close at all? Huge amounts of water can be lost through stomata. When the plant does not need CO₂ for photosynthesis, it shuts them.

Gas exchange isn't only in leaves — it happens across the surface of stems and roots too.

"The opening and closing of the stomatal pore is a function of the guard cells."

Two facts examiners love: (1) HCl creates the acidic medium pepsin needs, and mucus protects the stomach lining from that acid. (2) Bile has no enzyme — it emulsifies fat and neutralises the acidic food coming from the stomach.
Why the small intestine absorbs so well

The inner lining of the small intestine is folded into thousands of villi. Absorbed food enters the blood and is carried to every cell for energy, growth and repair.

Peristalsis — why food moves by itself

Herbivore vs carnivore: grass-eating animals need a longer small intestine to digest cellulose; meat is easier, so carnivores like tigers have a shorter one.

Large intestine: absorbs water from the unabsorbed material; the rest leaves via the anus, controlled by the anal sphincter.

"Acidity": the acid in your stomach is normal — but excess acid irritates the lining, which is exactly what adults complain about.

NCERT In-text Questions (5.2)
Autotrophic: uses simple inorganic raw materials (CO₂, water) and an external energy source (sunlight) to synthesise complex high-energy organic food; chlorophyll required; example green plants. Heterotrophic: takes in complex organic food prepared by other organisms and breaks it down with enzymes before use; no chlorophyll needed; example animals, fungi.
CO₂ — from air, through stomata. Water — from soil, absorbed by roots. Sunlight — from the sun. Chlorophyll — present in chloroplasts in leaf cells. Other minerals (nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, magnesium) come from soil; nitrogen is taken as nitrates/nitrites or as organic compounds made by bacteria.
Hydrochloric acid creates an acidic medium, which is needed for the protein-digesting enzyme pepsin to work. It also kills many bacteria that come with food. (Mucus protects the stomach lining from this acid.)
Digestive enzymes are bio-catalysts that break large complex food molecules into small, absorbable ones — starch - sugar, proteins - amino acids, fats - fatty acids and glycerol — so that they can be absorbed from the alimentary canal.
Its inner lining has numerous finger-like projections called villi which greatly increase the surface area for absorption. Villi are richly supplied with blood vessels, which carry the absorbed food to every cell of the body.

Respiration — breaking food to release energy

Note the first step is always the same: glucose - pyruvate, in the cytoplasm. Oxygen decides what happens next.

Aerobic respiration
  • Takes place in presence of oxygen
  • Pyruvate - CO₂ + H₂O
  • Happens in mitochondria
  • Releases a lot more energy
  • Example: humans, most plants and animals
Anaerobic respiration
  • Takes place in absence of oxygen
  • Pyruvate - ethanol + CO₂ (yeast) or lactic acid (our muscles)
  • Happens in cytoplasm
  • Releases much less energy
  • Examples: yeast (fermentation), muscle cells during sudden activity

How breathing works
Inside one alveolus
Alveoli by numbers

Spread flat, the alveolar surface would cover about 80 m² — roughly a tennis court inside your chest. Walls hold an extensive blood-vessel network.

Haemoglobin

A respiratory pigment in RBCs with very high affinity for oxygen. Without it, diffusion alone would take about 3 years for oxygen to travel from lungs to toes. CO₂ is more soluble, so it travels dissolved in plasma.

Fish vs us

Dissolved oxygen in water is low, so aquatic animals breathe much faster. Fish take water in through the mouth and force it past the gills, where blood picks up the dissolved oxygen.

Do plants respire too? Yes — but quietly
At night — no photosynthesis, so CO₂ elimination is the main exchange activity.
During the day — CO₂ produced in respiration is used up in photosynthesis, so no CO₂ is released; oxygen release is the main event.

Plants exchange gases through stomata; large inter-cellular spaces keep every cell in contact with air, so diffusion is enough.

NCERT In-text Questions (5.3)
Terrestrial organisms breathe atmospheric air, which contains far more oxygen than the small amount dissolved in water. So they do not need to breathe as fast, and can get enough oxygen with less effort.
Glucose (6C) is first broken into pyruvate (3C) in the cytoplasm. Then: (i) with oxygen — pyruvate - CO₂ + water + much energy, in mitochondria (aerobic); (ii) without oxygen in yeast — pyruvate - ethanol + CO₂ (fermentation); (iii) lack of oxygen in muscles — pyruvate - lactic acid, whose build-up causes cramps.
Oxygen is picked up by haemoglobin in the red blood corpuscles and carried to tissues; carbon dioxide is more soluble in water, so it is mostly transported dissolved in the blood plasma.
Inside the lungs the air passage divides into smaller and smaller tubes which end in balloon-like alveoli. Millions of alveoli together provide a huge surface (about 80 m²) whose walls carry an extensive network of blood vessels — ideal for gas exchange.

Our pump — the human heart (tap parts, then press Play)
Tap a chamber

Ventricles have thicker muscular walls than atria — they must push blood into organs.

Double circulation — in words
1

Pulmonary loop: right ventricle - pulmonary artery - lungs - oxygen picked up - pulmonary vein - left atrium.

2

Systemic loop: left atrium - left ventricle - aorta - whole body - de-oxygenated blood back through vena cava - right atrium.

Blood passes through the heart twice in one complete cycle — that is double circulation.

Why the separation matters: it prevents oxygenated and de-oxygenated blood from mixing, allowing a highly efficient oxygen supply — essential for birds and mammals, which spend energy maintaining body temperature. Amphibians and many reptiles have 3 chambers and tolerate some mixing; fish have 2 chambers, and blood passes through the heart only once per cycle.

The tubes — three types of blood vessels
Arteries

Carry blood away from the heart to organs. Blood emerges under high pressure - thick, elastic walls.

Veins

Collect blood from organs and bring it back to the heart. No high pressure - thin walls, but they have valves so blood does not flow backwards.

Capillaries

Extremely thin-walled vessels where exchange with cells actually happens — one cell thick.

Lymph: some plasma and proteins escape into tissue spaces; lymph collects this and returns it to the blood. It also carries digested fats and contains lymphocytes that fight infection.
Transport in plants — xylem & phloem
Tap a part

Two independent pipelines: xylem for water upward, phloem for food in both directions.

Transport of water — two forces
  • Root pressure: root cells actively take up ions - water follows in - a column of water is pushed upward. More important at night.
  • Transpiration pull: evaporation from leaf cells creates suction that pulls water up the xylem. The major force during the day when stomata are open.
  • Transpiration also helps temperature regulation.
Translocation — food movement
  • Transport of soluble products of photosynthesis — called translocation — happens in phloem.
  • Moves sucrose, amino acids and other substances to storage organs (roots, fruits, seeds) and growing organs.
  • Occurs in sieve tubes with help of companion cells, in both directions.
  • Needs energy from ATP: sucrose loaded into phloem raises osmotic pressure - water enters - pressure pushes material to low-pressure tissues.
NCERT In-text Questions (5.4)
Heart — pumps blood around the body. Blood — transports food, oxygen, CO₂, nitrogenous wastes, salts (plasma carries dissolved substances, RBCs carry oxygen). Blood vessels — arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins bring it back, capillaries allow exchange with cells. Platelets repair the network by clotting.
Mammals and birds are warm-blooded — they constantly use energy to maintain body temperature and have high energy needs. Keeping oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate allows a highly efficient supply of oxygen to the body. Mixing would reduce the oxygen content delivered to tissues.
The vascular tissue: xylem (vessels and tracheids) which transports water and minerals from roots upward, and phloem (sieve tubes and companion cells) which transports the products of photosynthesis.
Xylem vessels and tracheids of roots, stems and leaves form a continuous system. Root cells actively take up ions, creating a concentration difference that draws water in — producing root pressure. During the day, evaporation from leaf cells creates a suction (transpiration pull) which pulls water up the xylem. Both together move water and dissolved minerals to the highest parts of the plant.
Translocation in the phloem: sucrose and other substances are loaded into sieve tubes using energy from ATP. This raises the osmotic pressure of the tissue, so water moves in, and the resulting pressure moves the material to tissues with lower pressure. Companion cells help. Movement is bidirectional — e.g. stored sugar in roots moves to buds in spring.

Excretion — removing the harmful leftovers

Metabolic reactions leave nitrogenous waste. Filtering it out is the kidney's job — tap any part.

Excretory system in human beings 5 tappable parts
Tap a part

Each kidney contains a very large number of filtration units packed closely together.

Structure of a nephron — the filtration unit
Tap a part

Filtration is automatic (pressure); reabsorption is selective (the body takes back what it still needs).

How urine amount is regulated

The amount of water reabsorbed depends on how much excess water the body has and how much dissolved waste must be excreted. More excess water - less reabsorption - more dilute urine.

The bladder is muscular and under nervous control, so we can usually control the urge to urinate.

Artificial kidney (haemodialysis)

Tubes with a semi-permeable lining are suspended in dialysing fluid that has the same osmotic pressure as blood but no nitrogenous waste. Blood flows through the tubes; wastes diffuse out into the fluid; purified blood is pumped back.

Key difference from a real kidney: there is no reabsorption in dialysis.

Excretion in plants — completely different strategies
Oxygen itself is a waste product of photosynthesis.
Excess water is removed by transpiration.
Many wastes are stored in cellular vacuoles.
Wastes stored in leaves that fall off.
Stored as resins and gums, especially in old xylem.
Some waste is excreted into the surrounding soil.
NCERT In-text Questions (5.5)
Each nephron has a cluster of thin-walled capillaries (glomerulus) sitting in the cup-shaped Bowman's capsule, followed by a long coiled tubule. Blood pressure forces water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea out of the glomerulus into the capsule — this is filtration. As the filtrate flows along the tubule, useful substances (glucose, amino acids, salts and most water) are selectively reabsorbed into the surrounding blood capillaries. What remains is urine, which passes into the collecting duct and then the ureter.
Plants (i) release oxygen produced in photosynthesis, (ii) lose excess water by transpiration, (iii) store wastes in cellular vacuoles, (iv) store wastes in leaves that fall off, (v) store wastes as resins and gums in old xylem, and (vi) excrete some waste substances into the surrounding soil.
It depends on the amount of water reabsorbed in the tubules, which in turn depends on how much excess water the body has and how much dissolved waste must be excreted. If there is a lot of excess water, less is reabsorbed and more dilute urine is produced. The muscular bladder is under nervous control, so the urge can usually be controlled.
Revise in 60 seconds

What you have learnt

Movement of various types can be taken as an indication of life — but molecular movement is the reliable criterion.

Maintenance of life needs nutrition, respiration, transport and excretion.

Autotrophic nutrition: simple inorganic materials + external energy (sun) → complex high-energy organic material.

Heterotrophic nutrition: intake of complex material already prepared by other organisms.

Respiration breaks organic compounds to release energy as ATP; aerobic gives far more energy than anaerobic.

Human transport = heart + blood + blood vessels; in plants it is the vascular tissue — xylem and phloem.

Soluble nitrogenous wastes are removed by nephrons in the kidneys.

Plants excrete via vacuoles, gums/resins, falling leaves and into the soil.

Conditions: carbon dioxide, water, sunlight and chlorophyll. By-products: oxygen and carbohydrates (glucose; unused glucose is stored as starch).
Aerobic: occurs in the presence of oxygen; pyruvate → CO₂ + water; takes place in mitochondria; releases a large amount of energy. Anaerobic: occurs in the absence of oxygen; pyruvate → ethanol + CO₂ (or lactic acid in muscles); occurs in the cytoplasm; releases much less energy. Alveoli are balloon-like structures at the end of the finest bronchioles. Their very large number gives a huge surface area (about 80 m² if spread out); their walls are extremely thin; and the walls contain an extensive network of blood vessels. Together these make gas exchange fast and efficient.
Haemoglobin carries oxygen from the lungs to tissues. If it is deficient, less oxygen reaches the cells, so aerobic respiration is reduced, less energy is released, and the person feels weak and tired, may look pale, and can suffer breathlessness and dizziness. (This condition is anaemia.)
Blood passes through the heart twice in one complete cycle. In the pulmonary circulation, de-oxygenated blood goes from the right ventricle to the lungs and returns oxygenated to the left atrium. In the systemic circulation, oxygenated blood goes from the left ventricle through the aorta to the body and returns de-oxygenated to the right atrium. It is necessary because it keeps oxygenated and de-oxygenated blood completely separate, ensuring a highly efficient oxygen supply — essential for warm-blooded animals with high energy needs, which use energy to maintain body temperature.
Xylem: carries water and minerals; direction is upward (roots → leaves); vessels and tracheids are largely dead cells; no energy (ATP) needed — root pressure and transpiration pull do the work. Phloem: carries products of photosynthesis (sucrose) and amino acids; direction is both up and down; sieve tubes and companion cells are living; requires energy from ATP (osmotic pressure mechanism).
Both are the basic functional units of their organs and both work with a dense network of thin-walled capillaries. Alveoli: balloon-like air sacs; exchange gases by diffusion — O₂ in, CO₂ out; no reabsorption; large surface area. Nephrons: a glomerulus in a Bowman's capsule plus a coiled tubule; filter blood under pressure and then selectively reabsorb useful substances; produce urine. So alveoli only exchange, while nephrons filter and reabsorb.
