Section 12.1

Magnetic field and field lines

A compass needle is itself a tiny bar magnet. Bring a magnet near it and it turns — because a magnetic field fills the space around the magnet.

Field lines around a bar magnet — tap a part
Tap a part

By convention, field lines emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole — and inside the magnet they run from south to north, so the lines form closed curves.

Activity 12.1 — current deflects a needle

Switch the key on and the needle jumps — a current-carrying wire has a magnetic field around it. Reverse the cell connections and it deflects the other way.

Five properties examiners ask about
  • A magnetic field has both direction and magnitude.
  • Direction = the direction in which a north pole of a compass needle moves inside it.
  • Field lines emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole.
  • Inside the magnet, field lines run south → north, so field lines are closed curves.
  • Field lines are closer together where the field is stronger — near the poles.
Activity 12.2: iron filings sprinkled near a bar magnet arrange themselves along the field lines — each filing becomes a tiny compass needle.
Section 12.2

Magnetic field due to a current-carrying conductor

The pattern depends on the shape of the conductor. Reverse the current and every field line reverses.

Straight conductor — concentric circles

Field strength is directly proportional to the current and inversely proportional to the distance from the wire. Tap the button to reverse the current — the circles' arrows flip.

Right-hand thumb rule

Your curled fingers then give the direction of the magnetic field lines — a convenient way to find the field direction without a compass.

Circular loop

At every point of the loop the field is circular; near the centre those big arcs look like parallel straight lines. Each turn adds to the field, so a coil of n turns gives n times the field of a single turn.

Solenoid & electromagnet

A solenoid's field looks just like a bar magnet's — one end behaves as north, the other as south — but the field inside is uniform. Put soft iron inside and you get an electromagnet.

Shape decides the pattern

Straight wire → concentric circles. Loop → circles growing outward, straight at the centre. Solenoid → like a bar magnet.

More turns, more field

In a coil of n turns the current in every turn flows the same way, so the fields simply add up: n times the field of one turn.

Electromagnet = switchable magnet

A core of soft iron wrapped with insulated copper wire. It is magnetic only while current flows — and its strength can be changed by changing the current.

Section 12.3

Force on a current-carrying conductor

Ampere suggested it: if a current makes a field that pushes a magnet, the magnet must push back on the current. Flip the current and the rod jumps the other way.

Activity 12.7 — aluminium rod between magnet poles
Current A → B

The rod is displaced. Reverse the current, or interchange the magnet poles, and the displacement reverses — so the force depends on the direction of both the current and the field.

Fleming's left-hand rule
What changes the force
  • The force is largest when the current is at right angles to the magnetic field.
  • Reversing the current reverses the force.
  • Reversing the field (interchanging poles) also reverses the force.
  • With both perpendicular, the force is perpendicular to both — given by Fleming's left-hand rule.
  • Devices using this effect: electric motor, electric generator, loudspeakers, microphones and measuring instruments.
Why a rheostat in the circuit? To change the current and show that a larger current gives a larger displacement.
Section 12.4

Domestic electric circuits

220 V, 50 Hz, three wires and one very important green one. Tap any part of the house wiring.

Tap a part

Two separate circuits keep high-power and low-power appliances apart — so a geyser starting up does not dim your lights.

Fuse

The most important safety device. A thin wire that melts and breaks the circuit if current exceeds the rating — protecting against short-circuiting and overloading.

Overloading

Too many high-power appliances on one circuit draw more current than the wire can safely carry, heating the wires.

Short circuit

Live and neutral touch directly — resistance drops to almost zero and the current increases heavily.

Earthing

The green wire connects a metallic body to earth, giving leakage current a low-resistance path — so the user does not get a severe shock.

NCERT exercise — the oven problem

A 2 kW oven is used on a domestic circuit of 220 V with a 5 A rating. What happens?

I = P / V = 2000 W ÷ 220 V ≈ 9.1 A

9.1 A is far more than the 5 A rating → the circuit is overloaded. The fuse will melt (or the MCB will trip) and the circuit breaks — otherwise the wiring would overheat and could cause a fire.

Beyond the current NCERT

Motor, induction & generator

These sections were removed from the rationalised NCERT textbook, but many schools still teach them and they explain how the chapter is used in real machines.

Electric motor

Current in the coil's two arms experiences opposite forces, so the coil turns. The split ring reverses the current in the coil every half turn, keeping it rotating the same way.

Electromagnetic induction

Move the magnet towards the coil and the galvanometer deflects; hold it still and the deflection stops; pull it away and the deflection reverses. Only relative motion matters.
