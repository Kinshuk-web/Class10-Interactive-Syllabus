Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

The idea of development or progress has always been with us. We have aspirations about what we would like to do and how we would like to live, and ideas about what a country should be like. Development involves thinking about these questions and about the ways in which we can work towards achieving these goals.

The chapter begins with different people, different goals — a landless labourer, a prosperous farmer, a girl from a rich family, an adivasi from the Narmada valley all want different things. It shows that what may be development for one may not be development for another; it may even be destructive for the other.

It then asks how countries can be compared. Income is the most common measure, and per capita income (average income) is used by the World Bank. But averages hide disparities, and income alone cannot buy a pollution-free environment, good health or education — so health and education indicators and the UNDP’s Human Development Report are also used.

The last section raises sustainability: groundwater overuse in India and the world’s crude oil reserves show that present levels of development may not be sustainable. “We have not inherited the world from our forefathers — we have borrowed it from our children.”

Development

Thinking about the goals people and countries want, and how to achieve them; goals differ between persons and can conflict.

Per capita income

The total income of a country divided by its total population — the average income used by the World Bank to classify countries.

World Bank classes (2024)

High income: US$66,500 and above; low income: about US$2,300 or less; India is a low middle income country with per capita income of about US$11,000.

IMR

Number of children dying before age one per 1,000 live births in a year.

Literacy rate

Proportion of literate people in the 7-and-above age group.

HDI

The Human Development Index of the UNDP compares countries on education, health and per capita income; India ranked 130 of 193 (2021–22 ranking, HDR 2025).

Sustainability

Present development must not destroy the resource base for future generations; groundwater and crude oil are the chapter’s two examples.

What Development Means — Different People, Different Goals

The idea of development or progress has always been with us. We have aspirations or desires about what we would like to do and how we would like to live. Similarly, we have ideas about what a country should be like. What are the essential things that we require? Can life be better for all? How should people live together? Can there be more equality? Development involves thinking about these questions and about the ways in which we can work towards achieving these goals.

The way we live today is influenced by the past, and it is only through a democratic political process that these hopes and possibilities can be achieved in real life.

Table 1.1 — Developmental goals of different categories of persons (as filled in the chapter)
Category of personDevelopmental goals / aspirations
Landless rural labourersMore days of work and better wages; the local school should provide quality education for their children; there should be no social discrimination and they too can become leaders in the village.
Prosperous farmers from PunjabAssured high family income through higher support prices for their crops and through hardworking and cheap labourers; they should be able to settle their children abroad.
Farmers who depend only on rain for growing cropsA good monsoon, water for irrigation, and a fair price for the crop so that the family can live securely.
A rural woman from a land-owning familyShe gets as much freedom as her brother and is able to decide what she wants to do in life; she is able to pursue her studies abroad.
Urban unemployed youthA job with a regular income, security and the chance to use his education.
A boy from a rich urban familyA good education, freedom to choose his career and a high standard of living.
A girl from a rich urban familyAs much freedom as her brother, the ability to decide her own life, and to study abroad.
An adivasi from Narmada valleyTo keep his land and forest, not be displaced by a dam, and to have schools and health facilities.

Do all of these persons have the same notion of development or progress? Most likely not. Each one of them seeks different things — things that are most important for them, that can fulfil their aspirations or desires.

Goals can conflict

At times, two persons or groups of persons may seek things which are conflicting. A girl expects as much freedom and opportunity as her brother, and that he also shares in the household work; her brother may not like this. Similarly, to get more electricity, industrialists may want more dams — but this may submerge the land and disrupt the lives of people who are displaced, such as tribals, who might resent this and prefer small check dams or tanks to irrigate their land.

Key conclusion

So two things are quite clear: one, different persons can have different developmental goals and two, what may be development for one may not be development for the other. It may even be destructive for the other.

Teacher explanation

In the chapter’s photograph: a demonstration meeting against raising the height of the Sardar Sarovar Dam on the Narmada River is used as the example of conflicting goals — electricity and irrigation for some, displacement for others.

Income and Other Goals

If you go over Table 1.1 again, you will notice one common thing: what people desire are regular work, better wages, and a decent price for their crops or other products that they produce. In other words, they want more income.

Besides seeking more income, people also seek things like equal treatment, freedom, security and respect of others. They resent discrimination. All these are important goals. In fact, in some cases these may be more important than more income or more consumption, because material goods are not all that you need to live.

Money, or material things that one can buy with it, is one factor on which our life depends. But the quality of our life also depends on non-material things. If that is not obvious, think of the role of your friends in your life — you may desire their friendship. Similarly, there are many things that are not easily measured but they mean a lot to our lives. These are often ignored. However, it will be wrong to conclude that what cannot be measured is not important.

Consider another example. If you get a job in a far-off place, before accepting it you would try to consider many factors apart from income — facilities for your family, working atmosphere, or opportunity to learn. A job may give less pay but offer regular employment that enhances your sense of security; another may offer high pay but no job security and leave no time for family, reducing your sense of security and freedom.

Similarly, for development people look at a mix of goals. If women are engaged in paid work, their dignity in the household and society increases. But it is also true that if there is respect for women there would be more sharing of housework and greater acceptance of women working outside; a safe and secure environment may allow more women to take up a variety of jobs or run a business.

Key conclusion

Hence the developmental goals that people have are not only about better income but also about other important things in life.

National Development

If individuals seek different goals, then their notion of national development is also likely to be different. Different students in a class, asked what India should do for development, will give different answers — and you might yourself think of many different answers and not be too sure of any of them.

It is very important to keep in mind that different persons could have different as well as conflicting notions of a country’s development. However, can all the ideas be considered equally important? If there are conflicts, how does one decide? What would be a fair and just path for all? We also have to think whether there is a better way of doing things — would the idea benefit a large number of people or only a small group? National development means thinking about these questions.

Activity 1

Activity 1 — the Abidjan case: a vessel dumped 500 tonnes of liquid toxic wastes into open-air dumps in a city and in the surrounding sea. This happened in Abidjan in Ivory Coast, a country in Africa. The fumes caused nausea, skin rashes, fainting and diarrhoea; after a month seven persons were dead, twenty were in hospital and twenty-six thousand were treated for symptoms of poisoning. A multinational company dealing in petroleum and metals had contracted a local company of Ivory Coast to dispose of the toxic waste from its ship.

Teacher explanation

Questions on the Abidjan case: (i) Who are the people who benefited and who did not? (ii) What should be the developmental goal for this country? — The company saved the cost of safe disposal; the local contractor earned a fee; the people of Abidjan paid with their health and lives. A fair developmental goal would rule out such disposal altogether and require safe treatment of waste.

How to Compare Different Countries or States?

If development can mean different things, how come some countries are generally called developed and others under-developed? When we compare different things, they could have similarities as well as differences, and the criterion we use depends on the purpose of comparison — we use different criteria to choose a sports team, a debate team or a team to organise a picnic.

This is true of development too. For comparing countries, their income is considered to be one of the most important attributes. Countries with higher income are considered more developed than others with less income, based on the understanding that more income means more of all things that human beings need.

The income of a country is the income of all the residents of the country — the total income. But for comparison between countries, total income is not such a useful measure, since countries have different populations. Comparing total income will not tell us what an average person is likely to earn. Hence we compare the average income, which is the total income of the country divided by its total population. The average income is also called per capita income.

The rich countries, excluding countries of the Middle East and certain other small countries, are generally called developed countries.

Average Income — Useful but It Hides Disparities

While averages are useful for comparison, they also hide disparities. Consider two countries, A and B, assumed for simplicity to have only five citizens each.

Table 1.2 — Comparison of two countries (monthly incomes of citizens, in rupees)
CountryIIIIIIIVVAverage
Country A9,50010,5009,80010,00010,20010,000
Country B50050050050048,00010,000

Both countries have identical average income. Yet most of us would prefer to live in country A, because it has a more equitable distribution: people are neither very rich nor extremely poor. In country B most citizens are poor and one person is extremely rich. Hence, while average income is useful for comparison, it does not tell us how this income is distributed among people.

Teacher explanation

Work it out: (9,500 + 10,500 + 9,800 + 10,000 + 10,200) ÷ 5 = Rs 10,000. And (500 + 500 + 500 + 500 + 48,000) ÷ 5 = Rs 10,000. Same average, very different lives.

Definitions

Terms used in the table (NCERT explanations): Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) indicates the number of children that die before the age of one year as a proportion of 1,000 live children born in that particular year. Literacy Rate measures the proportion of literate population in the 7-and-above age group. Net Attendance Ratio is the total number of children of the age group 15–17 years attending school as a percentage of the total number of children in the same age group.

In Kerala, out of 1,000 children born, 6 died before completing one year of age, but in Haryana the proportion was 28 — nearly three times more than Kerala — even though Haryana’s per capita income is higher. How is it that the average person in Haryana has more income than the average person in Kerala but lags behind in these crucial areas? The reason is that money in your pocket cannot buy all the goods and services that you may need to live well. Income by itself is not a completely adequate indicator of material goods and services that citizens are able to use.

For example, normally your money cannot buy you a pollution-free environment or ensure that you get unadulterated medicines, unless you can afford to shift to a community that already has all these things. Money may also not be able to protect you from infectious diseases, unless the whole of your community takes preventive steps.

The last column shows that about one-third of the children aged 15–17 years in Bihar are not attending school in secondary classes — if you went to school in Bihar nearly one-third of your class friends would be missing.

Public Facilities

For many of the important things in life the best way, also the cheapest way, is to provide these goods and services collectively. Will it be cheaper to have collective security for the whole locality or for each house to have its own security staff? What if no one other than you in your village or locality were interested in studying — would you be able to study? Not unless your parents could afford to send you to a private school elsewhere. So you are able to study because many other children also want to study and because many people believe the government should open schools and provide other facilities.

Even now, in many areas, children — particularly girls — are not able to go to high school because the government or society has not provided adequate facilities. Most babies require basic healthcare; Kerala has a low Infant Mortality Rate because it has adequate provision of basic health and educational facilities. Similarly, in some states the Public Distribution System (PDS) functions well, and the health and nutritional status of people of such states is certainly likely to be better.

Human Development Report

Once it is realised that even though the level of income is important, it is an inadequate measure of the level of development, we begin to think of other criteria. There could be a long list, but then it would not be so useful. What we need is a small number of the most important things. Health and education indicators are among them, and over the past decade or so they have come to be widely used along with income.

The Human Development Report published by the UNDP compares countries based on the educational levels of the people, their health status and per capita income. By pre-fixing “Human” to development, it makes clear that what is important in development is what is happening to citizens of a country — it is people, their health and their well-being, that is most important.

Sustainability of Development

Suppose a particular country is quite developed. We would certainly like this level of development to go up further or at least be maintained for future generations. However, since the second half of the twentieth century, a number of scientists have been warning that the present type and levels of development are not sustainable.

Source extract

Example 1 — Groundwater in India: “Recent evidence suggests that the groundwater is under serious threat of overuse in many parts of the country. About 300 districts have reported a water level decline of over 4 metres during the past 20 years. Nearly one-third of the country is overusing their groundwater reserves. In another 25 years, 60 per cent of the country would be doing the same if the present way of using this resource continues. Groundwater overuse is particularly found in the agriculturally prosperous regions of Punjab and western U.P., hard rock plateau areas of central and south India, some coastal areas and the rapidly growing urban settlements.”

Groundwater is an example of a renewable resource: these resources are replenished by nature, as in the case of crops and plants. However, even these resources may be overused — if we use more groundwater than is being replenished by rain, we are overusing the resource.

Source extract

Example 2 — Exhaustion of natural resources: crude oil that we extract from the earth is a non-renewable resource. We may find a source of oil that we did not know of earlier — explorations are being undertaken all the time — but over time even this will get exhausted.

The table gives an estimate of reserves of crude oil and, more importantly, tells us for how many years the stock will last if people continue to extract it at the present rate. For the world as a whole the reserves would last only about 50 years more. Different countries face different situations: countries like India depend on importing oil from abroad because they do not have enough stocks of their own, and if prices of oil increase this becomes a burden for everyone. There are countries like the USA which have low reserves and hence want to secure oil through military or economic power.

“We have not inherited the world from our forefathers — we have borrowed it from our children.”

— Quoted in the chapter on sustainability

Non-renewable resources are those which will get exhausted after a few years of use. We have a fixed stock on earth which cannot be replenished; we do discover new resources that we did not know of earlier, and new sources add to the stock, but over time even this will get exhausted. The question of sustainability of development raises many fundamentally new issues about the nature and process of development.

Consequences of environmental degradation do not respect national or state boundaries; this issue is no longer region- or nation-specific. Our future is linked together. Sustainability of development is comparatively a new area of knowledge in which scientists, economists, philosophers and other social scientists are working together.

In general, the question of development or progress is perennial. At all times, as members of society and as individuals, we need to ask where we want to go, what we wish to become and what our goals are. So the debate on development continues.

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

Why do different persons have different notions of development? Which of the following explanations is more important and why? (a) Because people are different. (b) Because life situations of persons are different.

Model answer

(b) Because life situations of persons are different is the more important explanation. People seek what is most important for them, and that depends on the circumstances they live in — a landless labourer wants work and wages, a prosperous farmer wants higher support prices, a girl from a rich family wants the same freedom as her brother. Being “different” as individuals matters, but the situation explains the content of the goal.

2.

Do the following two statements mean the same? Justify your answer. (a) People have different developmental goals. (b) People have conflicting developmental goals.

Model answer

No. Statement (a) only says goals differ. Statement (b) says the goals cannot both be satisfied — one person’s development may damage another’s. A girl wanting the same freedom as her brother, and her brother not wanting to share housework, is a conflict; industrialists wanting more dams and tribals wanting to keep their land is another. Conflict is a stronger and narrower claim than difference.

3.

Give some examples where factors other than income are important aspects of our lives.

Model answer

Equal treatment and freedom from discrimination; security of employment; respect from others; friendship; a pollution-free environment; unadulterated medicines; protection from infectious disease through community action; good schools and health facilities; safe drinking water. A job may pay less but give security, or pay more but leave no time for family.

4.

Explain some of the important ideas of the above section in your own words.

Model answer

(i) Development means different things to different people because their situations differ. (ii) Goals can conflict, so what is development for one may be destructive for another. (iii) Besides income, people value equal treatment, freedom, security and respect. (iv) Because individual goals differ, notions of national development also differ, and we must ask what is fair and just and whether an idea benefits many or a few.

5.

Discuss the situations in Activity 1: (i) Who are the people who benefited and who did not? (ii) What should be the developmental goal for this country?

Model answer

(i) The multinational company benefited by avoiding the cost of safe disposal, and the local contractor earned a fee. Those who did not benefit were the people of Abidjan — seven died, twenty were hospitalised and twenty-six thousand were treated for poisoning, and the soil and sea were polluted. (ii) The developmental goal should be safe treatment and disposal of hazardous waste, enforcement of environmental law, compensation for victims, and a say for local people in decisions that affect their health.

6.

Give three examples where an average is used for comparing situations.

Model answer

Average income (per capita income) of countries; average marks of a class or a school; average rainfall of a region; average height or BMI of students. In each case one number summarises many observations — and hides how the values are spread.

7.

Why do you think average income is an important criterion for development? Explain.

Model answer

Because more income generally means more of the things people need — food, housing, health care, education. It is also easy to compare across countries once converted into a common unit. But it is only one criterion: it says nothing about distribution, health, education or environment.

8.

Besides size of per capita income, what other property of income is important in comparing two or more societies?

Model answer

Its distribution. In Table 1.2 both countries have an average of Rs 10,000, but in country B four people earn Rs 500 while one earns Rs 48,000. A society with a more equitable distribution is preferred even at the same average.

9.

Suppose records show that the average income in a country has been increasing over a period of time. From this, can we conclude that all sections of the economy have become better? Illustrate your answer with an example.

Model answer

No. The average can rise while many people remain poor — if the incomes of the richest rise sharply, the average goes up even though the poor gain little. Table 1.2 shows this in one snapshot: identical averages with completely different lives. Rising averages must be read with data on distribution, health and education.

10.

From the text, find out the per capita income level of about 10–15 low-income countries as per World Development Reports.

Model answer

The chapter gives the criterion — low income countries had a per capita income of about US$2,300 or less in 2024 — and India’s figure of about US$11,000. For a current list of individual low-income countries, look up the latest World Development Report or the World Bank’s data site, as the list changes every year.

11.

Write a paragraph on your notion of what India should do, or achieve, to become a developed country.

Model answer

Model answer: India should raise per capita income but, equally, invest in health and education so that the gains are shared. That means good government schools and primary health centres, safe drinking water and sanitation, and social security for workers in the unorganised sector. Growth must come with equity — reducing the gap between states such as Kerala and Bihar — and with sustainability, so that groundwater, forests and energy are not used up. (Student answers may vary.)

12.

Look at data in Tables 1.3 and 1.4. Is Haryana ahead of Kerala in literacy rate etc., as it is in terms of per capita income?

Model answer

No. Haryana’s per capita income (Rs 3,25,759) is higher than Kerala’s (Rs 2,81,001), but Kerala is ahead on every other indicator: IMR 6 against 28, literacy 94% against 82%, and net attendance 94 against 73. This is the chapter’s main point — income alone does not measure development.

13.

Think of other examples where collective provision of goods and services is cheaper than individual provision.

Model answer

Street lighting, drainage and sewerage, public parks, police and fire services, vaccination drives, public transport, community water tanks and rainwater harvesting structures, and public schools and libraries. In each case one shared facility serves many households at far lower cost than each household providing its own.

14.

Does availability of good health and educational facilities depend only on the amount of money spent by the government on these facilities? What other factors could be relevant?

Model answer

No. Money matters, but so do how it is spent and who is reached: the number and training of teachers and health workers, whether they actually attend, distance and transport, the quality of buildings and equipment, accountability and supervision, and the demand from families — for example whether girls are allowed to travel to school. Kerala’s low IMR reflects functioning facilities, not only spending.

15.

In a study it was found that in Tamil Nadu 90 per cent of the people living in rural areas used a ration shop, whereas in West Bengal only 35 per cent did so. Where would people be better off and why?

Model answer

In Tamil Nadu, where the Public Distribution System functions well and 90 per cent use it — the poor get subsidised food, so their health and nutritional status is likely to be better. A low figure like 35 per cent may mean the system is not working, not that people do not need it.

16.

Why is groundwater overused, and can there be development without overuse?

Model answer

Because farmers in prosperous regions such as Punjab and western U.P., industry and fast-growing cities pump more water than rain replenishes, since water is free or cheap and each user gains by pumping more. Development without overuse is possible: grow less water-intensive crops, use drip and sprinkler irrigation, recharge groundwater through harvesting structures, price or regulate extraction, and shift to crops suited to local rainfall.

17.

Is crude oil essential for the development process in a country? Discuss.

Model answer

At present, largely yes — it fuels transport, industry, fertilisers and plastics. But the reserves are finite (the world’s would last about 47–50 years at present rates) and India must import most of its oil, so rising prices burden the economy. That makes a shift to renewable energy and efficiency not just desirable but necessary for continued development.

18.

India has to import crude oil. What problems do you anticipate for the country looking at the above situation?

Model answer

A heavy import bill and vulnerability to price rise; pressure on foreign exchange and on the balance of payments; higher costs for transport, farmers (fertiliser, diesel) and industry; and uncertainty of supply. It also strengthens the case for energy efficiency, public transport and renewable sources.

3.
NCERT exercise2 marks

Assume there are four families in a country. The average per capita income of these families is Rs 5,000. If the income of three families is Rs 4,000, Rs 7,000 and Rs 3,000 respectively, what is the income of the fourth family? (i) Rs 7,500 (ii) Rs 3,000 (iii) Rs 2,000 (iv) Rs 6,000

Model answer

(iv) Rs 6,000. Total income = 5,000 × 4 = Rs 20,000. Income of three families = 4,000 + 7,000 + 3,000 = Rs 14,000. Fourth family = 20,000 − 14,000 = Rs 6,000.

4.
NCERT exercise3 marks

What is the main criterion used by the World Bank in classifying different countries? What are the limitations of this criterion, if any?

Model answer

The World Bank uses per capita income (average income) in its World Development Reports: countries with per capita income of US$66,500 and above in 2024 are high income, and those with about US$2,300 or less are low income. Limitations: (i) averages hide disparities — the same average can conceal great inequality (Table 1.2); (ii) income ignores health, education and other aspects of life; (iii) money cannot buy a pollution-free environment, unadulterated medicines or protection from infectious disease; (iv) it says nothing about whether development is sustainable.

5.
NCERT exercise3 marks

In what respects is the criterion used by the UNDP for measuring development different from the one used by the World Bank?

Model answer

The World Bank uses only per capita income. The UNDP’s Human Development Report compares countries on educational levels, health status and per capita income together, and ranks them by the Human Development Index. By pre-fixing “Human”, it makes clear that what matters is what is happening to people — their health and well-being — not income alone.

6.
NCERT exercise3 marks

Why do we use averages? Are there any limitations to their use? Illustrate with your own examples related to development.

Model answer

Averages reduce a large number of observations to one comparable number, which is useful when comparing countries or states of different sizes — total income would be misleading because populations differ. Limitations: an average hides how the value is distributed. Table 1.2 shows two countries with an average income of Rs 10,000 where one has four poor citizens and one very rich citizen. Similarly, an average class mark of 60 can hide students scoring 20 and 95, and an average rainfall can hide a season of floods and drought.

NCERT exercise5 marks

Kerala, with lower per capita income, has a better human development ranking than Haryana. Hence, per capita income is not a useful criterion at all and should not be used to compare states. Do you agree? Discuss.

The correct conclusion is that income is a necessary but not sufficient measure, and must be used along with health and education indicators, as the UNDP does.

NCERT exercise3 marks

Find out the present sources of energy that are used by the people in India. What could be the other possibilities fifty years from now?

Present sources: coal (the largest source for electricity), petroleum products, natural gas, hydel power, nuclear power, and — especially in rural homes — firewood and cattle dung cake, along with growing use of solar and wind. Fifty years from now, with oil and coal reserves depleted and prices high, India will rely much more on solar, wind, hydel, biogas, nuclear and possibly hydrogen, plus much greater energy efficiency. Since reserves of crude oil would last only about 50 years at present rates, this shift is a condition of continued development.

The earth’s resources are enough if used to meet everyone’s needs, but not if a few consume without limit — as with groundwater overuse by a few users, or the world’s oil being extracted at a rate that leaves only about 50 years of reserves. Development that satisfies greed is not sustainable and is also unjust, because it deprives others living now and future generations.

Important Questions and Answers

Board-style practice

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

“Different persons can have different developmental goals.” Explain with three examples. (3 marks)

Model answer

(i) A landless rural labourer wants more days of work and better wages, quality education for his children in the local school, and an end to social discrimination. (ii) A prosperous farmer from Punjab wants a high family income through higher support prices and cheap labour, and to settle his children abroad. (iii) A girl from a rich urban family wants as much freedom as her brother and the ability to decide her own life and study abroad. Each seeks what is most important in their own situation.

2.

“What may be development for one may be destructive for another.” Justify with two examples. (3 marks)

Model answer

(i) Industrialists want more dams for electricity, but the reservoirs submerge land and displace tribals, who may prefer small check dams or tanks. (ii) A girl wants the same freedom as her brother and a share of housework by him; her brother may not like this. In both cases one person’s development reduces another’s well-being.

3.

Why is per capita income used to compare countries? What are its limitations? (5 marks)

Model answer

Per capita income — total income divided by population — is used because total income is misleading when populations differ, and because more income generally means more of the things people need. Its limitations: (i) it is an average, so it hides disparities in distribution (Table 1.2 shows two countries with the same average but very different lives); (ii) it ignores health, education, nutrition and other aspects of well-being — Haryana has higher per capita income than Kerala but a much higher IMR and lower literacy; (iii) money cannot buy a pollution-free environment, unadulterated medicines or protection from infectious disease; (iv) it says nothing about whether development is sustainable.

4.

Explain the World Bank’s criterion of classifying countries. (3 marks)

Model answer

In its World Development Reports the World Bank classifies countries by per capita income. In 2024, countries with per capita income of US$66,500 per annum and above were called high income or rich countries, and those with about US$2,300 or less were low-income countries. India, with per capita income of about US$11,000 in 2024, is a low middle income country. The rich countries, excluding the Middle East and certain other small countries, are generally called developed countries.

5.

How does the UNDP measure development? How is it different from the World Bank’s method? (3 marks)

Model answer

The UNDP’s Human Development Report compares countries on the educational levels of the people, their health status and per capita income, and ranks them by the Human Development Index. The World Bank uses only per capita income. The UNDP method therefore captures what is happening to people — their health and well-being — rather than income alone, which is why Kerala can rank well despite lower income.

6.

Define IMR, literacy rate and net attendance ratio. (3 marks)

Model answer

IMR — the number of children who die before the age of one year as a proportion of 1,000 live children born in that year. Literacy rate — the proportion of literate population in the 7-and-above age group. Net attendance ratio — the number of children of the 15–17 age group attending school as a percentage of the total number of children in that age group.

7.

Why are public facilities important? Give examples. (3 marks)

Model answer

Because for many important things the best and cheapest way is collective provision. Individual money cannot buy a pollution-free environment or protection from infectious disease unless the whole community acts; and it is cheaper to have collective security or a public school than for every house to arrange its own. Kerala’s low IMR is due to adequate basic health and educational facilities, and states where the PDS functions well have better health and nutrition.

8.

Explain the two examples of sustainability given in the chapter. (5 marks)

Model answer

Groundwater: about 300 districts have reported a water-level decline of over 4 metres in 20 years; nearly one-third of the country is overusing its groundwater, and in another 25 years 60 per cent would be doing so if present use continues. Overuse is concentrated in agriculturally prosperous Punjab and western U.P., hard-rock plateau areas of central and south India, some coastal areas and growing cities. Even a renewable resource can be overused if use exceeds replenishment. Crude oil: a non-renewable resource with a fixed stock; world reserves of 1,732 thousand million barrels would last only about 47–50 years at present rates (Middle East 836, lasting 70 years; USA 69, lasting 10.5 years). India must import oil, so price rise hurts; the USA with low reserves seeks to secure oil through military or economic power. New discoveries add to the stock, but over time even this will be exhausted.

9.

Why is sustainability of development a matter of global concern? (3 marks)

Model answer

Because the consequences of environmental degradation do not respect national or state boundaries — this issue is no longer region- or nation-specific, and our future is linked together. Scientists have warned since the second half of the twentieth century that present types and levels of development are not sustainable, and sustainability is now a new area of knowledge in which scientists, economists, philosophers and other social scientists work together.

10.

Why do we need to think about national development, and what questions does it involve? (3 marks)

Model answer

Because individuals have different and conflicting goals, so their notions of national development also differ. National development means asking whether all ideas can be considered equally important, how to decide when there are conflicts, what would be a fair and just path for all, whether there is a better way of doing things, and whether an idea benefits a large number of people or only a small group.

11.

Explain the Abidjan case and what it teaches about development. (3 marks)

Model answer

A vessel dumped 500 tonnes of liquid toxic waste in open-air dumps in Abidjan, Ivory Coast; fumes caused nausea, rashes, fainting and diarrhoea, and after a month seven people were dead, twenty were in hospital and twenty-six thousand were treated for poisoning. A multinational petroleum and metals company had contracted a local company to dispose of the waste. It shows that what benefited the company destroyed the lives of local people — development for one can be destructive for another, and rules are needed to prevent it.

Calculate the average income of both countries and state whether they are equal.

Model answer

Country A: (9,500 + 10,500 + 9,800 + 10,000 + 10,200) ÷ 5 = Rs 10,000. Country B: (500 + 500 + 500 + 500 + 48,000) ÷ 5 = Rs 10,000. Yes, both averages are equal.

2.

In which country would you prefer to live if citizenship were decided by lottery, and why?

Model answer

In country A, because the distribution is more equitable — people are neither very rich nor extremely poor. In country B, if you drew any number but the fifth, you would be poor.

3.

What does this show about the use of averages in measuring development?

Model answer

That an average is useful for comparison but hides disparities. Development policy must look at distribution, not only at the average.

Source 3 — The Abidjan toxic waste case

Case study

A vessel dumped 500 tonnes of liquid toxic wastes into open-air dumps in a city and in the surrounding sea. This happened in a city called Abidjan in Ivory Coast, a country in Africa. The fumes from the highly toxic waste caused nausea, skin rashes, fainting, diarrhoea etc. After a month seven persons were dead, twenty in hospital and twenty-six thousand treated for symptoms of poisoning. A multinational company dealing in petroleum and metals had contracted a local company of Ivory Coast to dispose of the toxic waste from its ship.

1.

Who benefited from this arrangement?

Model answer

The multinational company, which avoided the cost of safe disposal, and the local contractor, which was paid a fee.

2.

Who bore the cost?

Model answer

The people of Abidjan: seven dead, twenty hospitalised, twenty-six thousand treated, and the soil and sea polluted.

3.

What does the case show about the idea that “what may be development for one may not be development for the other”?

Model answer

It shows the idea in its starkest form: the disposal was profitable for the company, but it was destructive — even lethal — for the people living there. Rules and regulations are needed so that one group’s gain cannot be another group’s poisoning.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

A district’s average income has doubled in ten years, yet its infant mortality rate has not fallen. What could explain this, and what should the district do?

Model answer

The average could have risen because a few earners — industry, contractors, rich households — gained, while most families saw little change, so health outcomes did not improve. Or the money may not have been spent on public facilities: there may be no functioning primary health centre, no safe water, no immunisation drive. The district should look at distribution and at health and education indicators, invest in public facilities, and ensure collective provision of water, sanitation and vaccination. (Model answer.)

2.

Why can a country be “rich” by the World Bank’s definition yet have poor human development? Give an example from the chapter and explain.

Model answer

Because the World Bank measures only per capita income. Haryana has a higher per capita income than Kerala but a much higher IMR (28 against 6), lower literacy (82% against 94%) and lower school attendance (73 against 94). Income does not automatically buy health or education; those depend on public facilities, on how money is spent and on whether the whole community acts — money cannot buy a pollution-free environment or protection from infectious disease on its own. (Model answer.)

3.

“We have not inherited the world from our forefathers — we have borrowed it from our children.” Explain this statement using both examples of the chapter.

Model answer

It says we hold the earth in trust for future generations. Groundwater: we are extracting more than rain replenishes, with 300 districts showing a fall of over 4 metres in 20 years and one-third of the country overusing reserves; in 25 years 60 per cent could be doing so. Crude oil: a fixed stock of 1,732 thousand million barrels that would last only about 50 years at present rates. Borrowing from our children means using up what they will need. (Model answer.)

4.

Should India stop using oil altogether to become sustainable? Argue both sides briefly and give your conclusion.

Model answer

For: oil is non-renewable, reserves would last about 50 years, and India imports most of it, so price rise burdens the economy and emissions damage the environment. Against: transport, industry, fertilisers and plastics still depend on oil, and an immediate stop would collapse production and employment. Conclusion: a planned transition — efficiency, public transport, renewables, and reduced dependence — rather than an abrupt stop. (Model answer; reasoned answers may differ.)

5.

Your class is asked to suggest three indicators to judge your own school’s “development”. Which would you choose and why?

Model answer

For example: (i) learning outcomes — average marks plus the share of students scoring below minimum levels, since an average hides the weakest; (ii) attendance and dropout rate, especially of girls; (iii) facilities — classrooms per student, drinking water, toilets, library and playground. Each combines an average with a distribution measure, exactly the lesson of Table 1.2. (Model answer.)

6.

Why is “national development” harder to define than an individual’s development?

Model answer

Because a country contains people with different and conflicting goals — a dam is development for an industrialist and destruction for a displaced adivasi. So national development must weigh whose goals count, ask what is fair and just, and judge whether an idea benefits many or only a few. There is no single measure that settles these questions. (Model answer.)

Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

Development means thinking about the goals people and countries want, and the ways to achieve them. Different people have different goals because their situations differ, and goals can conflict — a dam that gives electricity may displace tribals. Besides income, people value equal treatment, freedom, security and respect. Because individual goals differ, notions of national development differ too, and we must ask what is fair and just and whether an idea benefits many people or only a few.

For comparing countries, income is the most common attribute, and because populations differ we compare per capita income — the average income. The World Bank classifies countries by it: in 2024, US$66,500 and above was high income, about US$2,300 or less low income, and India at about US$11,000 is a low middle income country. But averages hide disparities, and income alone is inadequate: Haryana has higher per capita income than Kerala yet a much higher infant mortality rate and lower literacy and school attendance, because money cannot buy a clean environment, unadulterated medicines or protection from disease — many important things are best provided collectively as public facilities.

The UNDP’s Human Development Report therefore compares countries on education, health and per capita income through the Human Development Index; India ranked 130 of 193. Finally, the chapter asks whether present development is sustainable: groundwater is being overused in about one-third of the country, and the world’s crude oil reserves would last only about fifty years at present rates. “We have not inherited the world from our forefathers — we have borrowed it from our children.”
