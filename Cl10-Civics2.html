Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about, at a glance

In the previous chapter we noted that vertical division of power among different levels of government is one of the major forms of power-sharing. This chapter focuses on that form — federalism.

Federalism is a system in which power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units. A federation usually has two levels: a government for the entire country responsible for a few subjects of common national interest, and governments at the level of provinces or states that look after most day-to-day administration. Both levels enjoy their power independent of the other. Its dual objective is to safeguard and promote the unity of the country while accommodating regional diversity, which requires both mutual trust and an agreement to live together.

Federations have formed by two routes: “coming together” federations such as the USA, Switzerland and Australia, where independent states pooled sovereignty and retained identity, and “holding together” federations such as India, Spain and Belgium, where a large country divided its power and the central government tends to be stronger.

India’s Constitution declared India a Union of States. Though it did not use the word federation, the Union is based on federal principles: three lists of subjects, special provisions for some states, Union Territories, a rigid amendment procedure and a judiciary that umpires disputes. But constitutional provisions are not sufficient — the success of Indian federalism owes much to democratic politics: linguistic states, a flexible language policy and the restructuring of Centre–State relations after 1990. The chapter ends with decentralisation and the third tier of panchayats and municipalities.

Federalism

The area over which someone has legal authority — defined by geographical boundaries or by certain kinds of subjects.

Independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit, pooling sovereignty and retaining identity — USA, Switzerland, Australia.

“Holding together” federation

A large country divides its power between constituent states and the national government — India, Spain, Belgium.

Subjects of national importance — defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications, currency — on which only the Union Government can make laws.

State List

Subjects of state and local importance — police, trade, commerce, agriculture, irrigation — on which only the State Governments can make laws.

Subjects of common interest — education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption, succession — on which both can legislate, but the Union law prevails in a conflict.

What Is Federalism?

Recall the contrast between Belgium and Sri Lanka. One of the key changes made in the Constitution of Belgium was to reduce the power of the central government and give these powers to the regional governments. Regional governments existed even earlier, but their powers were given by the central government and could be withdrawn by it. The change in 1993 was that the regional governments were given constitutional powers that were no longer dependent on the central government. Thus Belgium shifted from a unitary to a federal form of government. Sri Lanka continues to be, for all practical purposes, a unitary system where the national government has all the powers; Tamil leaders want Sri Lanka to become federal.

Definition

Federalism is a system of government in which the power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units of the country. Usually a federation has two levels of government: one for the entire country, responsible for a few subjects of common national interest, and others at the level of provinces or states that look after much of the day-to-day administering of their state. Both levels enjoy their power independent of the other.

The federal system thus has dual objectives: to safeguard and promote the unity of the country, while at the same time accommodate regional diversity. Two aspects are therefore crucial: governments at different levels should agree to some rules of power-sharing, and they should trust that each would abide by its part of the agreement. An ideal federal system has both aspects — mutual trust and agreement to live together.

The exact balance of power between the central and state governments varies from one federation to another, depending mainly on the historical context in which the federation was formed.

The two routes to federation
RouteHow it happensExamplesBalance of power
“Coming together”Independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit, pooling sovereignty and retaining identity to increase securityUSA, Switzerland, AustraliaConstituent states usually have equal power and are strong vis-à-vis the federal government
“Holding together”A large country decides to divide its power between the constituent states and the national governmentIndia, Spain, BelgiumThe central government tends to be more powerful; units often have unequal powers and some get special powers

In this sense federations are contrasted with unitary governments. Under the unitary system, either there is only one level of government or the sub-units are subordinate to the central government, which can pass orders to the provincial or local government. But in a federal system the central government cannot order the state government to do something. State government has powers of its own for which it is not answerable to the central government. Both governments are separately answerable to the people.

Key Features of Federalism
  1. There are two or more levels (or tiers) of government.
  2. Different tiers govern the same citizens, but each tier has its own jurisdiction in specific matters of legislation, taxation and administration.
  3. The jurisdictions of the respective levels are specified in the constitution, so the existence and authority of each tier is constitutionally guaranteed.
  4. The fundamental provisions of the constitution cannot be unilaterally changed by one level of government; such changes require the consent of both levels.
  5. Courts have the power to interpret the constitution and the powers of different levels. The highest court acts as an umpire if disputes arise between levels of government.
  6. Sources of revenue for each level of government are clearly specified to ensure its financial autonomy.
Data point

Only 25 of the world’s 193 countries have federal political systems — yet their citizens make up 40 per cent of the world’s population, and most of the large countries of the world are federations.

What Makes India a Federal Country?

India emerged as an independent nation after a painful and bloody partition, and soon after Independence several princely states became part of the country. The Constitution declared India as a Union of States. Although it did not use the word federation, the Indian Union is based on the principles of federalism, and all seven features listed above apply to its provisions.

The Constitution originally provided for a two-tier system — the Union Government (the Central Government) representing the Union of India, and the State governments. Later a third tier was added in the form of panchayats and municipalities. As in any federation, these tiers enjoy separate jurisdiction, and the Constitution provides a three-fold distribution of legislative powers.

The three lists
ListSubjectsWho can make laws
Union ListSubjects of national importance: defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications, currency — a uniform policy is needed throughout the countryThe Union Government alone
State ListSubjects of state and local importance: police, trade, commerce, agriculture, irrigationThe State Governments alone
Concurrent ListSubjects of common interest: education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption, successionBoth — but if their laws conflict, the law made by the Union Government prevails
Residuary powersSubjects not in any list, such as computer software which came up after the Constitution was madeThe Union Government

Most federations formed by “holding together” do not give equal power to their constituent units, and all States in the Indian Union do not have identical powers. Some enjoy a special status: states such as Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh and Mizoram enjoy special powers under certain provisions of the Constitution (Article 371) due to their peculiar social and historical circumstances. These powers relate especially to the protection of land rights of indigenous peoples, their culture, and preferential employment in government services — Indians who are not permanent residents of such a State cannot buy land or a house there. Similar provisions exist for some other States.

There are also units of the Indian Union which enjoy very little power — areas too small to become an independent State but which could not be merged with any existing State. These are the Union Territories, such as Chandigarh, Lakshadweep or the capital city of Delhi. They do not have the powers of a State, and the Central Government has special powers in running them.

This sharing of power between the Union and the States is basic to the structure of the Constitution and is not easy to change. Parliament cannot on its own change the arrangement: any change has to be first passed by both Houses of Parliament with at least a two-thirds majority, and then ratified by the legislatures of at least half of the total States.

The judiciary plays an important role in overseeing the implementation of constitutional provisions and procedures. In case of any dispute about the division of powers, the High Courts and the Supreme Court make a decision. The Union and State Governments also have the power to raise resources by levying taxes, to carry on the government and the responsibilities assigned to each.

How Is Federalism Practised? — Linguistic States

Constitutional provisions are necessary for the success of federalism but not sufficient. If the federal experiment has succeeded in India, it is not merely because of clearly laid out constitutional provisions — the real success can be attributed to the nature of democratic politics, which ensured that the spirit of federalism, respect for diversity and a desire for living together became shared ideals.

The creation of linguistic States was the first and a major test for democratic politics. Comparing the political map of 1947 with that of 2019, many old States have vanished and many new ones created; areas, boundaries and names have changed. In 1947 the boundaries of several old States were changed to ensure that people who spoke the same language lived in the same State. Some States were created not on the basis of language but to recognise differences based on culture, ethnicity or geography — Nagaland, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand.

When the demand for linguistic States was raised, some national leaders feared it would lead to the disintegration of the country, and the Central Government resisted it for some time. But experience has shown that the formation of linguistic States has actually made the country more united, and has also made administration easier.

Carved out — Haryana from Punjab, Uttarakhand from Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand from Bihar, Chhattisgarh from Madhya Pradesh, Telangana from Andhra Pradesh.

Language Policy

A second test for the Indian federation is the language policy. Our Constitution did not give the status of national language to any one language. Hindi was identified as the official language, but Hindi is the mother tongue of only about 40 per cent of Indians, so there were many safeguards to protect other languages. Besides Hindi, there are 21 other languages recognised as Scheduled Languages by the Constitution. A candidate in an examination conducted for Central Government positions may opt to take the examination in any of these languages. States have their own official languages, and much government work takes place in the official language of the concerned State.

Unlike Sri Lanka, the leaders of our country adopted a very cautious attitude in spreading the use of Hindi. According to the Constitution, the use of English for official purposes was to stop in 1965; however, many non-Hindi-speaking States demanded that English continue. In Tamil Nadu this movement took a violent form. The Central Government responded by agreeing to continue the use of English along with Hindi for official purposes. Many critics think this solution favoured the English-speaking elite, and promotion of Hindi continues to be the official policy of the Government of India. But promotion does not mean that the Central Government can impose Hindi on States where people speak a different language. The flexibility shown by Indian political leaders helped the country avoid the kind of situation Sri Lanka found itself in.

Centre–State Relations

Restructuring the Centre–State relations is one more way in which federalism has been strengthened in practice. How the constitutional arrangements for sharing power work in reality depends to a large extent on how the ruling parties and leaders follow these arrangements.

For a long time the same party ruled both at the Centre and in most of the States. This meant that the State Governments did not exercise their rights as autonomous federal units. And as and when the ruling party at the State level was different, the parties that ruled at the Centre tried to undermine the power of the States. In those days the Central Government would often misuse the Constitution to dismiss State Governments controlled by rival parties. This undermined the spirit of federalism.

All this changed significantly after 1990. This period saw the rise of regional political parties in many States, and was the beginning of the era of coalition governments at the Centre. Since no single party got a clear majority in the Lok Sabha, the major national parties had to enter into alliances with many parties, including several regional parties, to form a government at the Centre. This led to a new culture of power sharing and respect for the autonomy of State Governments. The trend was supported by a major judgement of the Supreme Court that made it difficult for the Central Government to dismiss state governments in an arbitrary manner. Thus federal power sharing is more effective today than it was in the early years after the Constitution came into force.

Activity

Three test cases. (i) Pokharan, where India conducted its nuclear tests, lies in Rajasthan. Could the Government of Rajasthan prevent the Government of India from conducting the tests if it opposed the nuclear policy? — No: defence is a Union subject. (ii) If Sikkim plans new textbooks and the Union Government dislikes their style and content, must the State take permission? — Not permission, because education is in the Concurrent List and the State may legislate; but a Union law on the same matter would prevail. (iii) If the Chief Ministers of Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Odisha have different policies on the police response to naxalites, can the Prime Minister order them all to obey one policy? — No: police is a State subject, and the Union cannot order a State to do something.

Decentralisation in India

A vast country like India cannot be run only through two tiers. States in India are as large as independent countries of Europe — in population, Uttar Pradesh is bigger than Russia and Maharashtra is about as big as Germany — and many States are internally very diverse. There is thus a need for power sharing within these States, and for another tier of government below the State. This is the rationale for decentralisation of power, which produced the third tier of government, called local government.

Definition

Decentralisation: when power is taken away from central and state governments and given to local government. The basic idea is that a large number of problems and issues are best settled at the local level: people have better knowledge of problems in their localities, better ideas on where to spend money and how to manage things more efficiently, and it is possible for them to participate directly in decision-making — which inculcates a habit of democratic participation. Local government is the best way to realise the principle of local self-government.

The need for decentralisation was recognised in our Constitution, and there were several attempts to decentralise power to villages and towns. Panchayats in villages and municipalities in urban areas were set up in all the States. But these were directly under the control of state governments; elections were not held regularly; and local governments did not have powers or resources of their own. Thus there was very little decentralisation in effective terms.

The 1992 amendment

A major step was taken in 1992, when the Constitution was amended to make the third tier of democracy more powerful and effective.

  • It is now constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies.
  • Seats are reserved in the elected bodies and the executive heads of these institutions for the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes.
  • At least one-third of all positions are reserved for women.
  • An independent institution called the State Election Commission has been created in each State to conduct panchayat and municipal elections.
  • State governments are required to share some powers and revenue with local government bodies — the nature of sharing varies from State to State.

This new system of local government is the largest experiment in democracy conducted anywhere in the world. There are now about 36 lakh elected representatives in panchayats and municipalities all over the country — a number bigger than the population of many countries.

Constitutional status for local government has helped to deepen democracy and has increased women’s representation and voice. At the same time there are many difficulties: while elections are held regularly and enthusiastically, gram sabhas are not held regularly; most state governments have not transferred significant powers to local governments; and they have not given adequate resources. We are thus still a long way from realising the ideal of self-government.

Source extract

An experiment in Brazil. The city of Porto Alegre has combined decentralisation with participative democracy: alongside the municipal council, local inhabitants take real decisions for their city. The nearly 13 lakh people participate in making the budget, through ward meetings open to anyone living in the area, and city-wide meetings on issues affecting the whole city. About 20,000 people participate every year. The method has ensured that money cannot be spent only for the colonies where rich people live — buses now run to poor colonies, and builders cannot evict slum-dwellers without resettling them. In India, a similar experiment has taken place in some areas of Kerala, where ordinary people participated in planning the development of their locality.

NCERT In-text Questions and Answers

The questions printed inside the chapter

These are the “Can you…” and activity questions that appear inside the chapter text. Model answers are based on the chapter itself.

1.

What do we call the Indian government — Union, Federal or Central?

Model answer

All three names are used, and the Constitution’s own phrase is “Union of States”. It deliberately avoided the word “federation”, yet the system works on federal principles. “Central Government” and “Union Government” are used interchangeably in ordinary speech.

2.

Only 25 of the world’s 193 countries have federal political systems, yet their citizens make up 40 per cent of the world’s population. Can you notice an exception to the rule that most large countries are federations?

Model answer

China — one of the largest countries by area and population — is not a federation but a unitary system in which provinces have no constitutionally guaranteed autonomy. The chapter asks you to notice this exception on the map.

3.

Some Nepalese citizens discussed federalism: Khag Raj says it would lead to reservation of seats for caste groups as in India; Sarita says Nepal is not a big country and does not need federalism; Babu Lal hopes the Terai will get more autonomy with its own state government; Ram Ganesh likes it because powers earlier enjoyed by the king will now be exercised by elected representatives. What would you say to each, and whose understanding is wrong?

Model answer

Khag Raj confuses federalism with reservation — federalism is about dividing power between levels of government, and reservations are a separate choice India made for social groups. Sarita is wrong: Belgium is small and federal, and federalism is about diversity, not size. Babu Lal is right — federalism can give a region like the Terai its own government with real powers. Ram Ganesh is right too — federalism takes power away from a centralised authority, in Nepal’s case the monarchy, and gives it to elected bodies.

4.

If agriculture and commerce are state subjects, why do we have ministers of agriculture and commerce in the Union cabinet?

Model answer

Because the Union Government coordinates, funds and frames national policy — for example on minimum support prices, inter-state trade and exports — even though the power to legislate on agriculture and commerce rests with the States. Union ministers also represent India internationally and administer centrally sponsored schemes, without displacing the States’ legislative jurisdiction.

5.

Pokharan lies in Rajasthan. Could the Government of Rajasthan prevent the Government of India from conducting nuclear tests if it opposed the policy?

Model answer

No. Defence and atomic energy are Union subjects, and the Union Government alone can legislate and act on them. A State cannot veto a Union decision on a subject in the Union List — this is exactly what distinguishes a federation from a unitary system in the other direction.

6.

If Sikkim plans new textbooks and the Union Government does not like their style and content, does the State need permission before launching them?

Model answer

Education is in the Concurrent List, so the State may legislate and act without seeking permission. But if a Union law on the same subject conflicts with the State law, the Union law prevails — so the Union could, by legislation, set standards that the State must follow.

7.

Can the Prime Minister order the Chief Ministers of Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Odisha to follow one policy on the police response to naxalites?

Model answer

No. Police is a State subject and the Union cannot order a State government to do something. The Union can coordinate, advise, provide central forces in specific circumstances and share intelligence — but the constitutional power to direct the State police lies with the State.

8.

Has your village, town or city remained under the same State since Independence? Can you identify three States whose names changed, and three carved out of bigger States?

Model answer

Answers will vary for the first part. Names changed: Madras → Tamil Nadu, Mysore → Karnataka, Orissa → Odisha, Uttaranchal → Uttarakhand, Pondicherry → Puducherry. Carved out: Haryana from Punjab, Uttarakhand from Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand from Bihar, Chhattisgarh from Madhya Pradesh, Telangana from Andhra Pradesh.

9.

Make a bar or pie chart from the Scheduled Languages table; prepare a map of linguistic diversity by shading regions; find three languages spoken in India but not in the table.

Model answer

The chart will show Hindi as the tallest bar (43.63), then Bengali (8.03), Marathi (6.86), Telugu (6.70), Tamil (5.70) and so on. Languages spoken in India but not in the table include Bhili, Gondi, Tulu, Kodava, Khasi, Garo, Mizo and Bhotia. (Student activity.)

10.

Read the excerpts from Ramachandra Guha’s article and write a short note for or against his argument, using your own State as an example.

Model answer

Guha’s argument is that linguistic reorganisation strengthened Indian democracy by giving language groups a state of their own within the Union, so that language became a source of identity within the federation rather than a reason to leave it. A note in favour might cite Tamil Nadu: the linguistic state channelled the Tamil movement into administration and education rather than separatism. A note against might point to disputes over border areas and the claims of smaller languages within a linguistic state. (Student answers will vary.)

11.

What do the newspaper clippings on decentralisation suggest about efforts in India?

Model answer

That local bodies are active but uneven: some States have devolved funds and functions and hold regular elections, while others have not transferred significant powers or resources. The clippings support the chapter’s conclusion that the 1992 amendment created the framework, but real self-government is still a work in progress.

12.

Why does the D.M. or Collector administer the district when the chairperson of the zilla parishad is its political head?

Model answer

Because the two roles are different. The zilla parishad chairperson is elected and political, heading the district’s local government; the District Magistrate or Collector is a career civil servant appointed by the State government to maintain law and order and administer State functions in the district. The elected body decides, the administrative service implements.

Important Questions and Answers

Board-style practice

Practice questions

Written in the style of CBSE board papers. They are additional practice, not NCERT originals.

1.

Explain the key features of federalism. (5 marks)

Model answer

(i) There are two or more tiers of government. (ii) Different tiers govern the same citizens but each has its own jurisdiction in legislation, taxation and administration. (iii) These jurisdictions are specified in the constitution, so each tier’s existence and authority is constitutionally guaranteed. (iv) The fundamental provisions cannot be changed unilaterally by one level — both must consent. (v) Courts interpret the constitution and act as umpire in disputes between levels. (vi) Sources of revenue for each level are clearly specified to ensure financial autonomy. An ideal federation also needs mutual trust and an agreement to live together.

2.

How does the Constitution of India make the country a federal state? (5 marks)

Model answer

The Constitution declared India a Union of States and provides all the federal features: a two-tier system (later three, with panchayats and municipalities); a three-fold distribution of legislative powers through the Union, State and Concurrent Lists, with residuary powers with the Union; special powers for certain States under Article 371 and Union Territories with fewer powers; a rigid amendment procedure for the power-sharing arrangement — two-thirds majority in both Houses of Parliament plus ratification by at least half the States; a judiciary that decides disputes over the division of powers; and separate sources of revenue for each level.

3.

How has the creation of linguistic States and the language policy strengthened federalism in India? (5 marks)

Model answer

Linguistic States: boundaries were redrawn after 1947 so that people speaking the same language lived in the same State, with some States created for cultural, ethnic or geographical reasons (Nagaland, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand). National leaders feared disintegration and the Centre resisted, but experience showed that linguistic States made the country more united and administration easier. Language policy: the Constitution gave no language the status of national language; Hindi became the official language though it is the mother tongue of only about 40 per cent, with 21 other Scheduled Languages protected, and candidates for central posts may take examinations in any of them. When English was due to cease in 1965, non-Hindi States protested — violently in Tamil Nadu — and the Centre agreed to continue English along with Hindi. Promotion of Hindi continued, but imposition was avoided — the flexibility that Sri Lanka lacked.

4.

How did Centre–State relations change after 1990? (3 marks)

Model answer

Before 1990 the same party usually ruled at the Centre and in the States, so States did not act as autonomous units; when a rival party ruled a State, the Centre often tried to undermine it and misused the Constitution to dismiss State governments. After 1990 regional parties rose and coalition governments began at the Centre, so national parties had to ally with regional parties — producing a new culture of power sharing and respect for State autonomy, supported by a Supreme Court judgement that made arbitrary dismissal of State governments difficult.

5.

What is decentralisation? Why is it necessary in India? (5 marks)

Model answer

Decentralisation is the transfer of power from central and state governments to local government. It is necessary because a vast country cannot be run through two tiers alone: Indian States are as large as independent European countries — Uttar Pradesh is bigger than Russia in population, Maharashtra about as big as Germany — and are internally very diverse. Many problems are best settled locally, where people know the issues and have better ideas about spending money and managing things, and where they can participate directly in decision-making, which inculcates a democratic habit. Local government is also the best way to realise the principle of local self-government.

6.

What changes did the 1992 constitutional amendment bring to local government? (5 marks)

Model answer

It made regular elections to local bodies constitutionally mandatory; reserved seats in elected bodies and executive heads for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes; reserved at least one-third of all positions for women; created an independent State Election Commission in each State to conduct panchayat and municipal elections; and required State governments to share some powers and revenue with local bodies, with the nature of sharing varying from State to State.

7.

Describe the structure of rural local government in India. (5 marks)

Model answer

At the base is the gram panchayat — a council of ward members (panch) and a president (sarpanch) directly elected by all adults of the ward or village, and the decision-making body for the village. It works under the gram sabha, which consists of all the voters of the village and must meet at least two or three times a year to approve the annual budget and review performance. A few gram panchayats form a panchayat samiti (block or mandal), whose members are elected by the panchayat members. All panchayat samitis in a district together constitute the zilla parishad, most of whose members are elected, with MPs, MLAs and some district officials also members; its chairperson is its political head.

8.

What are the achievements and difficulties of local government in India? (5 marks)

Model answer

Achievements: it is the largest experiment in democracy anywhere — about 36 lakh elected representatives; it has deepened democracy, increased women’s representation and voice, and made elections to local bodies regular. Difficulties: gram sabhas are not held regularly; most State governments have not transferred significant powers to local bodies; and adequate resources have not been given. We are therefore still a long way from realising the ideal of self-government.

9.

Distinguish between a “coming together” and a “holding together” federation. (3 marks)

Model answer

In a “coming together” federation, independent States come together on their own to form a bigger unit, pooling sovereignty while retaining identity to increase security — the USA, Switzerland and Australia — and the constituent States usually have equal power and are strong vis-à-vis the federal government. In a “holding together” federation, a large country divides its power between constituent States and the national government — India, Spain, Belgium — and the central government tends to be more powerful, with unequal powers among units and special powers for some.

10.

What are residuary subjects? Who legislates on them and why? (2 marks)

Model answer

Subjects that do not fall in any of the three lists — such as computer software, which came up after the Constitution was made. The Union Government has the power to legislate on them, which keeps a uniform national position on new and unforeseen matters.

Source-Based and Case-Based Questions

Read the passage or table, then answer

Source 1 — What federalism is

Source extract

Federalism is a system of government in which the power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units of the country. Usually, a federation has two levels of government. One is the government for the entire country that is usually responsible for a few subjects of common national interest. The others are governments at the level of provinces or states that look after much of the day-to-day administering of their state. Both these levels of governments enjoy their power independent of the other… The federal system thus has dual objectives: to safeguard and promote unity of the country, while at the same time accommodate regional diversity. Therefore, two aspects are crucial for the institutions and practice of federalism. Governments at different levels should agree to some rules of power-sharing. They should also trust that each would abide by its part of the agreement.

1.

What are the two objectives of a federal system?

Model answer

To safeguard and promote the unity of the country, and at the same time to accommodate regional diversity.

2.

What two conditions does the passage say federalism requires?

Model answer

An agreement on rules of power-sharing, and mutual trust that each level will abide by its part — “mutual trust and agreement to live together”.

3.

How does this differ from a unitary system?

Model answer

In a unitary system the sub-units are subordinate to the central government, which can pass orders to them; in a federation each level’s power is independent, and the centre cannot order a State to do something.

Source 2 — The language policy

Source extract

Our Constitution did not give the status of national language to any one language. Hindi was identified as the official language. But Hindi is the mother tongue of only about 40 per cent of Indians. Therefore, there were many safeguards to protect other languages. Besides Hindi, there are 21 other languages recognised as Scheduled Languages by the Constitution… Unlike Sri Lanka, the leaders of our country adopted a very cautious attitude in spreading the use of Hindi. According to the Constitution, the use of English for official purposes was to stop in 1965. However, many non-Hindi speaking States demanded that the use of English continue. In Tamil Nadu, this movement took a violent form. The Central Government responded by agreeing to continue the use of English along with Hindi for official purposes.

1.

Why were safeguards for other languages built into the Constitution?

Model answer

Because Hindi is the mother tongue of only about 40 per cent of Indians, so making it the sole language of government would have disadvantaged the majority of citizens whose first language is another.

2.

What does the 1965 episode show about Indian federalism?

Model answer

That the Centre was willing to accommodate State demands rather than impose a language — a flexibility that avoided the conflict Sri Lanka created with its 1956 Act. It also shows that federalism in practice depends on politics, not only on constitutional text.

3.

What criticism is made of the outcome?

Model answer

That continuing English favoured the English-speaking elite. The chapter notes this criticism while pointing out that promotion of Hindi remains official policy — but promotion is not imposition.

Source 3 — Porto Alegre

Case study

A city called Porto Alegre in Brazil has carried out an extraordinary experiment in combining decentralisation with participative democracy… The nearly 13 lakh people in this city get to participate in making the budget for their own city. The city is divided into many sectors or wards. Each sector has a meeting, like that of the gram sabha, in which anyone living in that area can participate… About 20,000 people participate in this decision making exercise every year. This method has ensured that the money cannot be spent only for the benefit of the colonies where rich people live. Buses now run to the poor colonies and builders cannot evict slum-dwellers without resettling them.

1.

How is Porto Alegre’s experiment similar to the gram sabha?

Model answer

Both are open meetings of residents in which anyone living in the area may participate and take decisions about local priorities — in Porto Alegre, the city budget; in a village, the panchayat’s budget and performance.

2.

What concrete changes did participation produce?

Model answer

Spending shifted towards poor colonies — buses now run there, and builders cannot evict slum-dwellers without resettling them.

3.

What does this show about decentralisation?

Model answer

That it is not merely administrative: when people participate directly, the pattern of public spending changes in favour of those who usually go unheard. It is why the chapter calls local government the best way to realise local self-government.

HOTS and Competency-Based Questions

Think beyond the text

Higher order thinking

These ask you to apply, compare or evaluate. Model answers are indicative — your own well-reasoned answer can differ.

1.

“India is a federation with a strong centre — so it is not really federal.” Evaluate.

Model answer

The premise is right, the conclusion is not. India belongs to the “holding together” category, where a large country divides power and the centre tends to be stronger — as in Spain and Belgium. But strength at the centre is not the absence of federalism: the States have constitutionally guaranteed jurisdictions (police, agriculture, trade), the Union cannot order them on those subjects, the division of powers cannot be changed without two-thirds majorities and half the States, and courts umpire disputes. A strong centre within a federal design is a choice, not a contradiction. (Model answer.)

2.

Why might a State resist the transfer of powers and funds to panchayats, and what would change that?

Model answer

Because power and money transferred downward are power and money the State leadership no longer controls; local bodies may be run by rival parties, and State ministers lose discretion over schemes. What would change it: a constitutional or statutory formula for devolution, State Finance Commission recommendations made binding, audit and transparency requirements so that funds are seen to be used well, and electoral pressure — since local representatives are numerous and close to voters. (Model answer.)

3.

Compare India’s language policy with Sri Lanka’s, and explain what each produced.

Model answer

Sri Lanka made Sinhala the only official language in 1956 and followed preferential policies, producing alienation, a demand for Tamil Eelam and civil war. India made Hindi the official language but not the national language, protected 21 other Scheduled Languages, allowed examinations in any of them, and when English was due to cease in 1965 continued it alongside Hindi after protests. The result was accommodation rather than conflict — the flexibility that Indian federalism is built on. (Model answer.)

4.

A student says: “The three lists are out of date — cyberspace, climate and pandemics are not in any list.” How does the Constitution cope?

Model answer

Through residuary powers: subjects not in any list fall to the Union, which is how computer software is handled. That gives a uniform national response on new matters. But it also concentrates power, so in practice the Union consults States, uses the Concurrent List where possible, and relies on inter-governmental bodies. The design is flexible by intention — the residuary clause is a gap-filler, not a grab for everything. (Model answer.)

5.

What would Indian federalism look like if the 1992 amendment had never been passed?

Model answer

Local bodies would remain creatures of State governments, with irregular elections, no reserved seats for women and weaker sections, and no independent election machinery or shared revenue. Participation would be confined to State-level politics, which is remote for most villagers, and the “largest experiment in democracy” — about 36 lakh elected representatives — would not exist. Deepening democracy below the State is precisely what the amendment achieved. (Model answer.)

6.

Why does federalism need courts, and what does that tell you about constitutions?

Model answer

Because written divisions of power inevitably produce disputes — is a subject in the Union, State or Concurrent List? Who may legislate on a new matter? Without an umpire, the stronger level would simply take over. Courts interpret the Constitution and decide such disputes, which tells us that a constitution is not self-enforcing: it needs institutions — courts, election commissions, finance commissions — to make its words real. Chapter Summary

The whole chapter in a few paragraphs

Federalism is a system in which power is divided between a central authority and constituent units, each independent of the other. Its dual objective is to safeguard the unity of the country while accommodating regional diversity, which requires agreed rules of power-sharing and mutual trust. Federations form either by “coming together” — independent states pooling sovereignty, as in the USA, Switzerland and Australia — or by “holding together”, where a large country divides its power, as in India, Spain and Belgium; in the second the centre tends to be stronger and units may have unequal powers. The features of federalism are two or more tiers, separate jurisdictions guaranteed by the constitution, no unilateral change of fundamental provisions, courts as umpire, and specified revenue sources for each level.

India’s Constitution declared the country a Union of States and provides all these features: three tiers, a three-fold distribution of legislative powers through the Union, State and Concurrent Lists with residuary powers with the Union, special powers for some States under Article 371, Union Territories with fewer powers, an amendment procedure requiring two-thirds majorities and ratification by half the States, and a judiciary to decide disputes. But constitutional provisions are not enough — the success of Indian federalism owes much to politics: the creation of linguistic States, which leaders feared but which made the country more united and administration easier; a language policy that made Hindi the official language without giving any language national status, protected 21 other Scheduled Languages, and continued English after the 1965 protests; and the restructuring of Centre–State relations after 1990, when regional parties and coalition governments produced a new respect for State autonomy, backed by a Supreme Court judgement against arbitrary dismissal of State governments.

A third tier was needed because Indian States are as large as independent countries and internally diverse. Decentralisation takes power to local government, where people know local problems and can participate directly. The 1992 amendment made regular local elections mandatory, reserved seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes and at least one-third for women, created State Election Commissions, and required States to share powers and revenue. Rural government runs from the gram panchayat, supervised by the gram sabha, through the panchayat samiti to the zilla parishad; urban government from municipalities to municipal corporations headed by a mayor. With about 36 lakh elected representatives it is the largest experiment in democracy anywhere — though gram sabhas meet irregularly, and powers and resources are still inadequate.
